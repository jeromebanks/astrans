# Neith

## Chapter Two: Sélaire Observatory

Forty years is a long time to spend proving a single sentence true, and Dr. Sélaire had not lived to see the end of it.

Flight had never been the hard part. Reaching Neith, keeping people alive once they'd reached it, building something on its airless surface that could hold pressure and hold instruments and hold up where nobody had ever built anything before: that had taken most of the forty years by itself, and the two decades after that had gone into slow, patient, unglamorous work, the kind that never made headlines the way the falling light eventually would. By the time Tirian stood in front of a public lecture hall with the observatory's newest image on the screen behind her, forty other astronomers called the station home, and the whole strange project had a name that had stopped sounding strange to anyone who worked there: Sélaire Observatory. Neith. Second ground.

"We believe this one is our twin," Tirian said, and let the image sit there a moment before she went on. A pale marbled disk, blue and white, entirely unlike the flat grey coin of Neith or the smeared brightness of everything else they'd catalogued. "It has measurable air. There's an ocean-like liquid on its surface, in enormous quantities."

A hand went up near the front. "Does it have life?"

"We don't believe so." Tirian had answered this question at every lecture for three years running, and it never got easier to say with the certainty the room wanted from her. "The atmosphere is so thin you can see clear through it to the surface, which tells us it can't hold much heat, and the liquid isn't the kind ours is. It's water, not the dense carbon-dioxide seas we know. We cannot picture anything living in a sea so thin and cold, on a world that fragile." She clicked to the next slide. "We'll keep watching. That's the whole of the job up here. Watching, and being wrong less often than we used to be."

She believed every word of it when she said it. That was, later, the part she found hardest to forgive herself for.

***

Three weeks after the lecture, Dalon found her in the plotting room with two photographs laid side by side, a day apart, and an expression she'd never seen on him before.

"Look at this one." He tapped the first frame. "And now this one. Same field, same exposure, a day later. This light's gone." He tapped a fainter mark two squares over on the second photo, brighter than it should have been. "Except it isn't gone. It's here."

"There are two kinds of lights," Tirian said, the answer coming automatic before the question had fully landed. "Ones that hold their arc around the prime light, slow and steady. Ones that never move at all. This isn't either."

"I know."

"What is it, then?"

"I think it's falling." Dalon said it quietly, like a man testing whether a word would hold weight once it was out of his mouth. "I worked the path three different ways so I wouldn't have to believe the first one. It's coming toward us."

"How big?"

"I can't be certain yet. From the brightening curve, maybe half the size of Neith itself."

Neither of them said anything for a while after that. Outside the dome, Venus hung in its accustomed place above the crater rim, pale and veiled and, for as long as anyone had been alive to look up at it, entirely unaware of how much attention it had never needed to earn.

***

FALLING LIGHT COMING, the broadcasts said, within a week, and after that there was no part of the sky anyone on the planet looked at without also looking for it.

President Tavon went live from the capital with Tirian beside her, because the public wanted a scientist's face next to the reassurance and Tirian was the only one whose face they already half recognized.

"Dr. Tirian, is it true the falling light may strike us?"

"We can't say that with certainty. We know it will come close." Tirian kept her hands still in her lap, the way she'd practiced, because the cameras found hands faster than they found anything in a voice. "There's a real possibility it burns up before it ever reaches the visible air. Smaller bodies do, often. We're still refining the size."

"And if it doesn't burn up?"

Tirian didn't answer that one. Neither did Tavon. The silence did the work instead.

Every day after that a new chart went out to every household that wanted one, the cone of possible paths narrowing a little further with each night's observation, and every day Venus sat inside that cone, patient as everything else in this story had been patient, and there was nothing, in the end, that anyone on the ground or on Neith could do about any of it. No engine built could catch it. No signal could turn it. They had spent forty years learning to see the universe and not one day of it building anything that could touch what was in it.

A week out, the cone finally tightened past the planet's edge. It looked, on every chart printed that morning, like a miss.

The cities celebrated. Sélaire Observatory ran a live feed of the closest approach, a bright quick point sliding past at a distance the announcers called safe, a thousand miles clear, and across the whole of Venus people stood in the street and cheered the same way people had once cheered the first pictures of the hundred lights, relief indistinguishable, from a distance, from wonder.

Tirian and Dalon watched the same feed from inside the dome and did not cheer.

They had run the numbers themselves, one more time, quietly, without telling anyone what the numbers actually said about the distance between a safe pass of the planet and a safe pass of the moon the planet's people had built their whole understanding of the universe from. Tirian reached over and took Dalon's hand, and neither of them let go, and the light that had spent three weeks falling toward them finished falling.

Neith, the observatory, and everyone who had ever called it home were gone in an instant, in a silence no air was ever there to break.
