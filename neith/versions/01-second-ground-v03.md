# Neith

## Chapter One: Second Ground

Naelis Kerr had been a poor flyer as a boy. Every child in Meriddon built a set of wings the year their arms grew strong enough to work the oars, stubby things no longer than the child strapped under them, and spent their afternoons the way Naelis did: rowing up into the thick low air over the canopy to ram the others and knock them tumbling down. The falling was never the danger. The higher you were knocked the more air you had to catch yourself in and the more time to come down on your feet, so the children who climbed highest were the ones who feared it least. Naelis went down more often than he ever put anyone else down. He had not been fearless, and he had not been good.

He was good now. Thirty-four years old and strapped into the cockpit of a freighter whose wings ran twenty feet to either side of the hull, far longer than the stubby few-foot span that carried freight between cities, because it took that much wing to hold weight in the thin air he was climbing toward. No one alive had taken an airframe that high.

That was the job. Someone had to go first and find out whether the engine quit before the wings did.

Below him the fog closed over Meriddon, and above it the sky did nothing at all, empty all the way up, holding only the one great light that gave every day its shape and had never asked to be understood past that. Naelis had flown the old wings high enough, as a younger man, to watch that emptiness from underneath. This freighter was built to climb up into it, out of the visible air a person could breathe and trust and fall through safely, into the invisible sphere above, where the scientists swore there was still air though no one had ever seen it or proven an engine would catch in it.

He pulled the stick back and hoped.

The engine held. The air thinned as he climbed and kept thinning, and somewhere past the last marking the old charts bothered with he crossed out of the visible air altogether, the fog gone from beneath him, the cabin sealed and holding its own thin breath against the nothing outside. Twelve minutes up he leveled off, in air the instruments could barely find, and looked out at a sky he did not recognize.

Two hundred miles off, the lights of Tavilly hung faint and low along the curve of the world, farther than he had ever seen anything from the air. The second light was up too, swollen toward its full phase, closer and vaguer than the great one and never, in the whole of Naelis's life, anything but a smear of brightness no lens had brought into focus.

At this altitude it was not a smear.

It had an edge. He kept waiting for it to soften the way every edge softened when you stared long enough, and it did not, and the longer he held on it the more the thing behind it refused to be a light at all. It was a face. A surface. Ground, hanging up there with a rim to it the way Meriddon hung under him with a horizon, solid enough that some part of him started looking for where a person would set down on it.

He was still doing that when he noticed the small ones.

Tiny. Hard. Scattered across the deepening dark past the second light's rim, more of them the longer he let his eyes settle, and not one of them moved with him or faded or did anything a light was supposed to do. None of them had a name. Nobody had ever needed one.

He photographed everything he could hold the phone steady against, and flew back down before he ran low on fuel.

"Lights in the air," Avel said, reading it off the report as if saying it aloud might make it behave. "Above the visible air. Other freighters, maybe. Somebody out of Vélane, climbing where they shouldn't."

"No, ma'am." Naelis had rehearsed this the whole way down and it still came out sideways. "They didn't move. I held level flight four minutes and they sat exactly where they were. Nothing anyone built holds still a hundred miles up. There's nothing up there to hold it."

Avel let that go by. She tapped the second photograph instead, the wide grey disk with its hard sudden rim. "And this."

"The second light. It's a smear from the ground. It isn't a smear up there." He waited, then said the rest, because someone had to be first to say it. "I'm calling it Neith. Second ground."

Avel set the report down slowly, the way a person sets down something they have started to suspect is heavier than it looks.

"You understand we can't put this out. Not yet. Not like this." She looked at him a while. "They'll say we're inventing weather to move freighters."

"Avel, in eighteen months this airframe is running intercity. In three years there's a passenger model, and every one of those flights climbs up through that same air, and every window seat sees what I saw." He touched the photograph. "You can sit on it a year. Maybe two. What do you say the night a thousand people see it at once?"

She had no answer for that. Nobody would, for a long time.

***

It took four years for the secret to stop being one, and no time at all after that for the whole planet to lose its head over it.

The small lights had a name before anyone had built a single instrument worth pointing at them, the hundred lights, though there proved to be a great many more than a hundred once people learned to count them. Hand-drawn charts came first, hung in windows and traded at market like weather almanacs. Then the lens-grinders who had spent their lives making spotting glasses turned the craft toward the dark and kept making it bigger, and the charts drawn from those first real telescopes got the strangest thing right. Most of the hundred lights held their places against one another, night after night. A few did not. A handful wandered their own slow arcs across the dark, and those few got studied hardest of all, because a thing that moved was a thing with somewhere to be, and no one liked what it implied: that whatever carried them along might be carrying Venus too.

Spectra came after that, a thing no one had known how to make a year before and no one could agree on the meaning of now. Everyone agreed it meant something.

Dr. Sélaire brought the real proposal to the University congress four years into the frenzy, and she brought it with no patience left for halfway. "We are trying to do astronomy through six miles of moving weather," she told the room. "Every reading we take, we take through fog, from a freighter that won't hold still, in the twenty minutes it can loiter up there before it comes down. We need a station that stays put. Bigger instruments than any airframe will ever lift. Somewhere the weather cannot reach."

"There is no somewhere," said Dr. Serand, gently, the way a man says a thing he wishes were not true. "Nowhere on this world holds quiet long enough, and nothing here climbs above the weather to stay. You know that as well as I do."

"I know a place that isn't on this world."

The room went quiet in the particular way it had learned to go quiet that year, whenever someone said the word out loud instead of only photographing it.

"Neith," Sélaire said. "Second ground. It's close. It has no weather and no air worth the name, which is the whole point of it: nothing at all between a lens and the dark, and solid ground to stand it on. Somewhere a person could finally hold still and look."
