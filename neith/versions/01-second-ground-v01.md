# Neith

## Chapter One: Second Ground

Naelis Kerr had been a poor flyer as a boy. Every child in Meriddon built a set of wings the year their legs grew long enough to work the pedals, the way children had built them since long before anyone thought the trick worth writing down, and Naelis had spent his afternoons the way all of them did: rowing up into the thick low air over the canopy to ram the others and knock them tumbling out of the sky. The falling was never the danger. The higher you were knocked the more air you had under you to catch yourself in, the more time to bring your wings level and come down on your feet, so the children who climbed highest were only ever the ones who feared it least. Naelis went down more often than he ever put anyone else down. He had not been fearless, and he had not been good.

He was good now. He was thirty-four years old and strapped into the cockpit of a freighter whose wings ran thirty feet from either side of the hull, because thirty feet of wing was what it took to hold weight up in the thin air near the top of the sky, where he was going, and no one alive had ever taken an airframe that high.

That was the job. Someone had to go first and find out whether the engine quit before the wings did.

Below him the fog closed over Meriddon the way it had for as long as there had been a Meriddon to close over, and above it the sky did nothing at all, empty all the way up, holding only the one great light that gave every day its shape and had never asked to be understood past that. Naelis had flown the old wings high enough, as a younger man, to watch that emptiness from underneath. This freighter was built to climb into it.

He pulled the stick back and hoped.

The engine held. The air thinned as he climbed and kept thinning, past the markings the old charts had ever bothered with, until a boy's oldest rule ran backward up here: there was no longer enough of it under him to catch a fall, and height had quietly stopped being the safe thing and turned into the thing that would kill him. Twelve minutes up he leveled off, in air so thin the instruments complained about it, and looked out at a sky he did not recognize.

Two hundred miles off, the lights of Tavilly hung faint and low along the curve of the world, farther than he had ever seen anything from the air. The second light was up too, swollen toward its full phase, closer and vaguer than the great one and never, in the whole of Naelis's life, anything but a smear of brightness that no lens had ever brought into focus.

At this altitude it was not a smear.

It had an edge. He kept waiting for the edge to soften the way every edge softened when you looked at it long enough, and it did not, and the longer he held on it the more the thing behind the edge refused to be a light at all. It was a face. A surface. Ground, hanging up there with a rim to it the way Meriddon hung under him with a horizon, solid enough that some part of him started looking for where a person would set down on it.

He was still doing that when he noticed the small ones.

Tiny. Hard. Fixed, scattered all across the deepening dark past the second light's rim, more of them the longer he let his eyes settle, and not one of them moved with him or faded or did anything a light was supposed to do. None of them had a name. Nobody had ever needed one.

He photographed everything he could hold the phone steady against, and flew the descent back down before the fuel margin made the decision for him.

"Lights in the air," Avel said, reading it off the report as if saying it aloud might make it behave. "Above the visible layer. Other freighters, maybe. Somebody out of Vélane, climbing where they shouldn't."

"No, ma'am." Naelis had rehearsed this the whole way down and it still came out sideways. "They didn't move. I held level flight four minutes and they sat exactly where they were. Nothing anyone built holds still a hundred miles up. There's nothing up there to hold it."

Avel let that go by. She tapped the second photograph instead, the wide grey disk with its hard sudden rim. "And this."

"The second light. It's a smear from the ground. It isn't a smear up there." He waited, then said the rest, because someone had to be first to say it. "I'm calling it Neith. Second ground."

Avel set the report down slowly, the way a person sets down something they have started to suspect is heavier than it looks.

"You understand we can't put this out. Not yet. Not like this." She looked at him a while. "They'll say we're inventing weather to move freighters."

"Avel, in eighteen months this airframe is running intercity. In three years there's a passenger model, and every one of those flights climbs through that same layer, and every window seat sees what I saw." He touched the photograph. "You can sit on it a year. Maybe two. What do you say the night a thousand people see it at once?"

She had no answer for that. Nobody would, for a long time.

***

It took four years for the secret to stop being one, and no time at all after that for the whole planet to lose its head over it.

The small lights had a name before anyone had learned a single true thing about them, the hundred lights, though there proved to be a great many more than a hundred once people counted in earnest. Hand-drawn charts came first, hung in windows and traded at market like weather almanacs, and then the real observatories turned their instruments up instead of out, and the amateurs turned out to have been right about the strangest part: most of the lights held their positions against one another night after night, fixed and patient, and a few did not. A handful wandered their own slow arcs across the dark. Those few got studied hardest of all, because a thing that moved was a thing with somewhere to be, and nobody liked what it implied that whatever carried them along might be carrying Venus with it.

Spectra came next, thin and difficult and argued over without end. No one agreed yet what any of it meant. Everyone agreed it meant something.

Dr. Sélaire brought the real proposal to the University congress four years into the frenzy, and she brought it with no patience left for halfway. "We are trying to do serious astronomy through six miles of moving weather," she told the room. "Every reading we take, we take through fog, from a platform that won't hold still, in the twenty minutes a freighter can loiter at altitude before it has to drop for fuel. We need a fixed station. Bigger instruments than any airframe will ever carry. Somewhere the sky stays put."

"There is no somewhere," said Dr. Serand, gently, the way a man says a thing he wishes were not true. "Nowhere on this world holds still long enough. You know that as well as I do."

"I know a place that isn't on this world."

The room went quiet in the particular way it had learned to go quiet that year, whenever someone said the word out loud instead of only photographing it.

"Neith," Sélaire said. "Second ground. It's close. It holds fixed against us. And as far as anyone has been able to tell, there is nothing up there to stand between us and the looking."

Nobody in that room said it would be easy. Nobody said it couldn't be done either, and no one said the other thing, the thing none of them had any way yet to know to say, which was that the case Sélaire had just won would put forty people on the surface of the second ground on the one day in four hundred years it would have been survival to be anywhere else.
