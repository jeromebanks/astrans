Neith
Giovanni Domenico Cassini first saw a moon of Venus on January 25, 1672, described as one quarter the diameter of Venus with a period just over nine days. Over the next hundred years, dozens of astronomers confirmed the sightings, and the period was refined to eleven days, three hours.
Chapter One: The Hundred Lights
    Naelis had never been a good flyer as a boy. Every child in Meriddon built a kid plane the year their arms grew strong enough to work the oars, and spent their afternoons the way Naelis did: rowing up into the thick low air over the canopy to ram the others and knock them out of their plane. The dense air on Venus meant the higher you fell, the more time you had to land on your feet. Naelis was never very good at that game, and went down more often than he knocked his friends down. But he never stopped playing.
    Now he was thirty-four years old and strapped into the cockpit of a freighter whose wings ran twenty feet to either side of the hull, far longer than the three-foot span that most big, intercity planes used.  This test plane had so much wing because it was built to climb high, to where the air was invisible, less than two hundred psi. No one alive had taken an airframe that high. Most people didn’t believe it was possible to fly in air you couldn’t even see, but the engineers had built this plane with a special engine and those huge wings because they thought it would be faster and more efficient. It was up to Naelis to prove it could work.
   He watched the fog close over the Meriddon forest. The fog was getting thinner as he climbed, and he could see the glow of the city lights from Tavilere and even Darion, over a hundred miles away. The dim Second Light was up, and, as he rose, it became more distinct.  Brighter and localized, it looked less like a glow orb of energy he had learned about in school. It was solid, like the ground, but not connected to the ground. 
   The air was getting clear. He pulled the stick back and hoped. He watched as the air pressure dropped from five hundred psi, to two hundred, the design altitude. The engine hummed smoothly.  More smoothly than it had on the ground.  The oversized fan, taller than him, pushed the air with authority, maintaining speed. And the wings held. He pushed the envelope, climbing to 150 psi.  The plane still flew, and the cabin held its pressure.  As he looked up, he saw it.  A hundred tiny lights, scattered across the sky. And the second light was clear.  It had circular mountain ranges, like spots with valleys in them. He took pictures with his cell phone. He photographed everything he could, holding the phone steady against the frame of the plane. When the low fuel warning light came on, he returned to the air base in Avenel.

***

   "Lights in the air," Avel said, reading it off the report as if saying it aloud might make it behave. "Above the visible air. Other freighters, maybe. Maybe Tavilere is ahead of us and already has high altitude flyers."
   "No, ma'am." Naelis said. "They didn't move. I held level flight ten minutes and they sat exactly where they were. They were hundreds of miles away. I think they were beyond the invisible air.”
   “In the emptiness?  Impossible. Scientists tell us nothing is up there, and there is no air to hold it up if there was. It’s not just invisible, it doesn’t exist.”
   “I know.  But these lights do exist.”
   Avel tapped the second photograph, the wide, pockmarked, gray disk with its hard sudden rim. "And this."
   "The second light. It's a smear from the ground. I learned the same thing we all learned in school, that it was a bundle of energy. But, Avel, I watched it as I came up through the visible layer.  It became sharper and sharper. It isn’t energy at all.  It’s ground. As solid as what we are standing on right now, but not connected to the ground.” He looked Avel in the eyes. "I'm calling it Neith. Second ground."
   Avel set the report down slowly. "You understand we can't say this publicly. Not yet. Not like this." She looked at him a while. “They'll say we're inventing it. They won’t believe you were ever even up there.”
   “Avel, you can’t hide this.  In eighteen months this airframe is running intercity. Every one in the crew will see what I saw.”
   “The Prime Light will be shining.  They won’t see anything.”
   “Maybe. In three years a passenger version will be flying, and every window seat sees what I saw." He touched the photograph. "You can sit on it a year. Maybe two. What do you say the night a thousand people see it at once?"

***

   She sat on it through two cycles of the Prime Light. It leaked anyway, the way anything true eventually does. Maybe a pilot didn’t keep his mouth closed, or a mechanic overheard something, or someone sent a picture over an unsecure connection. Nobody ever traced it back further than that. Once it was out there was no putting it back.
   What surprised everyone, Avel included, was how little fear there was of it. People had spent their whole lives under a sky that did nothing, day after day, the same soft nothing their grandparents had looked up at, and here all at once was proof the sky had been hiding a second world from them the entire time. It felt like a gift.
   Within the year, three companies were running passenger planes up above the visible air during the dark months. The flights climbed slowly, so nobody's stomach turned, and leveled off around two hundred psi. Large windows in the ceiling let everyone look up. Whole families sat with their faces turned up, saying nothing at all. Naelis flew a few of those routes himself.  The sense of wonder never diminished.
   Children, before they had built their first plane, knew the second light was really a second ground. It got into the songs. It got into the schoolbooks. Every child in Meriddon learned all about Neith, a name for something their grandparents had spent whole lives calling a smear.
   But the scattered small lights were what really caught the public's imagination. Tiny lights, with no possible source.  Too small to be ground. They didn’t move like Neith. They all moved in unison, like they were spots on a sphere thousands of miles away. Hand-drawn charts came first, hung in windows and traded at market like weather almanacs, but they noticed a few lights were moving. One closer to the prime light, and four further away. Those five drew more attention than all the rest.
   The astronomers were slower to move than the amateurs, if only because they had more to lose by being wrong in public. Eventually, a handful of University scientists chartered flights that went up to a hundred psi.  It wasn’t just hundreds of lights; there were thousands, clearer than what could be seen from the tourist flight. And they brought something new: cameras with wide lenses, three inches across. The first picture came from Dr. Sélaire, a wide-angle, timed exposure titled “The Hundred Lights.” It showed ten thousand lights, each with a different brightness and some with different colors. Large prints of it sold fast, to be hung in millions of homes all over the world. It was the single most awe-inspiring picture Venus had ever seen. 



Chapter Two: Sélaire Observatory

  "We know there are two types of lights. Most of them are pinned to the distant sphere, but we have been unable to measure how far away that sphere is.  The five that move, however, follow the law of falling.  They endlessly circle the Prime Light. The amazing finding is that we, too, are falling in circles around the Prime Light. Still, the science of measuring the lights is limited. We are flying at one hundred psi, but even at fifty psi, the air above us distorts and moves the lights. Our measurements are not precise enough to tell us how far away the still lights are." Dr. Sélaire told the University. "Every reading we take, we take through fog.  I can’t see it, but our instruments can. Meanwhile, the plane and the air are moving around. We need a station that stays put, one that can support bigger instruments.  I have seen six-inch cameras. Maybe one day we will have two-foot cameras, but they need to be someplace steady, above all the fog."
   "There is no such place," said Dr. Serand, the president of the university. "You know that as well as I do. No mountain is tall enough; we can’t possibly build a tower high enough."
   Sélaire projected a picture behind her. "I know a place that is exactly what we need: Neith," Sélaire said. "The second ground is close, and it has no air we can measure. Nothing at all between a lens and the lights, and solid ground to stand it on."

***

   Her idea was popular, but Venus lacked the technological ability to fly to Neith. Flight grew higher and higher.  Planes could travel as high as five psi and stay airborne for hours.  The cameras were set in stabilized mounts, and real progress was being made in understanding the lights, but Sélaire continued to push for an observatory on Neith.  
   Rockets were launched from the highest-flying planes, and they could fly into the emptiness.  Within twenty years, rockets had landed on Neith, proving it was solid even to those who thought it might be an illusion. Sélaire lived to see that, but she had passed away before the great observatory, named for her, was built on Neith. 

***

   Sélaire Observatory housed forty scientists who studied the lights.  It sat on a broad plain on Neith, sealed against the emptiness outside, and it took pictures, and the pictures were sharper than any that had ever been taken before.
   An observatory allowed the development of new scientific techniques.  Not only pictures and maps, but spectroscopy, breaking down each light into its spectral components.  Chemists immediately set to understand the chemistry of the lights. They noticed it matched, mostly, hydrogen spectra that they could see in a lab. Hydrogen Incandescence was the headline.  The paper described that it was not only hydrogen; there were also contaminants, such as helium.  But the secret of the lights was out. They were made of atoms which could be found in the ground and the air. 
   The Sélaire Observatory held a symposium, broadcast to the universities on the ground, to discuss a new finding. Dalon, the principal investigator, had been investigating the moving lights.  
   “Our own world has a twin,” he said.  “I have been studying the moving light which is one further from the Prime Light than us, the third such light.”  He broadcast a picture of the light.  Not a small blue dot, a large blue and white and green disk.  “We believe this is not just a light, but it is also ground.  It is just like our own world, with air, seas, and ground.  It even has its own neith, a little larger and farther away than our own, but definitely a second ground.  Our twin’s neith even has the same circular mountain ranges our Neith has.”
    Hands shot up, and a microphone was given to one in the audience. Dalon couldn’t see who was asking, but the question was universal. “Does it have life?”
    “Evidence is not clear.  It seems the air is extremely thin. Less than twenty psi at the densest, so life seems unlikely.  It is enough to support local weather patterns. It has a sea, like our own, but it is made of water, which won’t dissolve minerals as well as our own superfluid CO2 sea does, and it has a stronger phase change, which leads to destructive waves near the surface.  We doubt it could support life, and anything that did manage to grow would probably be killed quickly.  And there is the matter of its spin.  The ground is spinning around faster than the speed of sound in their air.  So we believe there is no chance of life there.  However, the second most abundant component of the air is oxygen, and we are at a loss to explain how that happened without life.”
   “What about its neith?”
   “There is no life there, but, and maybe this dream is too big, but I believe we could build an observatory on that neith, and it would be fascinating.”
   “What about the other moving lights?”

Chapter Three: The Falling Light

   Dalon found it three weeks after the symposium, in the plotting room, with two photographs laid side by side, a day apart.
   "Look at this one." He tapped the first frame. "And now this one. Same field, same exposure, a day later. This light's gone." He tapped a fainter mark two squares over on the second photo, brighter than it should have been.
   "A sixth moving light?" Tirian asked.
   "I don’t think so."
   "What is it, then?"
   "I think it's falling, but not in circles," Dalon said. “Look at these other pictures.  I plotted its trajectory. I think it will intersect our own path.” He brought up the trajectory on the monitor.
   Tirian looked at the charts. "How big is it?"
   "I can't be certain yet. I am not sure of its distance or albedo, but my guess is half the size of Neith itself."
   Both of them fell silent.
   Over the next month, every scientist at the observatory had validated the track. It would come very close. It was time to alert the people on the ground.
   “Falling Light Headed For Us” the headlines read.  
   President Tavon went live from the capital with Tirian beside her. “Tirian, the people need to know what is going on, and rather than interpret what you scentitsts say, I want you to tell us directly.  What is this falling light?  Should we be scared?”
   "As scientists, we are not afraid, but we are concerned. We know it will come very close to us.”
   “Can we do something?  Can we slow down or speed up or stop it?”
   “No, the law of falling bodies will control what happens.  The energies are far beyond anything we can make.”
   “If it hits us, then what?”
   “Small ones hit us every day, and they burn up in the air, never affecting us.  We believe, however, that this one is too big for that.  We think it could, in theory, strike the ground, which would destroy any city nearby. We don’t fully understand what would happen, yet.  In all likelihood, it would strike the sea.  We have no idea what would happen then."
   She could see the fear in President Tavon’s eyes.  “Most likely, this will come close and go past us.  The latest calculation by Dr. Dalon is that there is a five percent chance it will hit the ground.  We will know much more in a month.”   
   Every day after that, a new chart was published, the cone of possible paths narrowing a little further with each day's observation.  The chance of impact grew steadily, but it never got to ten percent.  Some people were stockpiling oxygen and building tall chimneys so they could pump it down if the sea splashed too high. 
   A week out, the cone finally tightened, showing it would likely miss the ground, but not by much. 
   The cities celebrated. Sélaire Observatory ran a live feed of the falling light, with a computer graphic showing how close it would be to the world. Productivity fell to zero as all eyes were glued to the feed. It came in closer than expected, within a hundred miles of the ground.  The sky lit up as never before, but, in a moment, it was gone. In every city in the world, people came out in the street and cheered.
   Tirian and Dalon watched the same feed from inside the dome of the Sélaire Observatory, and did not cheer.
   They had run the numbers themselves, one more time, quietly, without telling anyone what the numbers actually said. Tirian reached over and took Dalon's hand, and neither of them let go, and the light that had spent a week falling toward Venus continued for two hours before striking Neith squarely.
   Neith, the observatory, and everyone who had ever called it home were gone in an instant, while the people on the ground were still cheering.
   The largest pieces came down within a day. They didn’t just obliterate the cities nearby.  They opened great holes in the world's crust, forming enormous volcanoes that released enormous amounts of sulfuric acid into the air killing all life within a hundred miles of the impact.  The sky was dark with ash spreading around the world from the hundreds of volcanoes. Tremors strong enough to crack foundations hundreds of miles from the nearest strike.
   And more kept falling.  
   It took geologists a month to understand what they were looking at.
   "There's no reason to tell them yet," Chief Geologist Meris told President Tavon. "Not until we're certain.  Right now we don’t know how the world might recover."
   President Tavon's eyes were desperate, clinging to Meris’s words. “You believe there is hope?”
   Meris looked away.
   They were certain in two more weeks. Tavon went live from the same podium she'd stood at the week of closest approach, and she did not bring a scientist with her this time.
   "Citizens of Avinel. Citizens of the world, I have spoken with our scientists at length, and I owe you what they've told me, exactly as they told it to me." She stopped speaking for almost a minute. "Neith did not simply fall all at once. It is still falling. Pieces of it will continue coming down for longer than any of us will live to see. Each strike is breaking our crust further. Each one is releasing more toxic sulfuric acid into the air. Our scientists tell me the sulfur content is already climbing faster than any model predicted, and it will not stop climbing. Our civilization will die. Not today. Not this week. But it will die, and in the centuries after that, the acid in our air will finish what the debris started, until there is no trace of our civilization anywhere on this world that could tell anyone we were ever here. This is a time to be with your families. Within a month, we will all be gone."
   The gray air they all knew and had flown in as children was rapidly becoming yellow.  Masks worked at first, but soon the sulfuric acid was eating the masks and their skin.  
    Life in the sea died out first.  The carbon dioxide concentrated the sulfuric acid, dissolving cell membranes.  Coastal cities went next.  Rain, the fresh showers they had always loved, became acidic and deadly.
   Within a  hundred years after the day the falling light struck Neith, the last visible trace of life in the world was gone.
