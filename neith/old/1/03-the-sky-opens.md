# Neith

## Chapter Three: The Sky Opens

By the fortieth night, Talis had learned to read the plotting board almost as well as the astronomers did.

It hung on the dome's inner wall, a grid of coordinates Sar had invented from nothing because there was no existing language for any of this, no charts, no tradition to borrow from, only four hundred years of two known lights and now this. Every clear night, someone marked a fresh set of points against the grid in colored wax, and every morning Sar stood in front of it with her arms crossed and her tea going cold, looking for what had moved.

Most points didn't move. That was, Sar said, the first real finding, and it had taken her three sleepless nights to say it out loud without her voice doing something strange: the vast majority of what they were seeing held its position relative to everything else, night after night, as if fixed to the inside of a shell too large to measure. Distant, she guessed, though distant wasn't really a word that meant anything yet at this scale. Far enough that Neith's own slow drift around Venus didn't shift them at all.

A few points did move. Five of them, so far, wandering against the fixed shell in slow, patient arcs, and it was one of these, on the fortieth night, that made Sar set her tea down without drinking it.

"Bren." Her voice was very careful. "Come look at this."

Talis crossed the dome. Osei was already at the board, chalk in hand, not writing anything, just staring.

"This one." Sar tapped a wax mark, then another, older, fainter, three positions back along a shallow curve. "It's been slowing. Every night, a little slower against the shell. Two nights ago I thought it had stopped."

"And now?"

"Now it's reversed."

Talis looked at the little curve of marks and did not, for a moment, understand why anyone's hands were shaking over it. Then Sar pulled the geometry out, the actual mathematics, sketched fast and rough on the slate beside the board: a small circle, Venus, and a larger one around it, the Sun, and then, farther out, another circle, and on that circle, moving at its own steady pace, independent of Venus and Neith entirely, the point that had slowed and stopped and reversed.

"It only looks like it stops and reverses," Sar said, "because we're moving too. We're not standing still watching it. We're going around the Sun ourselves, and so is it, only slower, farther out, and there are moments where our own motion overtakes its motion in the sky, and it looks like it's walking backward when really neither of us has changed direction at all."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Osei set the chalk down like it had gotten heavy.

"There are other worlds," Talis said, mostly to hear herself say it. "Going around the Sun. Like we do."

"At least five of them," Sar said. "That we can already see moving." She looked at the little diagram like it might rearrange itself if she stopped watching it. "Venus isn't the world. It's a world."

***

They didn't sleep much, that week. Nobody suggested it.

Sar drove the schedule harder than any flight commander Talis had ever served under, and nobody complained, because every single person in the dome had grown up with the same catechism, the same two circles on the same schoolroom wall, and every single one of them was now watching it get bigger by the night. They mapped the five wanderers' arcs across a full month, worked out that some circled close and fast and some circled slow and wide, worked out, from the faint colors that came through the long lenses, that at least two of them had their own visible disks rather than being points at all, small pale coins hanging against the black.

Talis kept flying, because someone had to run resupply and mail out to the relay satellite twice a month, and she came to treasure the run the way she'd once treasured the climb above the Ceiling: forty minutes each way, alone, with nothing to do but watch the wanderers hold their slow arcs and think about how strange it was that four hundred years of looking up had never once produced the thought that there might be more to look at.

A letter came through on the fifth run. Pell's handwriting, cramped and fast the way it always was when she had more to say than time to say it in.

*Everyone here thinks you've gone quiet because there's nothing to report. I know better. I know you, and quiet from you means you found something too big to put in a letter. Tell me anyway. Badly, if you have to. I don't care if it makes sense.*

Talis read it twice at altitude, in the particular stillness she'd once thought belonged only to her, and wrote back four pages that didn't make sense, and sent them anyway.

***

The anomaly showed up on the plotting board eleven weeks after landing, in Osei's hand, a single new mark added to a corner of the grid that had been empty on every previous night.

He almost didn't mention it. It was faint, fainter than most of the fixed shell, the kind of thing that could easily have been an error in the exposure, and Osei was tired enough by then that he nearly let it go until the next clear night proved it wrong or right on its own.

He marked it again the next night out of habit more than suspicion.

It had moved. Not the slow, patient arc of the five wanderers, the kind of motion that took a careful eye and a full month to be sure of. This had moved visibly, night to night, a distance the wanderers took a season to cover, and it had, in that same handful of nights, grown very slightly brighter.

Osei stood in front of the board a long time before he went to wake Sar.

"I don't think this one is standing still to be found," he said, when she came, still pulling her coat on over her sleep clothes, squinting at the two faint marks and the gap between them. "I think it's coming toward us."
