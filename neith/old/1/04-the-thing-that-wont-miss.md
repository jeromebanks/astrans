# Neith

## Chapter Four: The Thing That Won't Miss

By the time the orbit was certain enough to publish, six weeks had passed, and the object had a public name.

Venus called it the Third Light. Nobody at the College had chosen the name and nobody could trace exactly where it started, only that within days of the first broadcast it was everywhere, on every channel and in every street conversation, spoken with the particular reverence people save for things they are trying very hard not to be afraid of. Sun. Neith. And now this. The whole of the sky's population, doubled overnight, and the third one did not behave like the other two.

"It's going to pass close," Sar told the relay, in the flat, careful voice she used for anything she needed people to actually hear rather than merely feel. "How close, we don't know yet. The margin is still wide enough that either outcome is possible. A clean pass, or a strike. We will keep refining the arc every night, and we will tell you the moment we know more."

The response came back nine minutes later, the round trip to Venus and back, a College director asking the only question anyone ever asked once the shock wore off enough to let a question through.

"What can be done to stop it."

Sar looked at the transcript for a long time before she answered.

"Nothing," she wrote. "We have satellites. We have Neith. We have never built anything that leaves orbit under its own power fast enough to intercept something this size, this far out, and even if we had, we don't have a way to push something that large off a course it's held for longer than our species has been counting years. I'm sorry. There's no answer here I can give you that isn't that one."

Nobody at the College argued. Talis, reading over Sar's shoulder in the dome's small comms alcove, understood for the first time what people meant when they said a silence had weight.

***

Pell's letters got shorter as the weeks went on, which frightened Talis more than anything longer would have.

*Everyone here is either praying or making jokes about it,* one of them said. *I don't know which is worse. Mama's doing both, sometimes in the same sentence. I keep thinking about how we spent our whole lives under one sky and now everyone's terrified of a second thing that's been up there probably longer than the world has. Doesn't that feel unfair to you. It does to me.*

Another, shorter, arrived eleven days later: *They're building shelters along the coast. Not that it would matter, from what the broadcasts say, if it actually hit. I think people just need somewhere to be that isn't just standing outside looking up. Write when you can. I'd rather have nothing to say and hear from you than the other way around.*

Talis wrote back every time. She was careful, in every letter, not to mention the number that sat behind everything the College refined each week: a percentage, small and shrinking as the arc tightened, but never once reaching zero, that the object's path passed close enough to Neith itself to matter. Nobody talked about that number out loud in the dome either. It wasn't that anyone had decided not to. It simply never seemed to be the number worth spending fear on, not when the other number, the one for Venus, sat so much larger and held so much more.

If it took them, Talis thought, more than once, in the specific hours she couldn't sleep, there would be no one left on Neith to grieve it happening. Grief needed somewhere to land. Venus had eleven billion places for it to land. Neith had six.

***

The final estimate came through nineteen hours before closest approach, tight enough now that Sar read it twice before she trusted it.

The margin had not closed to zero. It had closed to a corridor, narrow, and Venus sat just outside its edge, the object's path threading past the planet close enough that the difference between a miss and a catastrophe had shrunk down to a distance Sar described, flatly, as smaller than Venus's own atmosphere was thick.

"It's going to miss us," she said, to the dome, to the six people who had spent eleven weeks discovering the universe and the six weeks since watching part of it aim itself at their home. "I want to be careful how I say that. It is going to miss Venus. The corridor doesn't touch the planet. I've checked it more times than I can defend as useful."

Nobody in the dome moved for a moment. Osei made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't come out broken in the middle.

Talis thought of the other number, the smaller one, the one that had never reached zero either, and did not say it out loud, because Sar's face told her Sar was already thinking it, and because there was nothing left to do now but what they had been doing all along.

They went outside together, all six of them, in suits rated for six hours nobody planned on using, and stood on the regolith at the edge of the shadow line, and waited to watch it go by.

Venus hung above the crater rim exactly as it always did, pale and veiled and utterly unaware of how closely it had been missed, and above it, small and bright and closing fast, the Third Light came on toward them across the last of the dark.
