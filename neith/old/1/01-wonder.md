# Neith

## Chapter One: Wonder

*Neith was observed up to 30 times by astronomers until 1770.*

Wikipedia

***

Talis Bren had flown above the Ceiling eleven times before she saw anything worth reporting.

The *Averil* climbed the way it always climbed, engines laboring thinner air, the hull ticking as the cold worked into the seams. Below her, the cloud deck stretched unbroken in every direction, the color of a healed bruise, lit from beneath by the cities it hid. Nobody who had not flown above it believed how strange it looked from up here. From the ground, the Ceiling was simply the sky: a soft grey nothing that brightened and dimmed on its own schedule, source unknown, permanent as breathing. From above, it looked like a floor. Something a person could stand on, if a person were foolish enough to try.

Nine kilometers. Ten. The altimeter needle crept past markings that had never had a name until the College gave them one, because nothing built by Venusian hands had ever needed to go this high before the *Averil*. Talis checked her oxygen feed, checked it again out of habit rather than need, and watched the last gauzy tendrils of true cloud thin out beneath her into something closer to haze.

This was the part of the flight she flew for. Not the data, though the data mattered and she'd log every second of it. The stillness. Down there the world was a wet, close, crowded thing, all cities and canopy and the sound of rain that never fully stopped. Up here there was nothing but the *Averil*'s engine and her own held breath.

She leveled off at twelve kilometers, the highest ceiling the engines could hold without flaming out, and that was when she saw it.

A point of light. Not the Sun, which sat low and diffuse behind her, a smear rather than a disc, the way it always was, the way it had always been in every description anyone had ever written of it. This was small. Hard-edged. Directly ahead, in the deepening color where the haze thinned toward whatever was on the other side of it, and it did not move with her, and it did not fade, and after four full seconds of staring at it with her heart going wrong in her chest, a second one appeared beside it.

Then a third.

Talis Bren, thirty-one years old, senior test pilot of the College of the Two Lights, sat in the cockpit of the *Averil* at the edge of everything anyone had ever known and did not breathe for longer than was sensible.

She had time for eleven seconds of clear sky before the haze closed back over it, some upper current shifting the deck the way it always did, patient and indifferent, and the small hard lights were gone, and there was only the Sun's smear and the deep, deepening color of nothing.

She flew the return descent on instruments she barely watched.

***

"You saw stars," Dr. Ilena Kade said, in the tone of a woman repeating something back to a child to give it a chance to correct itself.

"I don't know what I saw."

"You just told me what you saw."

"I told you what it looked like." Talis had rehearsed this on the way down, in the debrief, in the two hours since, and it still came out wrong. "I'm telling you I don't know what it was."

Kade's office at the College sat on the fourteenth floor, high enough that its single round window looked out on nothing but grey, the permanent condition of every window on the planet. She had been the first to fly instruments above the old eight-kilometer ceiling, twenty years before Talis was old enough to hold a license, and she had the particular stillness of someone who had spent a career being disappointed by altitude. She did not look disappointed now. She looked like she was deciding how worried to be.

"Three points of light," Kade said. "Fixed. Above the haze layer, roughly at your ten o'clock, holding position relative to you for the duration of level flight."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because."

"Because Renn saw something two years ago on the *Oisin* run and got laughed out of the debrief room, and I watched it happen, and I decided I'd rather be laughed at with data than believed without it." Talis set the flight recorder on Kade's desk. "It's all there. Altitude, heading, duration, the works. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to look."

Kade looked at the recorder for a long moment without touching it.

Everyone learned the same catechism as a child, the one that explained the whole visible universe in a single afternoon of schooling: there is the Sun, which gives the world its day, and there is Neith, which gives the world its month, its tides, the second and dimmer light that swelled and thinned above the Ceiling on its own slow twenty-nine day breath. Sun and Neith. Two lights, and nothing else, because the Ceiling did not permit anything else through, and it never had, in the whole recorded history of anyone looking up.

"If this is real," Kade said finally, "it isn't nothing. It's the biggest nothing anyone has looked at in four hundred years of trying."

"I know."

"You understand what I'm going to have to do with this."

"I'm hoping you're going to fly it yourself and prove I'm not seeing things."

For the first time since Talis had walked in, something in Kade's face loosened, not quite a smile. "I'm sixty-one years old. My flying days ended before your license did." She finally picked up the recorder, turned it over once in her hands like she could read the data through the casing. "But I know six people who'll want to see this before I've finished my tea, and I know exactly none of them are going to sleep tonight."

***

It took four more flights and two more pilots before the College would put its name on it. It took another two months of argument before anyone outside the College believed them, and the argument that finally worked was not the recordings, though the recordings helped. It was the simplest thing Kade could put in front of the funding board: a child's drawing, tacked to a projector screen, of two circles labeled SUN and NEITH, the entirety of the known universe as taught in every schoolhouse on the planet for as long as there had been schoolhouses.

"Every one of us learned this," Kade told the board, "and every one of us was taught it as complete. It was never complete. It was just as far as we could see."

The board asked the obvious question, the one Talis had been asking herself since the descent: if the Ceiling would not let this through to any instrument built on the ground, and would not let it through consistently even at the edge of what the *Averil* could survive, then where would anyone go to actually look.

Kade did not hesitate. "Neith."

Neith had no Ceiling. Neith, as far as anyone had ever been able to tell from four hundred years of watching its slow phases swell and thin, had no air worth the name at all, which was precisely why it had never occurred to a single soul in all that time to wonder what the sky looked like from its surface. Nobody had ever needed to. It was close enough to see with the naked eye and far enough to be, for all practical purposes, unreachable, and so it had sat in the collective imagination for four centuries as a second lamp and nothing more.

Talis sat in the back of the funding hearing and watched the board's faces change, one by one, from skepticism to something that had no name yet because nobody on the planet had ever had occasion to feel it before. It was not quite hope. It was closer to vertigo, the particular kind she'd felt at twelve kilometers with three lights sitting still against the dark, the sense of a floor giving way beneath a thing she had spent her whole life calling solid ground.

She raised her hand before the vote was called.

"I want to fly the survey ship," she said. "Whoever you send. I want to be the one who takes them up."

Kade looked back at her from the front of the room, and this time the almost-smile made it all the way.

"I was hoping you'd ask."
