# Neith

## Chapter Two: Ascent

Talis had promised her sister she would write from orbit, and she had every intention of keeping that promise right up until the moment orbit actually happened and there was nothing in her that could be spared for a letter.

The launch itself was almost gentle. The *Aphelion* rode a column of its own noise up through the last thick meters of the Ceiling, the hull shuddering the way the *Averil* used to shudder at altitude, except the shuddering never stopped the way it always had before. It kept going. The grey overcast that had been the entire sky for the whole of Talis's life thinned to haze, then to nothing, then to a black she'd only ever heard described in the reports she hadn't been sure she believed.

She looked out the small forward port and could not, for several seconds, make her eyes agree on what they were looking at.

Every one of them had grown up with the same two lights, and the same unspoken assumption that the space between the Sun and Neith was simply empty, a kind of restful nothing, because nothing else had ever needed explaining. Nobody had prepared her for how crowded it actually was. Hard points of light in every direction, more of them the longer she looked, some brighter and some so faint they seemed to come and go with the effort of finding them, and no pattern to any of it that her mind could hold onto, and behind her, from the aft cabin, she heard Dr. Miren Sar make a sound that wasn't quite a word.

"Bren." Sar's voice, when it came back, was carefully flat, the tone of someone keeping a very tight grip on something. "Bren, I need you to fly straight and level for exactly as long as it takes me to stop shaking."

"Copy that." Talis didn't take her eyes off the port. "For what it's worth, I'm not doing much better."

Sar was the mission's lead, forty years old, a specialist in atmospheric optics who had spent her whole career explaining why nobody could see past the Ceiling and had, in the space of two months, gone from writing the paper that said it was theoretically possible to being the first person selected to prove it. She had a daughter, six years old, whom she had left with her sister in the coastal district, and she had told Talis about her exactly once, over a meal three days before launch, in the specific tone of a person setting something down carefully so she wouldn't have to carry it onto the ship.

"I keep thinking," Sar had said, "that I'm leaving her under a sky I don't understand anymore. That's a strange thing to feel about your own child's ceiling."

Talis hadn't had an answer for that. She still didn't. She thought of Pell instead, seventeen and insufferable and probably at that exact moment telling everyone at the flight academy that her sister was in space, and found that the thought didn't loosen anything in her chest the way she'd hoped it would.

***

Neith, up close, was not what four hundred years of naked-eye observation had prepared anyone to expect.

It hung the way a held breath hangs, close and enormous, filling a third of the forward port by the time the *Aphelion* began its final approach: a grey, scarred, utterly silent thing, pocked with craters going back further than anyone had a way to date, its surface lit hard-edged by direct sunlight in a way nothing on Venus had ever been lit. No haze to soften anything. No twilight, no dusk, no gradual anything. Just light and its absence, drawn in a line as sharp as a blade.

"There's no air at all," said Renn Osei, the mission's second astronomer, a broad, quiet man who had flown two of the four confirmation runs with Talis back at the College and had asked, twice, to be assigned to this crew instead of staying home to raise his own children's opinion of him. "I knew that. I read the reports. I still keep expecting it to look like something's missing."

"Something is missing," Sar said. "That's the point."

They landed on a shelf of flat regolith the survey satellites had marked eight months earlier, in a crater whose far wall would, for eleven days out of every twenty-nine, throw a long cold shadow across the site and, for the other eighteen, leave it in unbroken light hot enough to cook exposed skin in minutes. The observatory dome went up in the shadow days, six people working in suits that had never been tested outside a pressure chamber, bolting together a structure that had been designed on paper by people who had never in their lives stood on ground that didn't have weather.

Talis worked the crane. It was easier, in a strange way, than she'd expected: a sixth of her weight, every motion too light and too slow, like moving through water that had forgotten how to resist her. She caught herself laughing once, for no reason she could name, and heard Osei laughing back at her over the comm, and understood that everyone on the crew was a little bit out of their mind in the same specific direction.

Assembly took nine days. On the ninth day, with the dome sealed and pressurized and the first bank of instruments still crated in the corner waiting for calibration, Sar declared an hour of rest before anyone touched a telescope, on the grounds that nobody did good science exhausted, and nobody argued with her, and nobody actually rested.

Talis went outside instead.

She stood on the regolith in a suit rated for six hours she didn't plan on using all of, at the edge of the shadow line where the crater wall's cold darkness met the hard bright light beyond it, and looked back the way they'd come.

Venus hung above the crater rim, and it was beautiful in a way none of the four hundred years of watching Neith from the ground had ever prepared her to expect of her own home. A pale, veiled crescent, soft-edged where the Ceiling caught the sunlight and scattered it into something closer to pearl than cloud, hiding every city and every canopy and every wet, close, crowded thing she had ever known underneath a single unbroken skin of light. Somewhere under that skin, in the coastal district, her sister was almost certainly not thinking about her at all, was probably arguing with someone about something that felt enormous and would not matter in a week, and Talis found that the thought did, after all, loosen something in her chest, standing here with nothing between her and the black for the first time in her life.

She could see stars now without trying. Hundreds of them, unmoving, patient, indifferent to the four hundred years it had taken anyone to notice they were there.

She stayed until her suit's cold warning chimed, and went back inside to help Sar break the crates open.
