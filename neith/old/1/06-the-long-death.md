# Neith

## Chapter Six: The Long Death

The first tremor came eighteen months later, and nobody connected it to anything.

It was small, the kind of shudder that rattled dishes and startled birds and gave the coastal district a story to tell for a week before the next thing worth talking about replaced it. The geological survey called it unremarkable. Pell, twenty-three by then and three years into a physics degree she had started for reasons she couldn't fully explain to anyone who asked, pulled the seismic records anyway, because she had learned by then that nobody else was going to think to look.

She found the second tremor before the survey did. Then the third. By the time she brought her plotted timeline to the same College director who had once read Talis's death off a list of six names to a room half of which was cheering, the pattern was already there for anyone willing to see it: tremors clustering, faint but climbing, on a period that matched nothing in the planet's own geological history and matched, exactly, the predicted decay of a debris field that had been given a name nobody wanted to say out loud anymore.

"It's not gone," Pell told him. "It's coming down. All of it, eventually. Just not all at once."

She was right, and being right did not change a single thing that happened next.

***

What followed did not happen quickly enough to be called a disaster and did not happen slowly enough to be called anything else.

The fragments came in over years, then in over a shortening rhythm of months, striking ocean far more often than land simply because there was more ocean to strike, each impact a fresh tremor and a fresh column of ash and a fresh round of survey teams measuring what had changed in the air. Sulfur first, faint, then less faint. Then the rains started coming back wrong, sharp enough to blister skin left uncovered too long, and the coastal cities began, quietly at first and then not quietly at all, moving inland, uphill, toward anywhere the water couldn't easily reach and the ash fell a little thinner.

Pell spent those years doing the only thing she had ever seen her sister do with an impossible number: she measured it, precisely, repeatedly, and told anyone who would listen exactly how much worse it was going to get and by when. She married. She had a daughter she named for no one, deliberately, because she had decided somewhere in that decade that she did not want to hand a child a name that carried this much weight before she'd had the chance to choose her own. She kept the Bren Survey running for thirty-one years, longer than anyone thought a single person's grief should be able to fuel a research program, and when she finally stepped back from it, old and slow and satisfied with nothing, the survey kept going without her, because by then everyone understood there was no version of the future that didn't need it.

She died at seventy-four, in a house built on high ground her daughter had insisted on, under a sky that had gone, by then, faintly and permanently yellow at the edges.

She never saw it finish. Nobody did. That was the particular cruelty the debris field had been built with by nothing more intentional than physics: it took long enough that no single grief could hold the whole of it, only a fraction, passed hand to hand, generation to generation, each one carrying it a little further and setting it down unfinished.

***

The Ceiling had always been the one constant, the one thing every Venusian in four hundred recorded years could stand under and call familiar. It went last, and it went by inches, so slowly that the generation who watched it finish never fully agreed among themselves on when it had actually happened.

The rains stopped being rain. The air over the equator, where the largest fragments had come down heaviest, thickened past what any lung built for the old atmosphere could use, and the retreat that had begun as an inland shuffle became, decade by decade, a retreat toward nothing, because there was no second world waiting the way Neith had once seemed to be, no ship built that could carry a civilization off its own planet fast enough to matter. They had reached their own moon once, in triumph, four generations before, and it had not occurred to anyone in the flush of that triumph that reaching it would turn out to be the single most important thing their species ever built the capacity to do and still, in the end, nowhere near enough.

The temperature climbed past what any council could vote to reverse. The clouds that had softened every sky over every city for the whole of remembered history thickened instead into something with a different chemistry entirely, sulfuric and closed and permanent, and beneath it, in the decades that had no one left keeping a survey running to mark them, the last of the coastal ruins went under a rain that no longer had anywhere living to blister.

Nothing was left standing that could be found again. No record, no ruin, no bone. The debris field that had been Neith went on falling, thinning, for longer than the civilization it had erased had ever survived to see, until there was no more of it left to fall, and no one left to have noticed the last piece land.

Above it all, unchanged and indifferent, exactly as it had always been, the Sun kept its old patient distance, the only light that had ever belonged to Venus by right and would, for that matter, go on belonging to it long after every other thing this story has been about was gone.
