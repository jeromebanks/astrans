# Neith: Worldbuilding

Canon reference for the Neith story. Drawn from the three chapters (`01-second-ground.md`,
`02-selaire-observatory.md`, `03-the-long-death.md`), the premise in `notes.md`, and
author-stated intent. Read this before drafting or revising any chapter. Where a fact is
unspecified or unsettled, it is listed under Open Questions rather than invented here. Where the
existing chapters contradict this file, see Known Chapter Errors: the chapters are wrong, not
this file.

## Premise

The world of the story is Venus, in a deep past, when it was a beautiful, living planet with an
advanced civilization. Its people have only ever known two lights in the sky, because a permanent
overcast hides everything else. Curiosity drives them to build an observatory on their moon,
Neith, where the sky is clear, and there they finally see the universe. Then they see an asteroid
inbound. It misses Venus and destroys Neith and everyone on it. Neith's debris deorbits onto
Venus over generations, floods the air with sulfur, and erases the civilization completely,
leaving the acid hellscape that Venus is in the present day. The whole thing is framed by the
real 17th-18th century astronomical mystery of a moon of Venus named "Neith."

## The planet: Venus

- A wet, close, crowded, living world. Cities and canopy and forest. Rain that never fully stops.
  Naelis's home region, Meriddon, sits under forest.
- The atmosphere is permanently, thickly overcast. From the ground, the sky is a soft grey
  nothing that brightens and dims on its own schedule.
- **The cycle of the Prime Light.** The great light is not up all year. Day and night are seasons,
  not hours: the world runs on a long alternation of lit months and **dark months**. This is
  load-bearing and must be respected. The hundred lights and Neith's true face are visible from
  altitude ONLY during the dark months. When the Prime Light is shining, a flight to two hundred
  psi shows nothing at all. This is why the sky could hide so long, why a discovery could be sat
  on, and why passenger flights later run seasonally. Avel's strongest objection to going public
  is exactly this: by the time the freighters carry witnesses, the Prime Light will be up and
  there will be nothing to see. **For all durations and unit lengths, see Units of Time below.**
- Venusians divide the atmosphere into three regions: the **visible sphere**, the dense breathable
  air near the surface where life is sustainable and where everyone lives; the **invisible
  sphere**, higher up, where scientists say there is still air but it is too thin to see through
  or breathe; and the **emptiness** above that, where there is no air at all.
- Because they live inside the visible sphere, Venusians have never seen stars. They never
  developed astronomy or telescopes, except small spotting glasses used at close range. They have
  no concept of the universe. They know the great light, which we call the Sun, and the second
  light, Neith, a moon. They do not know what either one is. They only know there are two lights.
- The seas are supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2). They do not behave like oceans on Earth;
  waves do not work the way water waves do. But sCO2 is an excellent solvent and a ready source of
  minerals leached from the bedrock, and life probably began there. The air above the seas holds a
  great deal of CO2, but, because of plant life, also plenty of oxygen, along with nitrogen and
  noble gases. In the visible sphere this air is breathable.
- The present-day, dead Venus (thick sulfuric-acid atmosphere, closed yellow haze, no surface
  water, no life) is the END STATE of this story, not its setting. The story takes place before
  the fall, in the living world.

## Units of time

**This section is the single authority on time. Nowhere else in this file, and nothing in the
manuscript, may state a unit length or a conversion. Everything points here.**

The recurring failure has been sliding between an Earth reference scale and a Venusian one
without marking which is in play. The fix is to keep the two layers physically separate below,
and to be explicit every time a number is written down about which layer it belongs to.

### Layer 1: The astronomy (real, fixed, not up for revision)

These are the actual figures for Venus and are stated in Earth reference because that is the only
scale they exist in. **They never appear in the manuscript.** They are here to keep Layer 2 honest.

| Quantity | Earth reference |
| --- | --- |
| Sidereal rotation (retrograde) | 243.02 Earth days |
| **Solar day** (sunrise to sunrise) | **116.75 Earth days** |
| **Orbital year** | **224.70 Earth days** |
| Solar day as a fraction of the year | 0.52, so close to but not exactly half |
| Neith's orbital period | ~11 days 3 hours (author-stated; see Decisions Needed #4) |

Consequence to hold on to: there are a little over **two solar days in a Venusian year**, and
because the day is about four percent longer than half a year, the dark seasons drift slowly
against the year rather than recurring in the same season each time.

## Physics of flight (the dense-air world)

This is central and must be gotten right. It was underused and mishandled in early drafts.

- Venus's air is roughly **one hundred times denser** than Earth's. In that air, powered personal
  flight is trivial and effectively prehistoric, "lost to antiquity" the way the wheel is on
  Earth. It was never a marvel and never an invention anyone is credited with.
- Because the air is so dense, wings are **very short**. Children build their own flying gear as
  soon as they are old enough, with wingspans of maybe a foot. Propulsion is muscular: fans,
  paddles, or oars. Flight is a normal part of childhood, like running.
- A common children's game is aerial battle: fly up and ram each other, trying to knock the other
  flyers tumbling down out of the sky.
- **Falling is safe** in the dense visible-sphere air. A fall is slow and survivable, and falling
  from higher is if anything safer, because there is more air beneath you to catch yourself in and
  more time to bring your wings level and land on your feet.
- Naelis Kerr, the protagonist of chapter 01, was a poor flyer as a boy. He lost the battle game
  more than he won it. He is a much better pilot as an adult.
- **Commercial flight** is common too, especially city to city. These craft are powered by
  internal combustion engines, still have wings only a few feet long, and look more like boats
  than like anything we would call a plane. In air a hundred times denser than Earth's, these
  engines are small and very powerful and tolerant of almost any fuel: they run on wood or oil as
  readily as anything else.
- The genuine frontier is not flight but **altitude**: climbing out of the visible sphere into the
  invisible sphere. The air there is still about ten times the density of Earth's, but it is thin
  enough to be invisible and too thin to breathe. People do not trust invisible air; visible air
  is what they breathe and prefer. No one had flown that high, and many doubt engines will even
  work up there.
- The story opens on **experimental high-altitude flight**. These freighters need longer wings
  than surface craft, about twenty feet to a side, to hold weight in the thinner air, and a
  pressurized cabin for the pilot.
  They are unproven and distrusted, but faster and cheaper than surface routes. In the invisible
  sphere the air thins enough that the true sky appears: Neith resolves from a smear into solid
  ground with an edge, and the stars, fixed and wandering, become visible.

### The pressure ladder

Altitude is measured and spoken of in **psi**, never in height. Use these numbers. They are the
spine of chapter 01 and they escalate deliberately.

| Reading | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| 500 psi | Thick air, climb already well underway. Nothing visible yet. |
| 200 psi | **Design altitude** of the experimental freighter, roughly ten times Earth sea level. Out of the visible air. Where the later passenger flights level off, because it is a gentle climb and nobody's stomach turns. |
| 150 psi | Naelis pushing past what the airframe was built for. Where he sees the hundred lights and Neith's face. |
| 100 psi | Reached later by chartered University flights carrying real instruments. Thousands of lights where the tourists see hundreds. |

Surface pressure is not stated in the text and should stay unstated unless a scene needs it.

The danger at altitude is not cold or dark but the two unproven things: whether the engine keeps
catching in air that thin, and whether the cabin holds pressure. A pilot up there watches the fan
and the seal, not the view, until he has reason to do otherwise.
- **Space flight does not exist** at the start of the story. It is developed over the following
  forty years, along with the systems needed to live off-world, on Neith.

## Technology and society

- **Freighters**: a new class of high-altitude airframe, engine-powered (stick control, not the
  oars and pedals of personal flight), running the same wood- or oil-burning internal-combustion
  engines as surface craft, with wings about twenty feet to a side, far longer than any surface
  craft, so they can hold weight in the thinner invisible-sphere air. Test pilots fly them first
  to learn whether the
  engine seizes or the wings fail at altitude. They are expected to dominate intercity transit
  within a couple of years, followed by passenger models whose window seats will let ordinary
  people see what the test pilots saw.
- **Passenger flights.** Within a year of the leak, three companies run tourist flights up past
  the visible air during the dark months. They climb slowly so nobody's stomach turns, level off
  around two hundred psi, and have large windows set into the roof. Whole families sit with their
  faces turned up, saying nothing. This is the emotional keystone of chapter 01 and the reason
  chapter 03 hurts: the sky arrived as a gift, not a threat. There is almost no fear in the public
  reaction, and that should surprise the characters who expected some.
- **Phones and cameras.** Personal phones with cameras exist and are how Naelis documents the
  discovery. Keep the register native: "phone," not "cell phone." Real optics come later, ground
  up by the spotting-glass trade: the first true astronomical instruments are lenses about three
  inches across, chartered aboard flights to a hundred psi.
- A **University** with a congress that debates and funds major science. After the discovery,
  amateur sky-charts come first, hung in windows and traded at market like weather almanacs. Only
  then do the lens-grinders turn their craft toward the dark, and only after that do purpose-built
  telescopes and observatories get developed. There was no prior astronomy to inherit. Nothing in
  the story may imply an existing observatory, telescope, or spectroscopy before the discovery.
- The **Sélaire Observatory**: the permanent astronomical station built on Neith's surface, named
  for the scientist who first proposed it. About forty astronomers live and work there. It is
  pressurized (Neith is airless).

## The sky and cosmology

As known from the surface, before the discovery:

- **The great light** (the Sun): the source of every day. Always referred to in prose as "the
  great light," "the one great light," or "the prime light," never named as "the Sun."
- **The second light** (Neith, the moon): dimmer, closer, and from the ground never more than a
  vague smear of brightness no lens can resolve. It swells and thins through phases.
- Nothing else. Every child is taught a two-light universe as complete: the great light and the
  second light, and nothing beyond.

After the discovery, from altitude and then from Neith:

- **The hundred lights** (the stars): tiny, hard points, first glimpsed by a test pilot. From the
  observatory they turn out to number in the billions, but the popular name sticks.
- **Etymology of the name, in order.** Naelis, at 150 psi, sees what he counts as "a hundred tiny
  lights." The phrase travels with the leak and enters common speech. Years later Sélaire titles
  her first great photograph *The Hundred Lights* using the people's phrase, and the picture shows
  ten thousand. Keep this chain intact; the irony is the point, and it should never be pointed at.
- **The sphere, deduced.** The lights all move together, in unison, as though they were spots
  painted on the inside of a sphere thousands of miles out. Venusians work this out from charts
  before they have any theory to hang it on. Render it as observation, not as astronomy.
- **The five wanderers.** Most lights hold their places against one another. Five do not: one that
  keeps close to the prime light, and four that range further out. These are studied hardest,
  because a light that moves has somewhere to be, and whatever carries it may be carrying Venus
  too. This is a deliberate plant: chapter 02's catastrophe turns on a light that moves wrongly.
- **No vocabulary for what they see.** Venusians have no words for crater, star, planet, orbit,
  or moon, and must not be given them. Neith's craters are "circular mountain ranges, like spots
  with valleys in them." This is the single most important prose technique in the story: describe
  the sky through the eyes of people who have no inherited language for it, and let the reader
  supply the names. Never let the narrator know more than the characters do.

## Neith (the moon)

- Venus's moon. From the ground, "the second light," a grey smear no spotting glass can resolve.
  From altitude and up close, solid ground with a hard edge. In the Venusian language, **"Neith"
  literally means second ground**, which is why Naelis gives it that name.
- Orbit is around 11 days 3 hours. Its distance from Venus is about 20 Venus diameters. See Units
  of Time, Decision #4, on what unit that figure is stated in.
- Airless, which is exactly why no one ever wondered what its sky looked like until they needed
  clear sky for astronomy. Its value as an observatory site is simply that: clear airless sky and
  a solid, stable place to stand. It is not sold or understood as being fixed in the sky.
- Moves through its phases as it orbits.

## The twin world (Earth)

- Catalogued by the observatory as Venus's "twin": a pale, marbled, blue-and-white disk, unlike
  the flat grey of Neith.
- Has so little air that it never shows a steady, permanent face the way Neith does, and what air
  it has is thin enough to see clear through to the surface.
- Its oceans are water, not the supercritical CO2 seas the Venusians know, which strikes them as
  strange. They assume nothing could live in water.
- Judged almost certainly lifeless: too cold, too thin-aired, too fragile, its seas the wrong
  chemistry for life. The dramatic irony (it is Earth, and it is us) is never stated in the text
  and must not be spelled out.

## The catastrophe (for continuity; mostly chapters 02-03)

- A wandering light turns out to be falling, not orbiting. Dalon works the path; it is inbound,
  roughly half the size of Neith.
- Every possible path is charted publicly; the cone narrows over three weeks.
- It misses Venus (passes about a thousand miles clear) and strikes Neith on its outbound path,
  destroying the observatory and all forty-plus people on it instantly. Venus grieves and
  celebrates its own survival in the same breath.
- Neith shatters into a debris field that does not stay in orbit. Fragments deorbit onto Venus
  over the following years and generations, cracking the crust, waking faults and volcanoes,
  flooding the atmosphere with sulfur. Over about two hundred years, seas turn lethal, air turns
  unbreathable, and every trace of life and the civilization is erased. The air becomes the
  permanent yellow haze of the present day.

## Characters

Genders below reflect the pronouns used in the text; keep them consistent.

- **Naelis Kerr** (he): test pilot, thirty-four, from Meriddon. A poor flyer as a boy, expert now.
  Makes the first clear observation of Neith's true nature and the stars; coins "Neith, second
  ground." Chapter 01 protagonist.
- **Avel** (she): the supervisor Naelis reports his discovery to. Cautious about going public.
- **Dr. Sélaire** (she): one of the first astronomers, in a generation that had no such thing to

  three-inch lenses and takes *The Hundred Lights*, the most awe-inspiring photograph Venus has
  ever seen, and then argues that this is the ceiling of what a freighter can do: astronomy
  through six miles of moving weather, from a platform that will not hold still, in the twenty
  minutes it can loiter before it comes down. Her own photograph is the exhibit for her petition.
  She proposes the permanent observatory on Neith and dies long before it is finished, its
  construction spanning forty years. The observatory carries her name. Note the order the reader

- **Dr. Serand** (he): scientist who voices the objection that there is nowhere stable to build.
- **Tirian** (she): astronomer at Sélaire Observatory, roughly forty-plus years after the

- **Dalon** (he): astronomer at the observatory who first realizes the wandering light is falling
  toward them. Dies in the impact.
- **President Tavon** (she): head of state. Delivers the address during the asteroid approach and,

## Terminology and prose conventions

- Demonym: **Venusian** (never "Venetian").
- The Sun is always "the great light" / "the one great light" / "the prime light," never "the
  Sun."
- Neith is "the second light" (from the ground) or "second ground" (its resolved, named form). In
  the Venusian language the word "Neith" means second ground; people stand on the ground, and
  Neith is a second ground.
- The stars are "the hundred lights." Do not use "hundreds of lights."
- There is no "Ceiling." That term is retired. Use the sphere language: visible sphere, invisible
  sphere, emptiness.
- Cultural attitude to fold into prose where relevant: visible air is breathable and safe to fall
  in; invisible air is untrustworthy.
- **No em dashes anywhere in prose.** Hard rule. Use a period, comma, or colon.
- Capitalization: all sky terms are lowercase in prose. "the prime light," "the second light,"
  "the great light," "the hundred lights." Never "the Second Light."
- Voice: close third with procedural grounding and restraint for the character and science scenes;
  a more lyrical, elegiac register reserved for the framing quotes and the final chapter's slow
  apocalypse. Emotion is delivered through action, not stated.
- Section breaks are `***`. No epigraph tables inside chapters; the framing Wikipedia line appears
  only at the very end of the final chapter.

### Do not reach for importance

This is the standing failure mode of this manuscript and the rule that overrides stylistic
instinct. Earlier drafts carried roughly one straining line every two paragraphs. The subject
matter is the discovery of the universe and the death of a world. It does not need help sounding
important, and every nudge from the narrator makes it smaller.

A previous version of this file instructed that "each section tends to land one quiet
forward-shadowing line." **That rule is deleted.** It took a move that works once, in chapter 02
("That was, later, the part she found hardest to forgive herself for"), and made it a per-section
requirement, which turned a good instinct into a tic. At most one such line per chapter, and only
inside a character's perception, never the narrator's.

The operative test: **if the significance is in the narrator's voice, cut it. If it lives in a
physical detail or a character's mouth, keep it.**

Cut on sight:

- Narrator-asserted weight the scene has not earned. "She had no answer for that. Nobody would,
  for a long time." "None of them had a name. Nobody had ever needed one." "because someone had
  to be first to say it."
- Similes that explain what an action means instead of letting it mean. "Avel set the report down
  slowly, the way a person sets down something they have started to suspect is heavier than it
  looks." The action alone is the line: "Avel set the report down slowly."
- Manufactured aphorism. "a thing that moved was a thing with somewhere to be." "He had not been
  fearless, and he had not been good."
- Sentences that sound like conclusions but carry no content. "Everyone agreed it meant
  something."
- The tell-phrases "in the particular way," "the way anything true eventually does," and any
  clause personifying the sky.

Keep, by contrast: "But he never stopped playing." "Circular mountain ranges, like spots with
valleys in them." "Somewhere a person could finally hold still and look." The first is plain and
does the character work; the second is a man reporting what he sees with no word for it; the
third is a scientist making a practical argument in a meeting.

A related and separate flaw, easier to see once the ornament is gone: **exposition routed through
a character's mouth.** Dialogue like "Scientists tell us nothing is up there, and there is no air
to hold it up if there was" is the author briefing the reader. Stripping the reaching does not
fix this; watch for it independently.

## Framing device

The story opens and closes on the real historical record of "Neith," a moon of Venus reported by
several astronomers between roughly 1645 and 1770 and never confirmed. Styled as a Wikipedia
quotation. The framing deliberately focuses on the 1769 transit of Venus to sharpen the moment
Neith vanishes from the record. (A 1761 transit also showed no moon, but the story stays with
1769.)

- Opening line: *"Neith was first observed by Cassini in 1672, and subsequently observed dozens of
  times by other astronomers."*
- Closing line: *"Neith was not seen in the transit of Venus in 1769. After 1770, it was never
  seen again."*

The gap between the clinical external record and the intimate catastrophe it is actually
describing is the emotional engine. Do not resolve or soften the irony.
