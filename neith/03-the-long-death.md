# Neith

## Chapter Three: The Long Death

The largest pieces came down within the week.

There was no gentleness to any of it. What had been a single grey coin in the sky, unchanged for all of memory, became, in less than a second, a spreading field of debris, and the field did not stay politely in orbit the way the intact moon had. Fragments the size of cities crossed the gap between Neith's old path and Venus's surface in days, not years, and where they landed the crust did what crust does when something strikes it hard enough: it broke. Fault lines that had held quiet since long before anyone had thought to name the second light woke up all along the impact belt, and volcanoes that geologists had spent careers calling extinct proved, within a month, that no one had ever actually checked.

It took the survey teams that same month to understand what they were looking at. Ash columns visible from Tavilly. Tremors strong enough to crack foundations two thousand miles from the nearest strike. And underneath all of it, in every reading anyone took, a number climbing in the atmospheric samples that nobody wanted to be the first to say out loud.

"There's no reason to tell them yet," the science minister told President Tavon, in the closed room where these things were decided before anyone outside it had a vote. "Not until we're certain."

They were certain within the month. After that, the only question left was how much longer there was a reason not to.

***

Tavon went live from the same podium she'd stood at the week the falling light had made its final approach, and she did not bring a scientist with her this time.

"Citizens of Avinel. Citizens of all of Venus." She had written the speech three times and thrown out the first two. "I have spoken with our scientists at length, and I owe you what they've told me, exactly as they told it to me."

She let the silence hold a moment before she went on.

"Neith did not simply fall once. It is still falling. Pieces of it will continue coming down for longer than any of us will live to see the end of. Each strike is breaking our crust further. Each one is putting more into the air than the air can hold. Our scientists tell me the sulfur content is already climbing faster than any model predicted, and it will not stop climbing." She did not look away from the camera. "Our civilization will die. Not today. Not this year. But it will die, and in the centuries after that, the acid in our air will finish what the debris started, until there is nothing left standing anywhere on this world that could tell anyone we were ever here."

There was no cheering this time. There was no crowd in any street on the planet who mistook this broadcast for anything but what it was.

***

What followed did not happen quickly, and it did not, in the end, take very long either, measured against how long the world itself would go on existing without anyone left to measure it.

The coasts went first, drowned under seas turned the wrong chemistry to support anything that had once lived in them. The cities that moved inland bought themselves decades, not centuries, before the air itself stopped being breathable at any altitude a lung could survive. Somewhere in that stretch of time, unrecorded because there was no longer anyone whose job it was to record such things, the last transmission from the last surviving research station went quiet, and nobody was left who noticed it happen, and nobody was left after that to have noticed anything at all.

The rains that had once simply been rain became something with no name that meant anything kind. The sky, which had shown every child on the planet the same soft grey nothing for as long as there had been children to see it, thickened past that softness into a yellow, closed, permanent haze, and it did not lift, and there was no one left beneath it to have hoped that it might.

Two hundred years after the day the falling light struck Neith, the last visible trace of anything Venusian hands had ever built went under, and there was nothing left standing anywhere on the surface of the world that could be found again by anyone who came looking. No record. No ruin. No bone.

Above it, unchanged, exactly as it had always been, the one great light that had given Venus its every day kept its old patient distance, indifferent to the fact that it now shone on nothing that could look back up and call it by name.
