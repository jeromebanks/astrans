Bastien Laurent never sketched on his slate. He had a B3 hardboard portfolio case filled with cream sketch paper. Marc Lafevre was watching him work under the sterile lighting of Lunar Tug’s Martian ferry. Bastien was drawing the interior of a dome. “Marc, look at Ellie’s pictures. Bounty is beautiful. I love the domes, how they blend in with the land, like they are supposed to be there. My hotel must be like that.”

“I’d have guessed those domes would be too plain for you, Bastien. Far from the classical French opulence of your *Le Maurice*.”

“Not at all. Ellie’s domes would look out of place in Paris, but Haussman architecture would look absurd in Bounty. I am working on a grand entrance to the Bounty Hotel. This would be travelers’ first contact with *L’Hôtel Bounty*. It is here that the patron must move from feeling comfortable to feeling well loved. A spacious dome. Inside, there is no counter, no separation. A hostess will greet them with a simple sparkling water, and check them in with their gear. We require a large seating area maybe with a fountain. Patrons may wish to get their bearings. Some may wash up in the pool.”

Marc looked at his drawing. It was a fifteen foot tall replica of Olympus Mons, with water running over the shield into a pool. Bastien had drawn two men in the pool *au naturel*. One was standing under a waterfall coming off the top of the shield. “That would be welcome. Using wipes to clean myself on this trip has been awful.”

Bastien winked. “Agreed. That is why I imagined the fountain. I am imagining what I want to be there when we arrive.”

“My goals when I arrive are much less beautiful. I need to increase ammonia production to support expanding agriculture. Did you try Astran Cuisine at *Le Puits Perdu*?”

“I just ate a roll. Earthy.” 

“I had the meal. Max paid for it. The absurdity of shipping food from Mars to Earth aside, the food is interesting and satisfying, and is in huge demand on the Moon and Ceres. They are expanding agriculture twenty fold. Max asked if I could expand *LDPE* production enough to meet that need. I can, but that is the wrong solution.”

“I thought *LDPE* was great. Admittedly, I heard that from Max, but what’s the problem?”

“*LDPE* is great. It is easy to manufacture, it mixes well with regolith, forming tough walls, and it is high in hydrogen, so it blocks radiation. I am planning to load it with boron for neutron shielding. It is easily foamed and it can be used to make sheeting, which is convenient. If you can only make one polymer, this is it.”

“You want to do more.”

“Right. Now they are buying ETFE sheeting from Earth for their greenhouse. We have some with us. It is stronger and clearer than *LDPE*, and is resistant to UV radiation. But shipping from Earth means they never have enough, and when it needs to be replaced, they have to sub in *LDPE* sheeting. I need to start making ETFE on Mars. I can reinforce it with aramid fibers and experiment with gauges until I find what we need. This means I need to find fluorine feedstock.”

“Where can you get that?”

“It’s not rare, but I need the right deposits. I need Callum to find them. Sulfur, too.”

“ETFE needs sulfur?”

“No, it’s a different issue. The primary use for *LDPE* is mixing with regolith to make buildings.”

“Yes, Ellie’s domes.”

“Exactly, but sulfur does that easier. *LDPE* is a radiation shield, but in places where radiation isn’t a concern, like foundations or greenhouse walls, sulfur-regolith concrete would be much faster, easier, and cheaper.”

“I am sure Max doesn’t mind the expense. Why are you worried about cheaper?”

“Cheaper means we can afford to build more, faster. Cutting costs means cutting total labor. If I can build the foundations for all those greenhouses with mined sulfur and loose regolith, I can put more effort into making ETFE sheeting.”

“Callum said he would point us to the right places, and I brought backhoes. Still, I don’t think Callum was completely happy with Max.”

“What do you mean?”

“Callum’s game is beryllium. Find a site, dig it up, make billions, find another vein, repeat. All these other minerals are a distraction.”

The ship fired its rockets to slow down and move into low Martian orbit. “Suit up,” the captain called over the loudspeaker. “We are about to depressurize.”

Bastien and Marc started getting their suits on, layer by layer. The captain had already begun moving the air into the long storage cylinders, bringing it from three psi travel pressure down to two psi minimum safe pressure. When everyone checked in that they were ready, the captain sped up the compressors, bringing the ship down to less than a tenth of a psi. Bastien watched a door close and heard nothing. 

They all assembled in the cargo bay. The captain’s voice now came over Local Band radio. “I need you guys to start loading up the cups. I will bring them in, you fill them with cargo. It is all color coded so you will know what goes with what. I hope you guys are good at tetris and Jenga.” The captain opened the bay door and there was a short puff as the remaining molecules of air escaped.

He tethered and scrambled out the door and on top of the ship. He came back down with a dixie cup the size of a semitrailer. “Red.” The team started loading up the red pallets. Weight was no problem. Arranging was. When everything was inside, they twist-locked the lid on. 

“What is this cup?” Bastien asked Marc.

“The moon is making more titanium than Earth, now. These are descenders used to drop from LEO to the surface. Lunar titanium cups and lunar heat shields, Earth made parachutes, electronics, and interiors. Did you think Lunar Tug was going to land?”

“Patrons would prefer that.” 

The captain verified the seal, and put it on the launcher. “We launch twenty degrees before we get to Bounty. Tell me when you see it.”

Bounty was far too small to see, but Olympus Mons was very clear. The ship lurched as the mass driver slowed the cup enough that it would intersect the Martian atmosphere. They watched as the lid started glowing white hot, then dulled, then a chute came out.

Green, Blue, Orange, and Yellow were all loaded and launched the same way. Then the captain came back with an unmarked cup with seats and life support gear. Everyone but the captain got in and buckled up. The captain moved them to the launcher. His voice came over the comm line. “Five Four Three Two One.” 

Instantly, Bastien couldn’t breathe. The launch was at eight g. But less than a second later, it was over. 

“Was that ‘comfortable’ or ‘well-loved’?” Marc asked when they could breathe again.

“We are going to have some unhappy patrons,” Bastien replied.

On impact with the ground, the cup flipped end over end twice, then rolled fifty feet before coming to rest against a boulder, leaving them upside down. No one said anything for a few seconds, until they were sure they stopped. One of Marc’s engineers was the first to release herself from the seat. She held onto the chair and landed on her feet. One by one they all came down and sat on what they thought was the ceiling.

Forty minutes later, there was a banging on the hull. Over the descenders FM radio, they heard Callum’s voice. “Goodday, mates. Anyone injured?”

Marc got on the radio. “Shaken, but not broken.”

“OK. I need you guys to release the lid. I can only take two of you at a time.”

Marc and Bastien watched the gyro take off with two of their staff. “Customer service may face some challenges,” Bastien confided. Marc laughed.

It was almost dark when Bastien and Marc finally arrived at Bounty. Pacha had prepared dinner for everyone. The ag dome was the only place large enough.

Jakob sat at the head table. He raised a cup of chicha. “Today we welcome two new teams to Mars. Sorry about your gear. It is scattered over forty miles. Tomorrow we will help you bring it in.” 

Marc didn’t say a thing. He just looked at Bastien, who rolled his eyes.

The food was good, better than what was served at *Le Puits Perdu.* They spent the night in the ag dome on cots. It was the best night they had in four months.

\*\*\*

After breakfast, Bastien insisted on a tour. Pacha and Mira took him to see the greenhouses.

Pacha wanted to start with greenhouse five. When they got through the airlock, Pacha and Mira removed their helmets. Bastien followed suit. 

“The air feels thin here. Are you sure it’s OK?”

“You see those?” Pacha asked, pointing to a cavy running under the leaves. “If they can run, we’ll be OK. Don’t exert yourself. We are at two PSI.”

“Why isn’t my blood boiling?”

“That’s not the real problem here. The real problem is the Armstrong limit. If the partial pressure of water vapor in your lungs exceeds the total pressure in the atmosphere, then you’re done. Here, the air is almost half water vapor, so you can breathe much easier. The other half is oxygen, with four percent *CO₂* and the balance sulfur hexafluoride. Your voice sounds normal, we are fairly safe from fire, and we don’t put too much pressure on the membrane.” Pacha pointed up at the clear sheet covering the greenhouse.

“So you can just walk around here? That’s amazing. This place smells wonderful. Why can’t you do this under Bounty’s dome?”

“It’s too big. The sheeting would never hold. Bounty has ten pascals overpressure. The strength of the sheets limits the width of the greenhouse.”

“What are you growing in here?”

“Pretty much everything. The purpose of this space is to raise cavies, let them graze, and feed the soil. Growing soil is the hardest part of Martian agriculture.”

“How about trees?”

“The sauco can grow forty feet tall. Here I keep it trimmed to six.”

“Can I keep them in pots?”

“They may be hard to keep watered, and they won’t grow as tall, but you can do it.”

“Why are you asking so much about the sauco tree?” Mira asked.

“Forgive me. I don’t mean to interrogate. But this space you have is beautiful. I must have such a space for *L’Hôtel Bounty.*”

Pacha looked at him funny. “A pasture in a hotel?”

“A park. People walking slowly, looking up at the night sky, sitting under a tree during the day. Flowers blooming, little beasts running around. And your pond\! I must have a pond. Do you have geese?”

Pacha laughed. “No way. Nothing bigger than a cavy. The only thing that flies are these butterflies,” he said, pointing to a few resting on the membrane.

“You have butterflies? You buried the lede, Pacha. Why do you have butterflies?”

“The air is too thin for bees. I have some beetles for pollinating, but butterflies do well for several plants. These are called Painted Ladies. No problem at this pressure. They seem to like the high humidity.”

“You are aware they are gorgeous, aren’t you? Are they poisonous or anything?”

“No, the cavies eat them sometimes with no problem. Not often, though. Cavies can’t fly.”

Pacha next took him to greenhouse twelve. When they came through the airlock, Bastien reached for the helmet. Mira noticed and jumped to slap his hand away. “No\! Stop\!”

She had knocked him over, so he stood back up. 

“In the pasture, you can take your helmet off. Here you can’t breathe. Running less oxygen helps the plants grow faster, and we don’t keep it as warm, so there is much less moisture in the air. More nitrogen and argon. This is for plants only,” Mira explained.

Pacha showed him around. “The amaranth grow well here. We share this with the cavies. They actually eat more than we do. The leaves make a good salad or cooked greens, and the seeds are grain. That’s where we get our bread and our chicha. We grow many types of plants, but this is our staple.” 

“These are all golden. In the pasture I saw some red ones, too.”

“I raise both, but more golden.” 

“Pacha, have you seen a field of wheat or corn? Soothing, but not beautiful. These are beautiful.”

After the greenhouse tour, they walked back to Bounty. Pacha elbowed Mira and switched to an FM band. “Mira, this guy gets it.”

“Gets what?”

“He loved the greenhouses. Jakob thinks they make food. Bastien sees they make life. He thinks its beautiful.”

“Jakob had to eat only algae biscuit soup for months until you got it growing. He has a different perspective. When Bastien eats at a restaurant with two michelin stars he thinks he is slumming.”

“Whatevs, Mira. He gets me.”

That night, at dinner Marc and Bastien sat with their teams. One spoke up. “You know what they made us do today? They made us trek all over Mars and haul back our luggage. It’s not our fault the equipment was scattered all over.”

Bastien looked at him. “You are a great chef. We need you. But we need everyone else here, too. Whose fault was it? Jakob’s? The miners? Who should take time out of their day to take care of us?”

“The staff can get it.”

“That’s us. We are the staff. Tomorrow Marc and I will join you. How much is left?”

“We found all the containers, but only brought two back.”

“OK. Tomorrow we get the other three. And if we don’t finish tomorrow, you and I can finish the next day.”

“*Oui, Monsieur Laurent.*”

\*\*\*

Over the next week, Bastien went everywhere in and around Bounty with his portfolio case, making hundreds of sketches, ignoring Lafevre and the rest of the camp entirely. Jakob kept asking him how it was going, and all he said was he was thinking.

Finally, Bastien agreed to describe his plans. “Beauty comes from order and contrast. The opposite of beauty is not ugly, it is disorder and monotony. Here we have the mountain, the scarp, and the plain. Beautiful. This ag dome. The colors and texture are of Mars. The shape mirrors the craters. Beautiful.”

Ellie adjusted herself in her chair, sitting straighter.

“*L’Hôtel Bounty* must fit in and stand out.” He took a drawing from his portfolio, the one he had been working on in transit. “Here is the grand entrance. A cableway brings patrons on a gondola. They emerge relaxed and calm, their baggage with them. They are immediately welcomed and cared for.”

Callum looked quizzically. “So, I pick them up from a descender and put them on a gondola?”

“That would never work. Do you know how you land on the Moon? Tan has built a small mass driver that helps slow incoming ferries and gives them a boost on launch. When it lands, the ship goes through an airlock. When you step out, you are in shirtsleeves. When you are ready to return, you are again in shirtsleeves. It is calm and relaxing.”

Jakob raised his hand. “Sure, but the Moon has much less gravity. Lunar Tug could never get back into orbit. It is tight as it is.” 

“Tom, your mass driver is sixty miles long.”

“Sixty-three.”

“The Moon has a driver about a mile long. We can do this. The first stage of *L’Hôtel Bounty* cannot be a room. It is a depot. Lunar Tug comes in, refuels, and leaves. Before we disembarked from Lunar Tug, I looked at the captain’s face. I saw sorrow. Resignation. He was looking at six more months to get back, without even a break when he got here. Imagine instead, he came down, landed with us, ate with us, refueled, and went home much faster. That is the beginning of hospitality.”

“I’m pretty sure Max expects rooms and beds,” Jakob said.

“When you were telling me about Ellie and the domes, you said Ellie knows the difference between the structure and the building. Power. Air. Thermostatic control. Plumbing. Cooking spaces. Max expects patrons to enjoy their experience. We will get to structures, but that isn’t where we start. A depot, a gondola, and an entrance are more important than a thousand rooms and beds. If they sleep in the ag dome, packed like sardines, they will have been welcomed and treated with dignity.”

Tom raised his hand. “Your design doesn’t have an airlock. Are those revolving doors?”

“Yes. An airlock is designed for efficiency. A revolving door can do the same thing, but it is designed for the user. Foot traffic enters and exits through a series of three revolving doors. Here, above the doors, is the air pump, constantly, silently moving air from the entrance side to the exit side. Patrons would be unaware of this. They only feel welcomed.”

Bastien pulled out another sheet, and stuck it with pushpins on the wall above him. “*L’Hôtel Bounty* is part of Bounty. You can see in this drawing how he must be designed. The entrance connects by a sealed walkway to the central building. It looks like one of Ellie’s domes, but it is larger and steeper. The ground floor has twenty suites. Not rooms. Spaces where a family can unpack their luggage and themselves. Above that would be *la grande salle*. Meetings, events, dining, dancing. The beautiful curved ceiling.”

Ellie said, “We don’t dance on Mars.”

“Pity. But perhaps that will change.”

Ellie blushed.

“Around this, we put in smaller domes. Each will have a restaurant and rooms. Smaller spaces. We will add more as we gain a measure of demand. We must satisfy Jakob’s need for rooms and beds.”

Everyone laughed. It was Jakob’s turn to blush.

“But here,” he said, pointing to the space next to the walkway between the entrance and the central building, “Nothing must ever be built. Connected by a revolving door, the pressure is lower here. This space is a park. Flowers. Trees. A pond. Meandering walkways and benches. Those little beasts running everywhere.” He pointed to the cavy pen in the ag dome. “No pen. Unfettered, inviting the patrons to enjoy the space as they do. Marc tells me he will be making a strong, clear ETFE membrane. We use this, allowing an unobstructed view of the stars, sometimes even of Earth. And these.” In a smooth gesture, he released five butterflies from his pocket, letting them flutter around the ag dome.
