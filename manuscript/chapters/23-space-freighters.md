Andrei Volkov and Max Juno climbed the narrow stairs into the jet. Once inside, the cabin closed around them, quiet except for the low vibration as the engines rose. 

Volkov settled into his seat, eyes half-closed, thinking of the work ahead. “Max, what should I know about Nakamura? How should I speak to him?”

“Nakamura is a friend and the lead investor in Kagutsuchi Vortex,” Max said, seizing the point. “He is grateful for the way Raines made that investment so valuable. He will take care of you. He is well-connected with the Japanese industrial technology sector. He will be able to help us find the right companies to make the equipment you need. You just need to be open and honest. He won’t play any games.”

Hours passed in silence. Volkov’s hands were steady, but his pulse wasn’t. He was about to bet everything on ships that didn’t exist for markets that might never form. Just like the lunar helium rush. Buy rockets, hire crews, and pray the business materializes. It had almost bankrupted him then. Now the stakes were higher, and he was no longer young.

Mercifully, fatigue overtook him on the long flight. When Tokyo’s haze finally appeared on the horizon, Max leaned across the aisle. “We are here, Andrei.”

Volkov nodded as the jet sank toward Haneda’s runways, his mind spinning. He’d come to build his company from cis-lunar courier to the mid solar system’s transport operator. If Nakamura could help, fine. If not, he would find another way. He would not stop until Lunar Tug reached its next horizon.

They arrived at the Shibata Capital Tower in the early morning. Not quite empty, but very quiet. A receptionist was waiting for them. She led them through a wood-and-glass corridor to a small conference room with a low table, no screens, and insulated walls. Nakamura was seated inside. 

Max gestured toward their host. “Nakamura, this is Andrei Volkov. He is the CEO of Lunar Tug. Raines has asked me to help him.”

Nakamura inclined his head, his tone measured but curious. “I know of Lunar Tug, though we haven’t crossed paths before. A friend of Max is a friend of mine.”

Volkov extended his hand, his expression direct. “A pleasure. Lunar Tug is at the point where expansion is critical. I need new infrastructure. I must have orbital hubs to reduce the number of intercepts. But that is not all. I wish to extend my range to Mars and Ceres. I will need a new class of interplanetary ship.”

Nakamura studied him for a moment, weighing the words. “Ambitious. Tell me more.”

Volkov projected an image from his slate. “The hub will be a large warehouse and an even larger pole warehouse. Refueling stations. Living quarters for up to a hundred people with light rotational gravity. Importantly, it will need a soft mass driver for both braking and launch.”

“How do you intend to power this, Andrei-san?”

“Solar panels and batteries will be adequate. The mass driver will only operate at one G, but it will be a ten-mile-long cable.”

Volkov switched to another image. “A major headache has been descent to Earth. I will need the hub to be able to handle descender craft so no one needs to send a rocket up just to bring cargo down. I think Mitsubishi Heavy Industries would be perfect. They have made modules for space stations before and I am sure they are capable of delivering. I won’t need many. One for Earth. Maybe two, eventually. Another for the Moon, Another for Mars. It’s a short run.”

Nakamura was silent for a moment. “One design, three orbits. That’s efficient. Once proven in LEO, replication becomes straightforward. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries works on space modules. They are exceptionally capable and would be an excellent fit for your hubs. However, I know of a smaller company in Oita, Kagayaku. They do exceptional work, and are hungry for a project like yours. As one of their primary investors, I can tell you they always deliver above expectations.”

Volkov leaned forward, skeptical. “An unknown sounds like a risk. Are you sure they can deliver?”

“They can,” Nakamura replied evenly. “The modules they build now are already designed for orbital assembly. That foundation is solid. While it is a small company, they will prove to be exceptionally adaptable. Their success will be as important to them as it is to you. Mitsubishi can build a hub for you, but it will be their hub, their specs. I believe your requirements are quite specialized. Kagayaku will deliver precisely what you require.”

Volkov leaned back slightly, then straightened. “That sounds promising. I will speak to them. Now, let’s talk about ships. My fleet today is all short-hop craft, lunar surface to LEO and back. What I need are ships that can push farther, to Mars, Ceres, even Jupiter, and return safely. Can Kagayaku build them?”

Nakamura folded his hands, considering. “That scale of ship requires a larger yard. Ishikawajima-Harima Heavy Industries comes to mind. IHI can build hulls of that size and integrate advanced systems. The challenge, of course, is power. You know there is a ban on fissile material in space.”

Volkov gave a short nod. “I am not planning to rely on fission. I want the Kagutsuchi Vortex. It has the capability I need.”

Nakamura’s expression flickered with interest. “Fusion for transport. An excellent proposition. IHI could integrate the reactor. With that kind of drive, what exactly do you have in mind?”

Volkov sent the list of specifications for docking, fueling, and mass driver interface to Nakamura’s slate. “I need four categories. First, passengers. A fast bus, sixty seats, designed to minimize transit time. People must spend days, not months, between worlds. Second, a pole ship for oversized cargo: hubs, other ships, equipment to build a city, items that no other freighter can handle, but which can be safely attached to a pole. And third, general cargo. Enclosed, versatile, but not as heavy. The workhorse. It can carry passengers or cargo or both.”

Nakamura scanned the specs line by line. “And what is the fourth type?”

“A very fast ship. Multiple engines, less room onboard. Something that could reach the outer solar system, if we go there. We don’t need it yet, but I’d like them to work on it.”

Nakamura set the paper down, his expression unreadable, then allowed himself a slight smile. “It is a bold beginning. And with Kagutsuchi Vortex powering the fleet, IHI will see the prestige. But to produce so few is difficult for any firm. Designing a ship costs far more than building one. Perhaps we can consolidate the designs of the main three, so the differences are only superficial.”

Volkov thought a moment. With used equipment, development costs were someone else’s problem. But this project would be engineering from scratch, and suddenly those costs were his. He wouldn’t just be taking out a loan on his ships. His entire business would be in hock, and it still wouldn’t be enough. He met Nakamura’s eyes. The man wasn’t selling; he was guiding. That guidance was what Volkov needed. “Yes. Seven total ships, based on a common frame. Three freighters, two passenger buses, and two pole ships.”

Nakamura bowed slightly. “I will bring your request to IHI. Even with your detailed specs, they will not simply build this. You will need to be involved throughout the process. You understand that, right?”

Volkov smiled. “That sounds great. I look forward to hearing from them.”

Nakamura grimaced slightly. “Much of what they will be talking about will be delays and cost overruns. We all hate that, but it is a part of the business.”

Volkov cringed, but appreciated the honesty.

The three men rose together. They shook hands, and the room emptied as quietly as it had filled.

On the flight home, Andrei talked to Max. “Max. I specced what I needed, but the price I set is all I have and more. I have no way to deal with cost overruns. What have I signed myself up for?”

Max smiled warmly. “It’s a massive bet. But you’re building the infrastructure everyone will need. Raines knows that. So do I.”

Andrei nodded but said nothing. Max poured two vodkas.

Andrei drained his. “Na zdorovye.”
