# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 3: The Moon is Hard**

| Price of *³He* | $8,000,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **6 tons/year** |
| **Current Consumption** | **12 kg/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **2 months to 7 months** |

The wall pulled her. It looked like a fortress. She needed to see it up close. They took turns driving. There were eight days left before lunar night when they finally arrived. The slope towered over them, jagged and geologically young.

Li climbed down from the cab, boots sinking into the fines. She tilted her head back, following the wall up until it met the stars.

“Li, that is too dangerous to climb, and it will take more than a day,” Darrin said.

She looked at him. Then back at the wall.

“We have over a week until sundown. I want to climb this.”

They stopped at the base and ran the first anchors. The slope wasn’t vertical, but it was steep enough that the crawler’s drive alone wouldn’t hold. The fine regolith was more like silted glass than soil.

Li checked the line tension, then engaged the front winches. The cables drew tight, whining as the crawler began to pull itself upward. The treads fought for grip. The whole frame shuddered, metal straining against rock.

“Keep the pace slow,” Darrin said over the intercom. “Too fast and we’ll shear a line.”

Li didn’t answer. The crawler inched forward, steady but tense.

A hundred yards up, the left tread slipped, sending regolith out in an arc, scattering on the slope below. The crawler lurched sideways until the cables caught, jerking them to a stop. Darrin cursed, unstrapped, and climbed out to reset the anchor. He moved carefully, checking every clasp before locking it again.

Back in the cab, Li feathered the winch controls until the cables vibrated like bowstrings. She slowly engaged the treads, and the machine crawled forward another few meters.

They guided the crawler up the wall with winches and determination. The treads slipped and bit again, throwing regolith with each surge. They fell into a rhythm of stopping, driving new pitons into the slope, and setting fresh lines every twenty meters. They spoke little. There was nothing to say except what the equipment already told them.

Halfway up, Darrin’s voice came through again. “We’re off alignment. It’s pulling sideways in the rear, and the track is close to coming off. If we keep this path, we’ll twist the frame.”

Li paused the winches. “Then we reset.”

“That’s another hour.”

“Then it’s an hour.”

They reanchored, realigned, and started again.

The last few meters of the climb were smooth. The treads engaged well, pulling them onto the rim with a final surge of power. They still had six days before lunar night. 

Li sat back, breathing hard. Her heart hammered. Sweat slicked her suit liner. She looked out across the crater.

The floor spread below them, gray, smooth, and silent, fifty miles to the far wall. A central peak rose in the middle distance, stark against the black. Nothing moved. Nothing had moved here in a hundred million years.

She climbed out of the cab slowly, leaving the first footprints on the crater. She walked to the inner edge of the rim where the crater opened at her feet. It was vast, empty, and utterly still.

Something tightened in her chest. Not fear. Not relief. Something harder.

Hers.

“The rest of the Moon can keep its dust,” Li said. “This crater is ours. That wall is our claim, and anyone who wants to contest it will have to climb it first.”

She raised her survey laser and aimed it across the crater. A bright green pulse struck the far wall, and a scatter of regolith tumbled down, leaving a clear scar.

“And then face us.”

She didn’t look at him. She looked at the scar she’d made, at the crater spread below, at the walls rising in every direction.

Darrin said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly: “What if someone does come? What if they make the climb?”

Her lips smiled, but it didn’t reach her eyes.

“Then those cheechakos will learn here what we learned at Ball Crater.”

Darrin looked at Li and saw the quiet hardness around her mouth. It sent a chill through him.

He changed the subject. “Li, if we are staying here, let’s set up the panels on the rim. Inside, we will get more shadow.”

“Does it really matter?”

“An extra ten hours per cycle, compared to the plain, but an extra twenty hours compared to the crater floor. I think it matters.”

They set them on a prominence and ran electrical cables down into the crater. On the Moon, fuel didn’t work. Nuclear would work, but was impossible to license, so solar was king: cheap, predictable, and reliable, with batteries for lunar night. 

Then they anchored fresh lines and began their descent into the interior. The ratchet and pawl clicked in slow rhythm as they descended. They stopped on each terrace until the brakes were cool enough to proceed. The descent was steeper than the climb had been, and went down much further, but the winches did almost all the work, lowering them from terrace to terrace until they reached the floor.

Li looked across the floor of the crater. “That’s where we’ll live,” she said, pointing to the central prominence. 

Darrin sighed. He would have preferred someplace closer, but the prominence would be fine. They unloaded their gear on the north side of the prominence and set up their hut. It looked so tiny in the crater. Darrin installed the Earth window so they could see Earth when lying down in bed. He set up a tube to protect the Earth view, then buried the hut in several feet of regolith, shielding it from temperature swings, micrometeoroids, and radiation. Inside, earthshine cast a blue glow, a steady reminder of home.

The first survey sweep crushed Li’s optimism. 3.2 parts per billion on the western floor. Four point eight near the central peak. She’d hoped for fifteen, more typical of the lunar surface, but Tycho was young and hadn’t had time to accumulate that much helium.

Darrin saw it in her face. “How bad?”

“One cycle to fill one bottle. Maybe. We have to work hard and smart.” She showed him the numbers. “We skip the first rendezvous, catch the second one. If the yields hold, we can ship a full bottle then. That should be worth enough to start making improvements.”

Darrin filled the chamber, and Li began their first extraction. She monitored the residual gas analyzer, narrowing the resonance window until the *³He* nuclei’s Larmor frequency matched the RF’s output. A microtesla drift, and the energy would bleed into the *⁴He*. She balanced maximum extraction with purity, settling on ten percent *³He*. She triggered the pulse, feeling the hum shift as the RF energy coupled directly into the target atoms, vibrating them loose from the mineral lattice while the surrounding regolith stayed cold and silent.

The extraction process was relentless trial and error. The RF extractor hummed beneath her boots, pulsing energy through regolith. First load: 4.7 milligrams. Not enough. Not nearly enough. She adjusted the magnetic field and extended the timing. Load by load, yields improved. 

“We hit eight\!” Li exclaimed. “That’s the target. If we maintain this level, we can fill a bottle per cycle. We are moving a lot more regolith than we planned, but we can do it.”

Darrin smiled weakly. “OK, Li.” He knew he would have to be as efficient as she was when it was his shift on the RF. The RF extractor defined them. It had to run constantly during the lunar day, or production would lag. Sweeping was much simpler. They could pile up an Earth day’s worth of regolith in six to ten hours. But they took shifts sleeping out of sequence, so someone was always up, ensuring the RF extractor never stopped.

The sun dropped below Tycho’s western rim. There was no dusk; the world became blue with earthshine. The temperature outside plunged to a hundred seventy below zero Celsius within hours. The solar panels up on the rim gathered power for a few more hours.

Inside the buried hut, the heaters for the algae tank kept them warm. In the last twenty minutes of solar power, they had both taken a brief shower before shutting off the water heater. Li was last. There was no door or privacy. The water was all redistilled for reuse. When Li stepped out of her brief shower, she looked at the name tag on her pressure suit. TAN, LI YUN. Because of the mix-up, everyone on the Moon called her Tan. Except Darrin. He got it right. He’d always gotten it right. 

She dried off and put on her inner layer. They wouldn’t wear their pressure suits until they were ready to go out again, after lunar night. She looked over at Darrin, who had done the same. He was inspecting his suit. She followed his lead. 

Darrin caught her looking at her name badge. “Why don’t you get that corrected?”

“Wasn’t important enough to delay our launch then; don’t care what people here call me now.”

In spite of the cold, heat loss through the regolith was slow. Li and Darrin shut down everything but life support. The only light was coming from the algae system. Just air recycling and warmth, and a little heat for cooking.

She sat at the console, legs folded under her, eyes half-closed. Darrin handed her a bowl of algae broth seasoned by a flavor packet with the vague taste of shrimp. She took it without thanks. The packets from Earth made it nutritionally complete and more palatable. It was not so much that it was bad as it was monotonous. 

“I miss the flavors I grew up with in Kuala Lumpur,” Li said.

“Maybe next time we order, we can get some curry fish head flavor packets.”

Li kept an eye on the power meters. She had calculated how much battery capacity they would need and added twenty percent. More batteries would have meant fewer solar panels. 

Day six, Darrin was watching Li check the monitors. “Are we going to live through it?”

“I’ve worked in power since they died. If the city had power, the ambulances could have gotten through in time. We’ll have enough.”

Day twelve of darkness, battery at twenty-six percent. They had let the *CO₂* climb to four thousand PPM, making it harder to concentrate, but reducing the electrical demands on the algae system. By day thirteen, the batteries had dwindled to nineteen percent.

“Twenty-six hours,” Li said, looking at the phase of the Earth.

On day fourteen, the sun returned. It hit the panels on the rim ten hours earlier than it hit the interior of the crater. The batteries began charging. They’d made it through their first lunar night. Li thought back to the wrecked rover. Not everyone did. 

They set the *CO₂* levels back down to eight hundred PPM. Li took one long breath, then got back to work.

In the second cycle, they were faster. They had a better strategy for moving regolith, they knew how to manage the magnetic field on the extractor, and had better routing for the crawler to minimize time between loads.

Darrin learned to anticipate failures before they happened. A slight vibration in the RF extractor housing meant a mounting bolt had worked loose. A half-degree temperature drift meant a seal was starting to degrade.

By the end of the second cycle, they’d pulled almost a hundred grams. They had started on their second bottle. 

The next night, they stopped with four hours of sunlight on the panels left, making sure to fully charge the crawler. They rested as before for ten days, then they loaded the bottle into the crawler along with every blanket they had and made the climb out of Tycho. The climb out was easier because they had left the cables mounted on the rim. The winches pulled them up and lowered them down. They kept it slow and cautious. When they got to the rendezvous site, about fifty miners were there waiting, quiet and watchful. This was their first time around other miners since Ball Crater, and Li was uneasy. They stayed in their crawler, covered in blankets, keeping warm. 

The cargo ship landed twenty hours before dawn. Li read the manifest. It listed the pilot as Andrei Volkov. The name didn’t ring a bell.

When Andrei climbed out, he recognized Darrin and Li immediately. “Li, Darrin, how are you?”

Li recognized the voice. He was the pilot who had ferried them here. She looked at his face. Different in a helmet, but definitely the same man. “Hi, Andrei\!” 

Andrei broke into a wide grin. “Come, see my ship.” He brought them over to his ship, where he had stenciled “LUNAR TUG” on the hull. “I incorporated in Miami. This is a growing business.”

“Smart move. Are you expanding?” she asked.

“As fast as I can hire pilots. Rendezvous are a huge business, even bigger than fares.” 

Most of the miners were here to ship helium to Earth. Andrei weighed each bottle, with no concern for what was in it. His only concern was the cost of shipping. Assay and helium purchase were handled by Helion Dynamics after the bottles got to Earth. Li shipped their first bottle to Helion Dynamics. The QR code for her account was engraved on the bottle. 

After he loaded their bottle, Li and Darrin said their goodbyes.

“Andrei, nice to see you again. We will see you at the next rendezvous.”

“No, don’t go, wait until the business is done. I have something for you.”

Li looked at Darrin. They negotiated with their eyes. “Sure, we can stay for a bit.” 

Andrei continued chatting as he processed everyone’s bottles. “So you two were headed for Ball Crater, right?”

“Yeah, well, change of plans. We ended up in Tycho.”

“Tycho\! That’s too far. Why would you go there?”

“It was available.”

“Da, everything on the Moon is available.”

With all the bottles processed, Andrei started distributing packages that people had bought. Water and air dominated, but replacement parts and other enhancements were also common.

When everything had been delivered and all the other miners had left, Andrei showed them what he had for them. “See these? Extra water, oxygen bottles, even some batteries this time. People paid for them, so I brought them. But the buyers of these packages darksided before I landed. It happens on every rendezvous.”

“You are giving them to us?”

“*Da*, I don’t have any reason to haul them back to LEO. You keep them.”

Li picked up one of the water containers. A family photo and inscription were laminated to the side. She set it down in the crawler, facing inward. They never got to say goodbye, either.

“Thank you, Andrei. This will be very helpful.” 

The sun hit some peaks around them. They were burning daylight. They needed to get back to extracting to fill the next bottle. To repeat the cycle.

Tycho was too far from the rendezvous for most miners. The climb in and out was hard, and the yields were not good. Those challenges gave them security. No one else would want what they had.

It was thirty hours into the lunar day by the time they made it to their hut. They started the RF extractor as soon as they got back. They had left the hopper half full. They both went straight to work, collecting regolith, extracting helium, and bottling it.

It would be a week before their shipment was assayed and sold. A week before, they’d know what it was worth, before they’d have money to improve anything. For now, there was only the work.

When the deposit finally hit, they cleared $730,000. Helion Dynamics took $146,000 to service the loan, leaving her with $584,000. Li stared at the number. “Darrin, it’s shopping time. If we order now, they will deliver it at the next rendezvous. Besides water and oxygen, what do we need?”

“Real food. I hate algae biscuit soup.”

“It’s nutritious, and it recycles our waste,” she chided. “Everyone on the Moon is boiling those biscuits.”

“Doesn’t make it good.” 

“No. But it keeps us alive.” She pulled up the logistics map. “We will need air, water, and food. Every time out of the airlock, we lose a little air and water, even though we pump it down. And our suits are well sealed, but some escapes while we are outside. My second priority is getting the equipment we need to make this all more efficient. First, batteries. They didn’t last as long as I expected last time.”

The rhythm became a relentless, exhausting routine. Mine, extract, bottle, sell. Only one of them would go to the rendezvous, so the other could start the RF extractor as soon as there was enough power. It was a hard, brutish existence. 

Four months in, Li tallied their production. Two hundred eighty-three grams so far. They were making it. They could afford real food now, not just algae. They filled a bottle every cycle and had enough profit for improvements: more batteries, more panels, and a fractional distiller, separating pure *³He* from the *⁴He* and other gases. Purified, they could put ten times as much *³He* in a bottle. Li calculated that shipping costs were over 15% of their income. Distillation cut shipping to less than 2%. She hated making the run every lunar night, anyway, time away, needless risks, power consumption. Li smiled to herself.

Darrin was doing maintenance on the RF. He pulled the housing cover and cursed. Dust again. The regolith was fine as smoke and sharp as broken glass. The jagged microscopic shards had never known the smoothing effect of wind or water. They crept into every gap, abraded all the moving parts, and shorted contacts. Constant maintenance was the only way to keep the equipment running well.

Then she read the new claim filings. “Darrin,” she called.

He came over, reading over her shoulder. “Ocean State filed a claim west of Tycho crater. That’s fine by me. But the plat is rectangular, and it contains ten acres of our crater floor. We have to defend it.”

“They filed from Earth. Haven’t even landed yet.” He zoomed in on the overlap. “Probably don’t know we’re here.”

“We have to defend our crater, but they outnumber us. We can’t win a fight. We need to find another way.”

“It’s their legal claim, Li. Let it go,” Darrin suggested, but knew that suggestion would be rejected as soon as he said it. 

Li looked at the calendar. Twenty-one days until Ocean State arrived. Seven days of light, then two weeks of night. 

“We strip it,” she said. “We pull every gram we can before they arrive. We clean them out, then put the stripped regolith back, making them think it was never touched.”

Darrin stared at her. “Li, that’s…”

“What?” She met his eyes. “Illegal? They filed paperwork from Earth for a crater they’ve never seen. We’re here. We hold this ground. That’s the only law that matters here.”

He remembered Ball Crater. The green laser dot on his chest. He didn’t answer. 

“We’ve got seven days before lunar night,” she continued. “Double shifts. When they land, there won’t be anything left worth taking. This is our crater.”

They were fast and efficient. Sweeping was their priority job. This cycle, they would not hit their extraction goals, but their claim had to be defended. They worked in shifts. Li would sweep for sixteen hours straight. Halfway through, Darrin would wake up and run as much of it through the RF extractor as possible. When she was done, she would head to bed, and he would start sweeping, continuing the long, methodical sweeps. The regolith piled up, coming in faster than they could process it. 

They had the power to keep running the crawler through the lunar night, but not the RF. They knew their margins pretty well. They went more slowly, saving power, but still sweeping. A week into the night, she climbed out to clear a jammed valve. Her glove came back with a shallow cut across the outer layer. She checked it. The suit held pressure. Any rational person would have stopped. Li taped over it and continued. A day before lunar dawn, they’d stripped about eight acres of the overlap. Darrin began quickly covering the rest with processed tailings. A shallow assay would show this area had no helium. At the same time, Li kept extracting from what remained, pulling every gram she could.

Suddenly, Darrin’s voice cracked over comms. “Li… incoming. West ridge.”

Li froze, the sweeper still whirring beside her. 

One last bag. The scoop arm dipped, dragging another scoop into the sack.

She reversed the crawler, slow and deliberate, the bag less than half full.

“How many?”

“Three rovers. Heavy-frame. Ocean State livery.”

A tone hit her headset. “Unidentified vehicle, you are inside a mineral claim held by Ocean State Resources. You are in violation. Acknowledge.”

She keyed the mic. “Tan, Tycho Outpost. Leaving now.”

Static. Then: “Leaving with our regolith.”

Three rovers crested the ridge, moving faster than her crawler could retreat. They stopped thirty meters out. Three figures stepped out of the lead rover, suits marked in Ocean State blue. The other two vehicles waited behind.

She pulled her survey laser from its mount, not pointing it, just holding it, ready.

“Tan,” the lead miner said. “You’re actively collecting inside our claim.”

“Leaving your claim,” she corrected. The crawler kept backing away. Five meters. Ten.

One of them raised his survey laser, aiming toward her cab.

Li’s laser snapped up, centering on the leader’s helmet. Her arm was steady. The crawler continued to reverse, engine humming.

“This is an Ocean State claim,” the leader said. “You can’t take our ore.”

A fourth figure stepped from the rear rover and began taking samples.

“This crater is mine,” she said. “Not some of it. All of it.”

“It’s not yours, Tan. It’s ours. Leave the bag.”

The fourth figure crouched again, dropping fragments into his mass spectrometer.

“This area doesn’t make sense for you anyway,” Li said, still backing away. “Most of your claim is on the far side of the crater. You planning to haul your loads over that wall every shift?”

A bright green dot appeared on the second man’s chest. Perfectly round.

Everyone froze.

Li recognized it. Darrin had climbed the ridge behind the hut. He was dug in under regolith, safe from any return laser shots.

The Ocean State team stopped moving. Li kept backing away, her laser still trained. Forty meters. Forty-five.

“You can work this ridge if you want,” she said evenly. “But every time you leave, I’ll be back. Every time you’re over that wall, I’ll strip it clean. You’ll burn more fuel climbing than you’ll ever pull in helium.”

The fourth figure said something over a private channel. She couldn’t hear it, but she knew what he’d seen. Darrin had covered that section with stripped regolith. It would look worthless.

“This crater is mine,” she said. “I’m here. You’re not. Stay west of the wall. You’ll find more helium there anyway, and you won’t have to climb a mile every shift. Stay out of Tycho.”

Silence. Then the lead miner tilted his helmet, turned, and the three of them climbed back into their rovers. They headed west, over the ridge and out of sight.

Li cut the channel. When she could no longer see them, she jumped out of the rig and secured the bag properly. Her hands shook. Once the seals held, she turned the crawler around. Forward wasn’t much faster than reverse, but she wanted distance.

No one followed.

Back at the hut, the processors hummed, pulling scraps of *³He* from the dust. Darrin stood by the airlock, arms folded.

“You didn’t have to,” she said.

He shrugged. “Didn’t seem like they wanted to talk.”

She looked at him for a long moment, then turned to the gauges. “They’ll be back.”

“Maybe. That ridge slows them down. Easier to work their western sites. And that sample they pulled showed no helium.”

“They’ll remember your name now,” Darrin said quietly.

“They already did.” She checked the pressure readings.

They were preparing for the next cycle when Darrin pulled up a relay feed. “Another darkside incident, Li. Just discovered.”

Li glanced at the slate. Four miners. They’d pushed their extraction run too long, trying to fill one more load after nightfall. Batteries died two days before dawn. Their hut lost heat, then oxygen. By the time the sun came up, it was too late.

“They were gambling on one more load,” Darrin said quietly. “They thought they knew their capacity, but they overplayed it.”

“They lost.” Li stared at the screen. Four names, four families somewhere on Earth who’d get a notification. “That’s what the solar power does. It makes you choose between shutting down for two weeks or gambling with your life.”

She closed the feed.

“We can’t keep shutting down for two weeks every month,” she said. “We can’t continue depending on sunlight.”

She paused, then added, “We need a reactor. Something that runs through nightfall.”

He hesitated. “Nukes are banned, Li.”

She gave him a long look. “So is pulling helium from someone else’s claim.”

“That’s not the same…”

“The law only matters if someone shows up to enforce it. If I can get one, it changes everything. No more blackout periods. No more power rationing. Just work, all cycle long.”

“And if they find out?”

“They will. Eventually. But if we’re smart, we’ll be too useful to stop.” She paused, then added, “Out here, production keeps the peace.”
