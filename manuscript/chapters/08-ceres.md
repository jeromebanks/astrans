# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 8: Ceres**

| Price of *³He* | $6,000,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **95 tons/month** |
| **Current Consumption** | **91 ton/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **37 months to 60 months** |

Raines sat alone in the narrow conference room, eyes fixed on the empty chair across from him. The air was too cold. His tea cooled, untouched, beside a decision he did not want to make. He disliked what he was about to do, but the list of palatable options was gone.

He faced a wall of demand that was both exponential and geopolitical. Every nation wanted a terawatt-class tokaplex, and they wanted it now. A terawatt meant energy sovereignty, freedom from oil and coal, and independence in every negotiation.

Between that ambition and reality stood refractory metals. Ten-gigawatt plants were feasible, but costly; a terawatt-class core would consume more strategic metals than Earth could produce. The fusion age demanded mass. 

Doubling the spot price had increased mining. Production had risen an incredible twenty percent. But a twenty percent increase was never going to build terawatt tokaplexes. Raines had not taken the meeting with Chen Zhengrong lightly. He needed a tenfold increase, and he was done asking.

The Asteroid Belt held the minerals, but it was untested, hazardous, and expensive. Raines saw no alternative. Helion Dynamics would build its own supply chain of ore pulled from the asteroids, refined, and sent Earthward. A heavy-metal pipeline from the Asteroid Belt to the Pacific.

Hundreds might die before the first shipment reached Earth. That did not change his decision. Success would depend on the director.

Catherine Dahl had run hazardous mining projects: Antarctic mining under miles of ice, deep-sea mining at enormous pressures, and magmatic brine extraction from the roots of active volcanoes. She had always delivered the ore, even when the loss of life was catastrophic.

Ceres would be worse. The odds of the first crew returning whole were slim to none. They might all die. He needed someone who would do it again.

There were many people whom he might ask to run this project. Many would refuse, squeamish at the thought of blood on their hands. Others would paper over the problems with endless procedures. Then, when something inevitably did go wrong, there would be investigations, recriminations, and resignations. A stalled project. 

Dahl was different; she understood projects where miners die. She acknowledged the risk to investors and to the miners upfront, then delivered anyway. Not from indifference, but from clarity.

Raines didn’t need sentimentality; he needed metal. Dahl could deliver.

The door opened without a knock.

Catherine Dahl stepped in, unhurried. No slate, no assistant. Just a canvas jacket worn smooth at the elbows and a look that said this was already taking too long. She scanned the room, gray eyes fixed on Raines.

“Catherine Dahl\! It’s a pleasure to meet you. I trust your flight was pleasant.”

“What do you want?”

“Helion Dynamics is in a bind. We need refractory metals in enormous quantities. The Earth can’t supply us. We are going to the Asteroid Belt.”

Dahl pulled out a chair and sat.

“Ceres,” Raines continued. “Not a pilot project. Our supply chain would depend on steady, massive output from this operation. Mine the Asteroid Belt, smelt it on Ceres, ship it to Earth.”

She studied him, measuring the gap between words and meaning, then leaned back. “I’ve never mined in space,” she said dismissively.

“No one has,” Raines responded. “Who do you think would do a better job running this operation?”

“It’s not just technical,” she said. “Ceres will put crews under pressure, long cycles in low light, and almost no gravity. The kind of work where people make mistakes. Keeping them focused and productive will be the real job.”

“I have to have this metal.”

She held his gaze, then gave a single nod. Not acceptance, but willing to talk.

“Finding people for technical jobs on Earth will be straightforward. Hiring people to go willingly to Ceres is different. Were I to be involved, I would be honest about the risk upfront. The hires would need to be men who won’t fold when things go sideways. You cannot send men who can’t do the job. The training must be as brutal as the work, or they will break when they get on site. I’ve seen it happen.”

“I am not looking for a butcher. I expect the miners to be treated with respect and integrity. While that won’t make this operation safe, we can work out hazard pay and insurance policies honestly.”

Dahl watched the Jet d’Eau through his window. “I can get you your metal,” she said. “I expect to be fairly compensated.”

“Of course. Helion Dynamics will compensate you more than fairly. Bonuses for meeting early delivery goals will ensure our motivations align.”

“Yes, but there will come a time when I will take the fall.”

“Helion Dynamics would never do that, Catherine.”

“I appreciate what you are saying, Raines, but this is not about you.”

“What is it, then?”

“You are willing to accept harsh conditions and a high death toll. As we become successful, society will accept neither, and you will no longer be in control of the story. This is not about you, it is about Ceres.”

“What do you need?”

“A substantial stake in Helion Dynamics. Five percent.”

“Elin’s share is five percent.”

“Elin won’t be scapegoated.”

“You are asking too much. So am I. I will bring it to the board, but they will not approve this lightly.”

“Then make them understand the importance of this project. At that point, they will have no choice. When do we get started?”

“Here, now. This is not some far-off goal. I need metal yesterday. I have already lined up two General Atomics ships for this project. You can do whatever you want, but cargo launches in two months. Crew follows fifteen months later on a faster ship.”

Dahl did the math. “Power, life support, base construction, full mining and refining. All in orbit in two months?”

“You figure out what you need. But I expect metal flowing six months after the crew lands.”

She gave a low whistle. “That’s aggressive, Tobias. It will take everything lining up.”

“That is the requirement.”

Dahl looked in his eyes to see if this was theatrics. “OK, we will do it. The first interplanetary operation.” She was in.

“Who’s on the ground on Ceres?”

“No one yet.”

“You’re not sending me?”

“No. You stay Earthside. But you choose who goes. Your team, your rules.”

She closed her eyes for a moment, letting the scope of the job take shape in her mind. She pictured the crews, the equipment, the operation in motion. Then she said, “Dr. Silas Knox.”

Raines leaned back, skeptical. “Knox? He’s radioactive. Half the ports on Earth have his face on their banned miners list.”

“I know. That’s why he’s still sharp. No safety net. He can’t afford to be mediocre. And his brilliance makes him worth the risk.”

She slid a slate across the desk. Knox’s record was there in black and white. Brilliant work, often illegal.

“Knox doesn’t get second chances. He can’t waste a shot, can’t afford to screw up and wait for someone to catch him.” She leaned forward slightly. “He’s exactly as good as he has to be, which is better than anyone else.”

Raines studied her, then the file. “You sure he won’t bolt when things turn bad?”

Dahl’s mouth tilted toward a smile. “Out there, there’s nowhere to run.”

“It’s your team, Catherine. Your call.”

Knox had been Scripps Mining’s golden boy, a rising star in the cutthroat world of deep-sea mining. He didn’t just survive it; he thrived. He pulled off impossible recoveries, turned marginal claims into record-breaking yields, and built fortunes for his employers and himself.

For a time, he lived like a king: a mansion in San Diego, cars bought on impulse, parties where champagne flowed from taps. But it was never about the money. It was the sense that he could beat the ocean itself.

The ocean doesn’t play fair.

One bad payout, a mine collapse, furious investors, and panicked insurers left him over-leveraged. The mansion and the lifestyle became a noose.

He panicked. Took a quiet side job. Helped a shady outfit steal ore from a competitor’s reserve, replacing it with sea rock convincing enough to pass. No one saw it happen. It was perfect.

Until it wasn’t. A man he’d worked with was arrested on an unrelated drug charge and, in a plea deal, he named Knox. 

Knox did time. Lost his license. Lost everything except his ability.

After that, he worked where no one asked for papers, the gray zones where contracts were enforced with guns, and betrayal was just another line item. The jobs were brilliant. They were also illegal. Eventually, he stopped caring.

Two days later, Dahl found him in a half-lit cargo bay, elbow-deep in a crate of stolen spectrometry modules. The ship belonged to black market ocean miners, rough, half-legal, trawling the Atlantic floor for minerals no one could prove they had stolen.

“Dr. Silas Knox?”

He didn’t look up. “Depends who’s asking.”

“Catherine Dahl. I’m building a mining operation. Legitimate work. You would run site ops.”

That earned a flicker of interest. His eyes flicked to the shadows, still wary. “What kind of mining?”

She stepped closer, boots ringing on steel. “Ceres.”

Knox snorted, dry as cracked concrete. He straightened, wiped his hands on a rag black with grease.

“You serious?”

“Dead.”

He studied her, eyes sharp and cold, like a man measuring exits with nowhere left to go.

“You think I’ll live long enough to deliver metal?”

“You might.” She didn’t flinch when she said it.

The silence stretched. Dahl didn’t press.

Knox sealed the last crate, slow and methodical. When he straightened, his eyes were on the floor, as if the decision had been made before she even arrived.

“I got into ocean mining for the freedom,” he said. “You dropped off the map, ran your own operation, answered to no one but the weather.”

He glanced at the rust-streaked walls, the flickering lights, the smell of salt, oil, and rot.

“That’s gone. Now it’s just jurisdictions and contracts and lawyers with maps. You try to cut a trench, and five governments file injunctions before your rig hits mud.”

He met her gaze, and for a moment, the cynicism cracked.

“I used to build things that weren’t supposed to work. Projects that should’ve failed in weeks, yet I made them hold.” He swallowed. “I don’t do that anymore.”

Dahl said nothing.

“I thought maybe I’d crawl my way back. Do enough gray work, stay off the grid, wait for someone to forget.” He shook his head. “Earth doesn’t forget. Every mistake’s on record, every port scans your name, and the only thing they want is you gone.”

He looked toward the open hatch. Outside, the hull creaked with the tide. A cold wind cut across the dock.

“Ceres is a rock. No borders, no laws, no one telling you what can’t be done. It’s not clean or safe, but it’s empty. And empty’s the best deal I’ve had in a long time.”

He turned back to her. “I’m not doing this to run. I’m doing it because I remember who I was before I had to run.”

He stepped closer, boots ringing on the deck. “You’re offering me a hostile place with no rules and no margin for error.” His voice was steady. “That’s where I started. Maybe it’s where I belong.”

Dahl held his gaze. “When can you be ready?”

He stood up and walked out of the cargo bay without looking back. “I already am.”

Dahl built the home team. None of them would ever set foot on Ceres. Their job was to make sure everything that did go worked. She brought in Dr. Feld, a geologist with a long history in remote analysis. He would be searching the Asteroid Belt with a space telescope, cross-referencing spectral returns with mineral data. When samples came back from Ceres, he would read their structure, grade their potential, and flag new targets. He would be in charge of choosing which asteroids were mined.

Dahl’s system engineers went over the equipment lists. Make sure the team survives. Her chemical engineers focused on extraction. What to refine, how to do it, and what form the metal should take to survive reentry and recovery. If the chemistry didn’t work on Ceres, there would be no way to fix it, no way to refine that metal. 

The General Atomics spine ship: a long, skeletal truss with a nuclear-electric drive at one end, was already parked in high Earth orbit, its frame slowly filling with extractor ships, tenders, folding habitats, smelter kits, environmental modules, anything bulky or heavy they would need. It looked less like a spacecraft than a junk cart piled high. Once the ship left, there was no means of correction. That was final.

Food was the last thing anyone wanted to talk about. It came from algae and bacteria, grown in sewage and nutrient broth. The tanks would convert it into a slurry of macros, micros, and amino profiles. Balanced. Efficient. Knox sampled a swig during prep. It hit the back of his throat like rot and copper. He gagged. Swallowed. Didn’t complain. Told Dahl it was an acquired taste. Then checked the manifest for a redundant water processor.

Knox didn’t involve himself in the logistics. He skimmed manifests, asked a few questions, and turned back to what mattered, the crew.

While Dahl engineered the infrastructure from Earth, Knox focused on the people who would inhabit Ceres. His job was to hire the people who would live and die on Ceres. Miners, riggers, smelter operators. The crews who would drill into rock, refine metal, cast it into reentry shapes, and fire it toward Earth. They answered to Knox and Knox alone. 

Dahl wanted to start with fifty men on Ceres. Men who would not flinch. Skill was optional. Desperation was not. She told him to recruit two hundred.

“Do you want two hundred or fifty?”

“Fifty, plus a few that may go later. But I expect most candidates to wash out. If they are going to be a liability, I don’t want you coddling them. I want them out. If they get to Ceres and can’t handle it, everyone’s lives will be at risk.”

“What happens to the washouts?”

“That’s not our concern.”

“It is mine. I will know these people.”

“Be careful, Silas. Don’t get too close.”

He didn’t like it, but couldn’t fault her logic. He began recruiting. First, he talked to miners who had started as children and were now trapped in the most hazardous corners of the trade. They had lived with danger for so long that they treated it as routine. They had watched friends die and developed a practical instinct for when a job was drifting off script. They were quick on their feet. Ceres would be harsher than anything they had faced, but the pay exceeded what they were used to.

He next recruited from deep-sea mining crews. They worked in conditions that punished small mistakes. They understood pressure, machinery, and the routines that kept both from turning lethal. Knox had worked with some of these men, and they respected him enough to consider the offer seriously. Only a few were willing to sign on. They were not as desperate as the others and understood the risk. Knox was satisfied. The few he got would be enough.

He couldn’t promise them safety. Only work, and a paycheck if they lasted long enough to collect it. Their estates would be paid if they didn’t.

Dr. Knox finished selecting two hundred candidates three months after the cargo ship departed. In San Diego, they boarded two C-130J-30s. One held all their equipment, gear, luggage, and Catherine Dahl with a dozen of her technical staff. The other plane, the sardine can, was packed with two hundred candidates, facing the rear cargo ramp. Dr. Knox acted as their flight attendant. 

“Buckle up, this is going to be a long flight,” Knox said over the rising whine of the four turbines. “If anyone needs to use the bathroom or get nauseous, raise your hand and a plastic cup with a lid will be passed to you. When you are done, pass that back. I will empty it and rinse it in the washroom. There will be no food or beverages on this flight. You’ll thank me for that.”

They stopped at Dutch Harbor to refuel. As they descended, Knox noticed one of the fishing warehouses seemed to have a Helion Dynamics logo. He could only wonder why.

The stop was worse than the flight. The pilot parked on an incline, with the front of the plane being two feet higher than the back. A firehose was brought to the front door, and the rear cargo bay was opened. The cold air hit like a blizzard, turning the interior to fog.

“Everyone, feet up\!” Knox barked.

A few seconds later, seawater came out of the hose, washing the floor and pouring out the cargo ramp. When Knox was satisfied that the water was running clean, the hose was pulled, and the hold hissed shut. The temperature started to rise, as did the humidity and the marine stench.

Knox reappeared with a long pizza peel. “One cup per person. Drink it down, throw the cups forward.” He set the water on the pizza peel, eight-ounce cups, twenty at a time, reaching them out to the candidates. 

When everyone was done, they took off for Dahl’s training camp in the Gobi Desert. The plane only had enough fuel to get there. An hour past the Kuril Islands, Knox spoke up. “Brace yourselves. We are going into a toboggan maneuver.”

Their stomachs felt it as the plane pitched down, losing five hundred feet per minute. The plane sped up as it descended, pacing the KC-130 tanker. Sixty thousand pounds of fuel were transferred in about twenty minutes. They disengaged, and the pilot struggled to level the flight, then began a slow ascent. 

Six hours later, they saw the airport, a hardened, dirt track two miles long. They arrived on April first, during the Karaburan. The winds were over forty miles per hour, and the air was dark with dust. The sun was a pale disk. To the candidates, it looked like heaven. 

The other plane was already in a hangar and had been unloaded. They towed the sardine can into the hangar beside that, and everyone offboarded and scrambled to get their gear. They picked up their duffels, put on their coats, and headed to the barracks, a low, circular building made of rammed earth. 

The training hall had once been a warehouse. Fluorescent tubes buzzed overhead. Rows of folding chairs faced a cargo net.

Day one, Knox called them in for orientation. “I am looking for fifty people to go to Ceres, with another twenty in reserve. A couple of you may become trainers. For the rest of you, thank you for coming out. While you are here, you will become an expert with every piece of gear we will use on Ceres. If you cannot master it, a truck will take you back to town, and Helion Dynamics will fly you back to San Diego. Four of your classmates did not enjoy the flight out here and have already departed.”

He made a slow circuit while the trainees waited. Each had a survey laser in front of them, a foot long and an inch and a half in diameter, oversized for gloved use. Beside it sat a rock, a dummy mass spectrometer, and stainless steel cams they’d use to stay anchored.

When he had finished the circuit, Knox stood on a half-buried 55-gallon drum. He spoke calmly. “The rocks in front of you are hematite iron ore,” he said. “Dense, but the rocks on the Asteroid Belt will be even denser.”

“The extractor ship lands first on three legs. It drills in slowly. Anchors screw in, locking it to the asteroid. That is your home. You are always connected to the extractor.”

He gestured to the gear on the tables.

“Once the extractor’s anchored and your tethers are clipped, the hatch opens. Gravity’s point-zero-two. You’re not walking; you’re crawling. Every motion’s a maneuver. Every ten meters, you hit the rock with the survey laser, open a crack, and jam in a cam. Ours are stainless steel for durability. The extra weight won’t matter at the G forces you’ll face.”

He stepped down from the drum and moved between the rows.

You don’t trust your boots. You don’t trust handholds. You trust the line. If it’s not clipped, you don’t move.”

He paused.

“You fall off, gravity won’t bring you back. You’ll reel yourself in slowly. Stay calm. Pull gently. If you come off the tether, the extractor’s bolted in. It’s not coming to get you. Maybe another miner can, but don’t bet on it. Their hold is as tenuous as yours.”

A few chuckles. Knox didn’t smile.

Off to the side, Dr. Feld watched the projection. Then he looked down at the rocks.

“Go ahead,” Knox said, waving to the trainees. “Use the survey laser on your rock. Break off a chip.” He demonstrated, steady and simple.

The class leaned in. Gloves on. Fingers careful. A soft snap-crack echoed through the hall as laser pulses sheared off thumb-sized fragments from the training rocks.

“Drop the chip in the spectrometer,” Knox continued. “Lid closed. Ready.”

There were a few fumbles. One trainee dropped his sample. Another held the laser backward. Knox ignored it.

Feld stepped up. “A mass spectroscope requires hard vacuum. Out there, you will have it. That’s why these little bottle-sized devices work. Simple time-of-flight measurements. When you put your sample on the sample plate, hit it with the laser. Full strength, but millisecond duration. You will vaporize a point. The spectrometer reads the cloud and tells us what minerals it sees. The mass specs you have here are dummies, since we have no vacuum.”

Knox was walking between rows, observing.

Feld continued. “You’ll get a green or red ping. Green means it read. The ship relays that data to Ceres. Someone there will make the call or send the data to Earth. “Sometimes it’ll take longer. Especially early on. Engineering will model the sample against the recovery cost. That’s when we decide whether to move the rock. As we gain familiarity, both on Ceres and on Earth, the response times will improve.”

Silence. The class looked down at their equipment. The task was simple.

Feld continued. “Once the ore gets back to Ceres, the samples go through another round of tests. The planetside mass spectrometer is much more informative. We also run other assays, chemical, density, hardness, magnetic, the works.”

Feld folded his arms.

Knox let them fumble for the first ten minutes, answering questions, demonstrating technique. But then he started tapping them on the shoulder, directing them to go talk to Catherine. At the end of the exercise, there were eight fewer candidates.

Feld had worked out the doctrine of asteroid mining. Lunar mining taught them how to deal with low gravity and fine regolith, but asteroids were different beasts, tumbling, porous, occasionally hollow. Tools had to change. Cutters needed adaptive bracing systems. Drills required multi-point stabilization. Every rig needed to lock itself to rock or drift uselessly into space. Explosives were out. Precision pulsed cutters and hydraulic crackers were in. The crew practiced with each machine. “99% of this is simple, routine operations,” he stressed. “But if you can’t do the other 1%, you won’t last.”

“When you’re working the dig, you’ll load ore into these,” he pointed at a ten-ton polymer bag. “Cinch it closed. Haul it back to the extractor ship. On Earth, it’s too heavy to move, but on an asteroid, it will be light. At the ship, start loading up one of the tumblers,” he gestured to truncated steel cones that looked like paper cups scaled up to handle hundreds of tons. “Two tumblers screw together at the wide end, containing the load. Then the extractor’s mass driver launches the pair back to Ceres. You’re not firing it at Ceres, you’re giving it a shove. From there it coasts, maybe for weeks.” The projector behind him showed a simulation of the tumbler pair arcing away from an extractor in slow motion, curling toward a pale gray speck labeled CERES. 

A hand went up. Young guy. Tattoo on his neck. “What if it misses?”

Knox didn’t blink. “They will always miss, but not by much. Ceres will send up tenders to make the catch. But if you are mining, don’t worry. The extractor will do all the calculations and launch the load itself. You just fill bags and pack them in the tumblers.”

Dahl watched from the side, clipboard in hand. She watched how Knox was doing the presentation. He had a way of making bad news sound like instructions. Every night, she got the staff together to go over the day, making sure that the next time, when Knox was gone, they would be able to train new recruits.

The candidates drilled hard nonstop. They got in and out of mock-up extractor ships in suits. They trained with survey lasers, drills, cutters, loaders, and everything they’d use in the Belt. Every move was deliberate. Just like it would be in space.

Two months in, the program switched up. Knox brought them to a mockup of the Ceres receiving station. There, they found tumbler pairs filled with ore in a designated impact zone, transport sleds, and ore processing, smelting, and casting equipment.

“Ceres has low gravity. That helps. Tumblers land softly. We haul them to the plant for comminution. First pass is a jaw crusher, then a burr grinder. It turns rock into coarse sand.”

Someone in the back asked, “Why not just skip that and smelt it whole?”

“You burn energy doing that,” Knox said. “Smelt garbage, waste heat. That reduces throughput. Beneficiation saves time and energy. Basic gravity separation with vibration tables. Light stuff floats up: Those are the tails. We keep the heads, the dense fraction. That’s the value.”

“What happens to the light stuff?”

“We use some of it to make the ablative shields for the metal we are sending to Earth. Most of it will go to tailings piles.”

He turned back to the group.

“Today’s smelting practice isn’t a game,” Knox said. “You’ll haul tumblers on sleds, crush ore, sort by density, then smelt. Each step can kill you if you’re careless.”

His tone shifted.

“Molten metal runs at thousands of degrees. One slip, and you’re cooked. Respect the process. No shortcuts.”

The crusher was loud. The team was focused. Two hours in, someone screamed.

A trainee had leaned into the burr grinder to clear a jam. Just a bit of rock caught on the lip. He reached under the guard plate. The machine caught the fabric of his shirt, then the arm, then everything else. It was fast, just seconds. No one could have stopped it.

Knox stood beside the blood-slicked housing. He didn’t look away.

“It’s not fair. It’s not just. Any one of us could have made that mistake. He should’ve gotten a second chance. None of you will,” he said. “Every machine out there is stronger than you, and it doesn’t care. Respect it, or it kills you.”

Feld threw up. He stayed in his room the rest of the day.

Catherine got a few people on her crew to clean up the grinder. The drills resumed an hour later in silence. Seven more men were heading to town.

At the end of the day, Knox lifted a 100-pound casting from the mold.

“Small batch here,” he said. “On Ceres, it’ll be thousand-ton ingots. Mistakes don’t just kill people. They stop production. Remember, production is why we are there. This isn’t safe work. It’s not meant to be. But done right, it’s productive. And that’s the difference between a graveyard and an operation.”

That night, Knox found Feld in the cafeteria. The fish stew was tasteless, which was a blessing. Knox sat across from him. “Bloody day.”

“Yes.”

“Mining is like that. It will be worse on Ceres. What I need to know is if you can train the next crew.”

”Surely you would train them.”

“There is a good chance, according to Catherine, that none of us will survive. But you will survive, and you understand the operation. Can you do the job?”

“I will make sure they are safe.”

“Then they will surely die on Ceres. Make sure they are alert.”

“Of course I will do both.”

“That death today probably saved ten lives on Ceres.”

“He didn’t do anything wrong.”

“No one does. There is a bus leaving tonight. Are you going to be on it?”

Training continued. They were copying Tseng’s system for delivering titanium from the Moon: thousand-ton, bowl-shaped ingots, a retrieval eye on the back. wrapped in an ablative glass mesh. 

Feld brought up a video and explained the last step. 

“Crew uses the crane to load the ingot on the launcher. Aiming, timing, and firing are automated. A small, solar-powered, ionic-thrust rocket will make fine corrections until the package is about half an astronomical unit from Earth. At that point, the guidance package separates, goes around the sun, and heads back to Ceres. The ingot continues to Earth. The flight is about eight months.”

They trained hard with Knox beside them the whole way, demonstrating, guiding, and helping them become a skilled team. After six months in Dahl’s desert camp, the crew was down to a hundred-twenty.

Catherine and Knox talked. “Fantastic job, Silas. Training went really well.”

“Thanks. Are we ready to send these guys to Tycho Crater?”

“I am ready to send the hundred you select.”

Knox didn’t like it. In the morning, a bus brought twenty men back to town, while the hundred loaded back on the sardine can. It was a lot easier with half as many people.

Their trip went from the Gobi to Florida, where they were lifted to low Earth orbit and then ferried to Tycho. 

Darrin met the incoming crew as they disembarked. “Welcome to metallurgy in a vacuum.” They all moved to their huts. Four to a hut, narrow racks against the walls. 

Darrin gave them an overview. “Earth sees this as a titanium operation. That is what we sell them. But we also make iron for Lunar infrastructure, and our most valuable product, oxygen,” he said. “We make enough for breathing, and tons more to power the Lunar Tug ships. They load up with Hydrogen in LEO. Hydrogen is light and relatively cheap to launch from Earth, but oxygen weighs much more. We dramatically reduced the cost of deliveries.”

“On Ceres. You’ll get enough oxygen to survive. Recyclers will split the carbon out of *CO₂*, so you can keep on breathing, but every time an airlock opens, you lose a little. Mistakes will happen. You’ll need to replenish. Ore buys you backup oxygen.”

The crew performed well. Drilling, transport, sorting, smelting, they followed protocol, worked efficiently, and made no excuses. Darrin and Knox were both impressed.

Darrin’s mass driver differed from the Ceres design, but the operation was the same. The crew loaded the mass drivers and waited to watch as the thousand-ton payloads were launched towards Earth, delivering metal on target in the central Pacific.

They trained on the crater walls too, rock climbing in pressure suits, cutting ore from vertical faces. The suits were uncomfortable, but they adapted.

Early in the third session, a trainee cut his leg on a fractured edge just below the knee. The calf depressurized, but no one panicked. He felt the pressure drop instantly, sealed the tear with a wrap of suit tape, and made his way back to operations for repairs. His fast action had minimized the trauma to his calf.

Darrin was watching. “These pressure suits look new. Different seals?”

Knox beamed. “Multiple seals at every joint. A cut glove doesn’t vent the suit. Just the hand. Same for knees, elbows, and shoulders. You lose the segment, not your life. Built for field failure.”

He brushed a hand over the reinforced joint. “Dahl had them designed after talking with lunar crews. Ripped suits have killed a lot of people up here.”

Darrin gave a low whistle. “I want some for my team.”

Two weeks later, they were working a steep section of crater wall. The man ahead of Lennox reached for a handhold. The rock crumbled. His cam popped free.

He dropped. Lunar gravity was weak, so it looked like slow motion, but the fall was real and could be deadly. Lennox moved without thinking. He lunged, hooking the other man with a carabiner to his own suit. They both fell off the face, but Lennox’s cam was secure. Both men slammed against the crater face, but held.

The man gasped into the comm. “Jesus. Thanks.”

Lennox didn’t answer immediately. He was breathing hard, checking the anchor points, making sure everything was secure. Finally: “Check your clips before you weight them.”

That night in Tan’s Cantina, they bought Lennox a drink. Knox watched from across the room and made a note on his slate. Lennox was solid. Quick thinking. Good instincts. He’ll make a good team leader.

After five months, Dahl called from Earth. It was time. Lunar Tug took fifty, including Knox, down to high Earth orbit, where their transport ship was waiting to take them to Ceres. 

Their ride was a General Atomics nuclear thermal rocket, stripped down with no space and no concessions to comfort. Fifty seats packed into a hull that looked more like a shipping container than a spacecraft. The mass budget was tight. 

“You guys remember the sardine can?” Knox asked. “This will be tighter and will last a full year. But no toboggan maneuvers, so relatively pleasant.”

It was still six months before the cargo would land when they started. The nuclear thermal rocket was much faster, so the trip that took the cargo twenty-one months would only take them twelve. They passed the time quietly, reading and playing cards. 

Six months in, the monitors came up. The cargo ship reached orbit around Ceres. With rapt attention, the crew watched as the cargo ship began deploying descent pods down to the rocky south pole.

From the cargo ship, cameras showed the first wave of machines being activated. Their programming unfolded without voice or oversight, executing commands written years ago.

First, the surface was insulated. Self-expanding foam burst from canisters, boiling outward in slow waves. It spread over the South Pole, curing in vacuum to a fragile, velvet skin.

“That’s too fragile. When we step on it, won’t it be crushed?” Lennox asked.

Knox smirked. “Keep watching.”

Next came the hard foam. It was black, dense, and load-bearing. It formed the structural surface: walkable, buildable, and thermally resistant. Low albedo, high emissivity. Ceres had to be shielded from heat. The south pole was rocky, but most of the dwarf planet was not. Too much warmth, and it would erupt with steam, destabilizing everything.

Smelting and forging would bring immense heat. Every watt from the reactors, every ton of molten metal poured in the foundries, would soak downward. The foam had to stop that.

Only once the insulating layers had cured to spec did the rest of the deployment begin. 

The hauler finally landed, its massive spike pointed skywards. It was built for only that one trip. Its nuclear reactor, still humming from the long burn out of Earth, was now repurposed to power the camp. It would power the foundries, the workshops, and life support. 

The barracks modules unspooled themselves, delicate and empty as abandoned eggshells. A thin layer of black foam spread over the building. The crew watched, imagining life in their new home.

“Those look too weak to live in,” Jameson said.

Massive, blocky foundries followed. They landed hard and locked themselves onto the cooling slab. These were set on an extra meter of black surface foam, with coils of ammonia for active heat removal.

Storage barns. Fuel depots. There were five extractor ships, all parked in a row. Water processors, food supplies.

They still had six months before they arrived. That was time enough to get the systems running and confirm stability. The future was coming. It would not pause for tungsten, or for tantalum, or for anyone.
