# The Astrans

# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 5: Argentina**

| Price of *³He* | $8,000,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **19 tons/year** |
| **Current Consumption** | **11 tons/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **19 months** |

Elin’s Gulfstream descended into San Fernando International Airport, just north of Buenos Aires. While the aircraft taxied into the hangar, she reviewed the inspection briefs. She stepped out with only an overnight bag and her slate.

The Ministry of Energy representative approached and extended his hand. “Dr. Elin Sarai?” he asked. “Welcome. I’m Álvaro Luis, Director of Strategic Projects. The preparations for this tokaplex have been my responsibility. Would you like to inspect our infrastructure?”

She shook his hand. “No worries. Show me what you want me to see.”

Álvaro smiled and gestured toward the car. “We’ll take the river drive. You’ll see the northern substations on the way.”

They pulled away from the airfield in silence, the black sedan’s windows sealed tight against the outside heat. Cranes loomed behind transit depots, where new transformers were being craned into place. There were Ministry placards posted on utility poles and a banner stretched across an overpass. *“LA NUEVA RED – PARA UNA ARGENTINA ELÉCTRICA.”*

He glanced at her. “You’ll find we’re ready.”

She didn’t reply.

Past the Retiro yards and the old port, they came to a hardened facility surrounded by armored fencing and a ring of security. Álvaro leaned forward. “This is Subestación Belgrano Norte. One of eight new HVDC hubs. It’s part of the megavolt redundancy mesh.”

Elin checked the converters and cabling. She had already seen the testing documentation. She had verified test data. Now she was verifying build quality. 

After passing a few more sites, Elin noticed a generator truck. “Commissioning under load?” she asked.

“Yes. We are connecting a mountain village that was previously off-grid. They are installing a step-down transformer.”

Elin and Álvaro got out and donned hard hats. The familiar scent of ozone and mineral oil told her testing was underway. She inspected the wiring, noting the runs were sharply dressed and tied down. She talked to the foreman. “They never had power?”

“Pero claro. They have a diesel generator, but no road. Mules bring the fuel.” 

Elin kept the helmet with her when they got back in the car. She gazed at the transmission towers crossing the countryside. “You’ve done the hard part. The routing, the redundancy. You built for more than ten gigawatts.”

“We built for forty. Our president is looking to the future,” Álvaro said.

Elin gave a short nod. “Let’s go see the first one.”

The car pulled back into traffic. Elin watched the transmission lines stretch south as they drove to Balneario Monte Hermoso airfield. From there, they boarded a tiltrotor for the fifty-mile flight to the tokaplex.

In the air, she could see three large black lines converging near the sea, connecting to submarine cables. Twenty minutes later, they arrived at the artificial island, Central de Fusión de Monte Hermoso. It was larger but simpler than Ilha Fusão. Álvaro led her off the landing pad to a low-rise brick building with rounded corners and large glass windows on the second floor.

Once inside, Álvaro led her to the N.O.C. door. “Ready to see the beast?”

The room’s walls were lined with screens showing actual and predicted demand, voltages, maintenance schedules, and crew assignments.

“This is the mesh,” Álvaro said, gesturing to the largest screen, which displayed a map of nested loops and arterial corridors. “We’re the first in Latin America to attempt a full megavolt HVDC overlay. Drop any node, and it automatically reroutes without cascade failure.”

Elin studied the model. Interlocking rings in the north, deep ties south to the mining zones, fresh connectors across the pampas where before there had been only thin, century-old transmission lines. Every connection lit in a low amber that pulsed as loads shifted in real time.

“You designed this quickly,” she said, with a matter-of-fact tone.

“We didn’t design it alone,” Álvaro replied, tapping the screen. “ABB handled the HVDC overlay architecture. Our team integrated them with national policy. This isn’t a local tie‑in. It’s a national backbone. If a section goes offline, every node has failover paths without loss of synchronism.”

He tapped a zone northwest of Córdoba.

“Ilha Fusão was smaller,” he said. “A hundred megawatts. Simulations were sufficient to ensure smooth, safe operations. Here, simulations aren’t enough. Every component has been pushed to its limit.”

He pointed to a secondary display. Three heavy black lines radiated from the tokaplex, each connecting to a different node of the mesh. She recognized them from the flight in.

“These are the HVDC links. Fully isolated, with separate substations and protection. If one fails, the others carry the load.”

Elin looked at the links. “Meets the requirements. The real question is what happens when it goes live.” She looked again at the projected grid, breathing, humming, ready to distribute enormous amounts of energy. With Ilha Fusão, she had been there every day for five years before it went live. For Monte Hermoso, all she got was some reports and less than a week for inspections. The stakes were much higher, the risks were much greater, and she had no time to work with the installation.

They didn’t linger at the control center. Álvaro had a schedule, and the schedule moved fast. He brought her to a conference room down the hall. Elin checked her itinerary, and this meeting wasn’t on it. 

Elin had designed the situation room for engineers, with hard lines instead of wireless and integrated displays. Today, it held several ministers, envoys, and one uniformed military observer who said nothing and made no introductions. Elin took her seat near the far end. A plate with chipas and facturas and a glass of sparkling water sat at each place. She broke a chipa open, releasing the steam and the aroma of toasted Reggianito cheese before popping it in her mouth.

The Minister of Energy opened the session briskly. “We’re in final integration for Monte Hermoso,” he said, tapping a projection to his right. “The island is on schedule. The mesh is ready. We would like to discuss future commitments.”

A secondary map appeared with three red outlines across the country, one near Buenos Aires, one near Mar del Plata, and one further south near Comodoro Rivadavia, each marked *SOLICITADO*, all of them with planned tie-ins to the mesh.

“These are the follow-ons,” he said. “This will support development of transportation, industry, and mining operations around the country and balance our mesh.”

Elin didn’t answer at first. Looked around the room. Not a single engineer. She read the names, the grid ties, the siting logic. “You’ve assumed Helion Dynamics will be able to schedule these deployments,” she said finally. “Who confirmed those deployments?”

The Trade Minister answered. “We were your first paying customer, and we have paid in full for Monte Hermoso. We expect priority.”

Elin nodded slowly. “I’ll relay that, but it’s not my contract to make. You were first, but there have been many orders since, all fully paid.”

Another official, this one from the Ministry of Industry, leaned in. “Aluminum and lithium plants have been built already. The demand is high, and they can employ several thousand, but they need power.”

“We know you have Raines’ ear,” said the Energy Minister. “And we know you understand what this kind of energy makes possible. We are not just talking about supply. We are talking about national transformation.” He slid an envelope to Elin.

Elin placed her hand flat on the envelope, stopping it before it reached her. Blushing hotly, she looked at each person in the room.

“I am in manufacturing. I built this place, even this room. Raines decides where they go.”

Elin’s eyes moved from minister to minister. “If you need the power to arrive sooner, build the other islands quickly and connect them to the mesh. You know the requirements. Then talk to Raines.”

After the meeting, Álvaro found Elin. “The tiltrotor is ready, Dr. Sarai. We’ve reserved a room in town and dinner is arranged.”

Elin shook her head. “I’ll stay on the island tonight.”

Álvaro paused. “The apartments are very basic.”

“I know. I designed them.”

He smiled slightly. “The vending machines may not satisfy you. My wife packed a dinner for me in case things went long. It is in the refrigerator. It’s yours if you want it.”

“Thank you, Álvaro. That sounds perfect. I would just like some time alone now.”

“*Buenas noches*, Dr. Sarai. I will see you in the morning.”

She found the milanesa a la napolitana con puré in the refrigerator and warmed it. The solitude was perfect, just her and the quiet machine. After dinner, she walked the corridors alone, listening, connecting. The tokaplex hummed softly on standby: cooling systems, auxiliary pumps, metal contracting in the evening air.

The apartments were simple with just a cot, a sink, and a shared restroom at the end of the hall. Elin had designed them herself, years ago, for overnight crews. She’d never expected to use one. But staying close to the machine was grounding. This is why she was here. She slept well.

The next morning, Álvaro brought her medialunas from home. Elin thanked him as she ate. 

Her first meeting that morning was with the general manager, Gabriel Mansilla. She found him windblown, looking across the floor.

“Elin,” he said, nodding once.

She skipped the pleasantries. “How’s the floor?”

“Cleared, cleaned, and ready,” he said. “We pulled the last crates two nights ago. Gantries are programmed. Power couplings are aligned and tested.”

She glanced down at her notes. “Turbines?”

“Hot,” he confirmed. “Running on simulation heat. We’re venting most of it, but efficiency is higher than spec. The *sCO₂* units stabilize faster than we expected.”

“Any anomalies?”

He hesitated. “Minor fouling on line B. There was some grit in the condensing loop, probably from the cleaning process. Flushed and rebalanced. They are running it again.”

She made a mark on her slate. “And the core?”

“A few hours out. I expect it around noon, plus or minus.”

“Assembly team is ready?”

“Staggered shifts. They’re tuning the robotic frames and walking through dry runs. They’re ready.”

“And you?”

Gabriel gave a tired half-smile. “I’ve got three hours of sleep and a cooling jacket strapped to my lower back. So yes. Ready.”

Her eyes laughed, but her voice stayed steady. “Stay on comms. Notify me when the core clears port.”

Elin headed to the tiltrotor pad. Gonçalo Silva was coming. The tiltrotor dropped onto the eastern pad. Elin was already there when the rotors began to slow. Gonçalo stepped out with his usual pace, unhurried, steady. He wore the same field jacket he had worn in Ilha Fusão, light, practical, unbranded. His bag was slung crosswise, heavy with tools and notebooks. Before he made his way to her, he paused to take in the east gantry, then the core floor scaffolding. She let him walk the first few meters alone.

When they met, they didn’t shake hands. She tilted her head slightly. He smiled.

“Looks smooth,” he said.

“More streamlined than Ilha Fusão,” Elin replied. “We learned from all the mistakes we made there.”

Gonçalo was looking at the skyline. “I can see that. It’s much larger, but it feels less cluttered.”

They started walking toward the operations mezzanine. Between them, the control tower loomed in prefabricated concrete and fiber panels, with broad window slits set under sun-shielding eaves.

They climbed a short flight of metal steps that led up to the observation level. Below them, the control room staff ran diagnostics in near silence, each screen tracing voltages and heat gradients across systems not yet powered. Gabriel stood with one of the interface engineers, looking up at a wall display of turbine feedback loops.

Gonçalo gestured at the far end of the floor. “Gabriel’s getting there. He’s detail-forward, but he’ll learn to step back. You can’t fly a thing this size like you’re tuning it by hand.”

“He respects the systems,” Elin said. “He doesn’t try to outthink them.”

“Is the emergency cooling loop really that simple?” he asked.

Elin brought up a diagram on her slate. “The core is below sea level now. If containment fails or thermal load runs away, gravity pulls seawater straight through. No pumps, no heat exchangers. Just open the valves. Four separate valve sets. The computer controls the automated valve set. If it detects a thermal runaway, it opens the valve.”

“That’s nice if everything works perfectly. What if the computer shuts down or the sensors fail?”

“If the computer fails for any reason, there are two manual valves, accessible from different locations. And even if no one notices, there is one final failsafe, a thermal valve with a fusible plug. If the temperature gets too high, the solder will melt, opening the valve.”

He leaned on the railing. “That’s the difference, isn’t it. Ilha Fusão is safe, but it needs babysitting. This one’s safer because it doesn’t.”

Elin and Gonçalo were eating lunch when the core arrived. Elin stayed in the cafeteria, but Gonçalo got up to watch Mansilla’s crew install it.

The gantry cranes lowered it into position, where a team of a dozen technicians immediately swarmed the core, connecting the feed pipes, monitors, and cooling loops. 

After installation was complete, Elin found Gonçalo. “How did they do?” 

“Where did you get these guys, a Formula One team? Mansilla has trained them very well. Installation went smoothly and much faster than I expected.”

“At Ilha Fusão, we didn’t design for installation. This time around, ease of assembly was a key constraint. It’s supposed to be smooth.”

With the core secured, the site shifted tempo. The Argentine crews moved quickly, hands practiced, expressions focused. The last hookups were made and checked. 

Heavy conduit arms were swung into place and locked down. The s*CO₂* turbine, already hot-tested, was mated to the flow channel. A hiss and pressure check marked the moment when the *CO₂* storage tank came online, dumping its contents into the s*CO₂* turbine.

At the perimeter of the island, a low-power carbon collector clicked back to life, drawing in atmospheric *CO₂* to replenish the storage tank. It would refill the tank in about a month. In theory, they would not need more *CO₂*, but if there were a leak developed, they would need to be fixed and completely recharged.

Power lines were checked, then rechecked. The HVDC terminals blinked status green. Routing software loaded. Backup systems synced. The mesh recognized the plant’s endpoints. For the first time, the island registered as a node in Argentina’s electrical system. 

Assembly was declared complete at 14:42 local time. Just a line item in the log, signed by the work crew chief, seconded by Gabriel.

Gabriel found Elin near the mezzanine, hands in his jacket pockets, boots dusty from the floor. “Dr. Sarai, *ya está listo*.”

Elin notified her contact, a US Navy lieutenant on the amphibious assault ship offshore. It had brought the container down from Dutch Harbor with a destroyer escort around the horn. The US government had asked to secure the delivery due to the extraordinary value. The *³He* in the container was enough to power this plant for a year. It had been scraped from the Moon at enormous cost in labor, equipment, lives, and eight billion dollars. 

The cryotank came in low, under fighter escort. Two F-35s bracketed the CH-53E as it descended on its pad. Propwash kept everyone at a distance.

From the operations platform, Elin watched as the rear ramp unfolded. Marines deployed first, fanning out with unhurried precision. Then the cargo came down, matte silver, heavily insulated, marked with stenciled black codes, and frost blooming around the seals. The cryotank held a year’s supply of *³He*, one metric ton. 

A Marine captain held his salute until Elin acknowledged him. He passed over the digital manifest. Elin inspected it, then signed.

Installation was already underway. The hauler maneuvered the container to the sealed platform near the reactor housing. Hydraulic arms lifted it into position. The cradle clicked into its locks. Engineers verified coolant flow and containment pressure. Readouts held steady.

The Marine escort didn’t linger. Before departing, their commander turned to Gabriel and saluted sharply. Gabriel crisply returned the salute. Then the Marines were back aboard the helicopter and into the air.

She walked with Gabriel to the control floor for the test run. The control room was already full. Gabriel was at the operator’s station, Gonçalo standing behind him, arms crossed, leaning slightly forward. Elin watched from one of the side consoles, monitoring the turbine feedback loops.

On screen, the core came to life in steps. Systems primed. Vacuum held. Field coils charged. Then the plasma ignited briefly, controlled, a soft event visually, just a line of green across the diagnostic pane. Not even enough to raise the turbine feed pressure, but the superconducting ring registered the field. 

Gonçalo leaned in and called for another pulse, this one longer. Seconds, not milliseconds. Heat output rose. The turbine responded, barely. A ripple in the pressure. Cooling held steady. The s*CO₂* cycle hadn’t fully ramped, but it was alive. The storage buffers reported a charge. Grid output was still zero, as expected.

They ran five more bursts. In each, the response was tighter. The plasma shape held. The pressure gradients behaved. Thermal gradients stabilized faster than the simulations had suggested. The reactor wasn’t just stable, it was predictable and controllable.

Gabriel turned and gave Elin a nod. “This thing listens.”

She checked the timestamp. The whole break-in run had lasted under twenty minutes. Long enough to show continuity across fusion, routing, recovery, and control systems, but not enough to prove efficiency. The turbine had barely started to turn. Just a machine confirming it was what they had built.

Gonçalo stepped back from the console. “The next run will be for power, Gabriel.”

The control room was silent in the seconds before ignition. Gabriel keyed the final command. The reactor ramped up in a controlled ascent. Plasma formed, and ignition held. The display confirmed a stable burn in under three seconds. Turbine readouts spiked. Heat flooded the s*CO₂* loop, and pressure hit threshold. Grid output climbed, six, eight, ten gigawatts. Steady. Smooth.

For a moment, it all held.

Then one of the HVDC lines dropped. Just a blink and the connection was lost. Power continued to route smoothly as the mesh’s reroute protocols kicked in. Load spilled to the southern loop and northern loops, then back across the mesh. Power rebalanced.

Gabriel swore under his breath. Gonçalo stared at the numbers, expression flat.

Elin leaned forward and logged the fault. The mesh had responded exactly as designed. But the line had failed under load, and that meant something had slipped.

She didn’t say anything yet.

Instead, they left the control center and followed the long service corridor toward the power conditioning chamber. This was the part no one showed on press tours. The CL filters filled the space like machinery in a cathedral, vast, industrial, sculptural. The capacitors stood fifteen feet tall, six feet across, ribbed in black insulation. Inductors coiled behind caged housings, humming faintly. Each unit shaped and stabilized the rectified current, converting raw internal power into clean HVDC output.

Gonçalo paused in the aisle between them. He thought of Ilha Fusão, where the output had been AC, conditioned by standard hardware. It had been elegant, minimal. Nothing like this. He felt like a mouse in the land of giants.

He turned to Elin. “Isto é outra escala.”

She turned to him. “Another kind of responsibility.”

They continued walking, passing into the thyristor hall, where the problem had occurred. Too much feedback on the western line had caused multiple commutation failures as it tried to ramp up. The valves were blocked to prevent them from melting. Gabriel restarted the process, letting the western line climb slowly. A thousand volts, ten thousand, a hundred thousand. Gabriel touched one more button on his slate, and full power was restored. There was no friction to the power now. It moved outwards with a strange ease.

Back in the control room, the grid displays showed full delivery restored. Argentina was receiving power. Gabriel gave his diagnosis. “It was a harmonic echo. The western line’s fundamental resonance matched our switching frequency. The circuit couldn’t provide sufficient reverse-bias duration to allow the thyristors to shut off between pulses. From now on, we ramp the western line on a staggered curve to stay below that resonant peak.” 

Gonçalo beamed. He didn’t understand DC power nearly as well as Gabriel, but it was clear the plant was in good hands. 

They had 58.3% thermal conversion efficiency. Elin made a note. 

The system had worked, and so had the failure. The recovery system had proven itself at full power. 

The tiltrotor lifted in a smooth vertical climb, skimming above the breakers before banking east. Through the cabin window, Elin watched the artificial island shrink into geometry, cranes stilled, intake arms folded, the blunt white dome of the reactor glinting in sea haze. A line of pylons faded into the mainland.

She turned back to the slate in her lap. Uptime has held steady since the full ignition. Grid telemetry showed stable dispatch at ten-point-four gigawatts, even with the reroute. Cooling margins were conservative. Substation frequency held. Forecasted demand peaks from the southern industrial corridor had already started to adjust upward.

There were six more contracts in early-phase development. Different soils. Different governments. Different tolerances for delay.

She tapped through Gabriel’s final site report. Nothing unexpected. Turbine efficiency was holding.

The plant would run. But more importantly, it could be repeated.

She closed the report and looked back out the window, but the island was already gone. Just sea, now, layered and flat. On the horizon, faint in the morning light, the HVDC towers on the island fed the submarine cables that went to the mainland. She could see where the submarine cables joined the aerial cable towers on land. Argentina was powered. Elin had work to do.
