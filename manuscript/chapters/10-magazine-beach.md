Catherine woke to the ringing of her phone and a splitting headache.

“Hello?”

“Hello. That contract you signed last night, do you still mean it?”

She blinked at the ceiling, trying to place the voice. “What? Who is this?”

“My name is Jakob Raskin. We met last night. You said you would pay two hundred dollars per kilogram for water delivered to Ceres. You asked for one hundred tons weekly.”

She remembered the stranger she met last night. She even remembered the contract. “Sure,” she replied. “There’s no way to do it, but if you could do it, I’d be more than willing to pay that much.”

“You may be right, Catherine, maybe there is no way. But I can tell you this: I will do everything I can to get you that water.”

“If you make any progress on that, let me know, and I will get an official contract from Helion Dynamics, signed by Tobias Raines.”

After the call, Jakob opened his contacts. He didn’t need many. Just the right ones.

That evening, he boarded a flight to Boston. The next afternoon, they all met in Magazine Beach Park in Cambridge. They sat in the shade at a picnic table, the Charles River flowing slowly by.

Mira Davis was the youngest, eighteen, still in her freshman year at MIT. She’d shown up in work boots and a tank top with grease under her fingernails.

Tom Wilson had come straight from his shift at Eversource, still wearing the utility vest with the company logo. He had his slate out, already working on something.

Ellie Thompson sat with her arms crossed, radiating impatience. Her degree was in Ocean Engineering. She had worked as an undersea medic and moved into habitat design. Sharp edges, the kind of person who’d seen too much to be impressed by mystery.

Pachakuti Quispe was the only one who looked relaxed, leaning back against the table, watching the river.

“Thanks for coming,” Jakob said.

“You didn’t say what this was about,” Ellie said. “Just that it was a huge opportunity.”

Jakob pulled a napkin from his jacket and spread it on the table. Wine-stained, wrinkled, covered in hasty handwriting.

*One hundred tons. Pure. FOB destination: Ceres. Weekly.*  
*Two hundred dollars per kilogram. Paid on arrival.*  
*Catherine Dahl, Helion Dynamics.*

No one spoke for a moment.

“That’s…” Mira leaned forward, squinting. “That’s twenty million dollars. Per week.”

“It’s real,” Jakob said. “I met her in Paris two nights ago. She’s desperate. Ceres is dying. Two of their water processors failed. They need water, or everyone up there dies.”

Tom looked up from his slate. “Where do you get water?”

“Mars,” Jakob said.

Ellie laughed. Short, sharp. “You’re joking.”

“Mars has ice,” Jakob said. “Polar caps, subsurface deposits. We mine it, purify it, and launch it to Ceres. They pay on delivery.”

“We,” Pacha said quietly. “Meaning us.”

“Meaning the five of us here at Magazine Beach. Whoever’s willing to go, anyway.”

Silence. The Charles River flowed past, indifferent.

“No one’s on Mars,” Ellie injected.

“Not yet.”

“There’s no infrastructure. No base. No support.”

“Can we build it?” Jakob asked.

Ellie burst out, “This is insane\! There is no way we can do this.”

“It’s twenty million dollars a week,” Mira said softly.

Tom was staring at his slate, fingers moving across it. He’d gone quiet.

“Even if we could survive,” Pacha said, “how do we get the water there? Mars to Ceres isn’t simple.”

“No,” Jakob conceded. “It’s anything but simple. If it were simple, they wouldn’t be offering eighty million dollars a month. I don’t have answers. I think, though, that you four might. Not right away, but if you think about it, discuss it, I think you can work something out.”

“We’d need some equipment,” Tom stated. “We would need a mass driver to launch the ice blocks to Ceres. Similar to the system the Moon is using to deliver titanium to Earth, but much stronger. It will take time to spec that out. Getting out of the Martian gravity well all the way up to Ceres won’t be easy.”

“What will we eat?” Pacha asked. 

“I was hoping you would tell me. Do you think we will starve?” Jakob asked.

Pacha hesitated. “Amaranth, oca, cavies. High altitude crops, low pressure tolerance. It’s possible. But I’d need a closed system. Water recovery, waste recycling, full nutrient loop.”

“We’d have sewage,” Mira said.

“That’s the point,” Pacha said. “Everything recycles. No waste. Nothing is replaceable. Everything has to be reused indefinitely.”

Ellie’s mind was reeling with the dangers. “If the habitat breaches, we die; if the power fails, we die; if the crops fail, we die. This is a long list of easy ways to die.”

“What would you need?” Jakob asked. “For the habitat.”

Ellie exhaled slowly. “3D printer. Regolith processor. Plenty of polymer. Get life support components from lunar suppliers. Those are proven. Air recyclers, heaters, air locks. It’s doable. I’ve done pieces of it before.”

“Power?” Jakob asked.

Tom was quiet, still looking at his slate.

“Tom?” Jakob said.

“Mars gets about forty percent of Earth’s sunlight,” Tom said distantly. “We’d need a lot of area. And dust is a major problem. Wiring, batteries, redundancy.” He paused, looking at his slate. “Kicker is, launch window opens in six weeks.”

Everyone turned to look at him.

“What?” Mira said.

“Earth to Mars transfer window,” Tom said. He looked up, and his expression was strange. Not excited. Not afraid. Just… certain. He turned his slate to show them the chart. “Favorable alignment. If we don’t launch in six weeks, the next good window isn’t for two years.”

The weight of that settled over the table.

“Six weeks,” Ellie said. “To build a Mars base.”

Pacha was watching the Charles. “If we go, we’re not coming back. Not for years. Maybe never.”

“I know,” Jakob said.

Mira leaned forward. “How much do we get? If we do this.”

Jakob smiled slightly. “That depends on how long we survive. But if we run for six months, that is twenty-six deliveries.” He let them do the math.

“A hundred million dollars,” Mira said quietly. “Each.”

No one spoke.

Tom was still staring at his slate, but his hands had stopped moving.

Pacha broke the silence. “What do you need from us?”

“A plan,” Jakob said. “Enough that I can pitch an investor. Can we actually do this? Can we survive? Can we eat? Can we save Ceres? I need a loose plan by tomorrow morning. “

Ellie was skeptical. “We’d need to test everything,” she said. “Habitat, life support, food production. We can’t just guess.”

“We have six weeks,” Jakob said.

“That’s not enough time.”

“No, but it’s the time we have.”

Ellie looked at the others. Mira was leaning forward, eyes bright. Tom was calculating something, lost in numbers. Pacha was unreadable.

“I need to know you’re serious,” Jakob said. “If I get the money, if I get the launch, are you in?”

Ellie pressed Jakob. “How much money are you asking for?”

“Money for equipment. I am hoping you guys can figure that part out. Plus the launch.”

“How much is the launch?”

“Half a billion. Maybe more.”

Ellie whistled. “Who’s paying that?”

“I’m working on it. I have a lead.”

“You don’t have the money yet.”

“Not yet.”

She stared at him. Then at the napkin. Then back at him.

“If you get it,” she said slowly, “I’ll go.”

Mira nodded immediately. “I’m in.”

Pacha studied Jakob for a long moment. “You’ll need a better plan than this.”

“I know.”

“But yes. If you can make it real, I’ll go.”

They all looked at Tom. He was still staring at his slate, silent.

“Tom?” Jakob said.

Tom looked up. His face was calm, but his hands were shaking slightly.

“Six weeks,” he said. “We have six weeks.”

“Is that a yes?”

Tom closed his slate. “Get the money. I’ll make the plan work.”

Jakob looked around the table at this group. Four people who barely knew each other. Four people who were about to save Ceres.

“Alright,” he said. “Let’s figure out what we’re actually building. I need to leave this in your hands. I have a flight to catch.”

The group continued their discussions without Jakob. He headed back to Logan International to catch a flight to Lagos, Nigeria. He had an appointment with a man he had only ever heard of, never met. A man who might listen.

Max Juno woke with gravity in his bones and smog in his lungs. It was his seventh month back on Earth. It still felt wrong.

The Moon had been sterile, hostile, and dangerous. He missed it every day.

He’d been a *³He* miner who got a polar claim in perpetual darkness. He had set up his solar panels on a ridge, giving him nearly perpetual light. It was incredibly productive. After two years of mining, he leased out the claim, returned to Earth, and continued to collect royalties. Now he paced through his penthouse suite in Lagos, irritated by the weight of the air.

The meeting was at a half-finished skyscraper downtown. He could smell wet concrete when he got out of the cab. He didn’t need a palace. Just a clean room and a screen. This room would be adequate, and the carpet was plush. 

Jakob was already waiting when Max entered.

“You’re the lunar exit, right?” Jakob asked.

“And you’re the Mars guy,” Max said. “Which is funny, because there’s no one on Mars.”

“Yet.” Jakob gestured, and a screen flickered on. No graphs, no pitch deck. Just a satellite photo: a pale streak of ice, buried in red Martian dust.

“Ice?” Max asked.

“Mars has plenty. Poles, craters, and pockets under the regolith. The volatiles are clean. All I need is a shovel and a mass driver.”

Max narrowed his eyes. “You plan to sell it?”

“Two of the Ceres water processors failed. They’re begging Earth to fill the gap, but Earth can’t help. Launch window’s years out, and even then, it’s a long ride. They’ll be dry before anything gets close. I can send water for a fraction of the cost. And Mars has a launch window early next month.” Jakob pulled a folded napkin from his jacket. “This is the offer.”

Max skimmed it. The money was incredible, but so are the expenses in space. He looked at the signature, scrawled crudely at the bottom.

“Is that the Catherine Dahl from Helion Dynamics?”

“The same. I met her in a bar in Paris.”

Max rocked back. He started to believe the opportunity was real. He also doubted this scrawl would count as a legitimate contract when it came time to collect. 

“Obviously, I will need to talk to her about this.”

“Of course. Of course you will. I have her number; you can call her right now.”

“For now, I will listen. If you have something to say, maybe I will call her.”

Jakob was nervous. “I have a crew from MIT. I met them all at school and alumni events. Our plan is to go to Mars, set up a stable living environment, and send large blocks of ice by mass driver to Ceres.”

“How are you going to hit Ceres at that distance?”

“No way. That isn’t our goal. Ceres has a small gravity well, and there are lots of other asteroids to mess with it. But they are already collecting ore. They have tenders, small ships that work in and around Ceres, catching payloads and guiding them in. We just have to get within tender range.”

Max had lived on the Moon. He knew how hostile Mars would be without Lunar Tug to bring them supplies. ”What about food?”

“We’ll grow it. Algae’s simple. But one member of our team is planning to grow high-elevation crops, since they can grow at lower pressures. He has a plan to make regolith into dirt.”

“You’re serious.”

“I just have to feed five people. Nothing fancy.”

Max studied him. Gaunt. Sharp. A little unhinged. Definitely broke.

“What’s your name?”

“Jakob Raskin.”

“What do you want?”

“Maybe thirty million for equipment. Plus a ride to Mars. With that, I pay the team and build what we need. You arrange the ride. In return, you get forty percent of the company. First Martian exporter. First to deliver offworld water.”

Max looked at the photo again. Ice, red dust.

“And if it doesn’t work?”

Jakob shrugged. “Then we die on Mars.”

Max frowned. “You say that like it’s obvious.”

“It is,” Jakob said. “This isn’t the Moon. Resupply is nearly impossible, and there’s no lift capacity for a return stage. No launch window for two years, even if you could afford to send a rescue ship. We go, we live off what we brought and what we make. If that fails, we die. The crew knows that, and they are working right now to ensure we don’t die.”

Max sat back. He knew the math. The Moon had always offered at least the illusion of rescue. Mars didn’t. If they failed, they wouldn’t be coming back. 

Jakob watched him. “We have to make it work.”

Silence. The kind of silence you only get when someone actually means it.

Max thought, remembering his days on the Moon, knowing even if he did everything right, a stray micrometeoroid could end him at any time. Risky, but exhilarating. This project filled him with the same exhilaration. So many ways to fail, but there was a way to succeed, too.

“Jakob, let’s see what we can do. I’ll fund your shopping spree. You guys get started now. I have some other phone calls to make, starting with Catherine Dahl. We will see about getting you your ride. Maybe Lunar Tug has something. In the meantime, what can I call your company?”

Jakob looked at Max, caught completely off guard. He had no answer. Then it came to him. “Magazine Beach.”
