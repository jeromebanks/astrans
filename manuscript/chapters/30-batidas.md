The design came together quietly, over late evenings and sketches on slates passed around the table. There was no formal announcement, just the slow coalescence of agreement between engineers who trusted one another’s instincts. Daily there were seminars, small talk given by each researcher where they described their works in progress. 

Rithika Patel stood confidently at the podium, surveying the audience of her peers. She cleared her throat and began her talk.

“Good morning, everyone. Today, I’d like to present our design for a helium separation system.”

She clicked a button, and a diagram of the system appeared on the screen behind her.

“Our design uses a combination of compressors, membrane separators, and cryocoolers to achieve high-purity helium. The process starts with the ingestion of atmosphere at a steady rate, optimized for the density band around 2 atmospheres of pressure.”

Rithika gestured to the diagram. “The hydrogen-helium mix is ingested by a large fan which blows the air across a zeolite membrane shield. The membrane passes only the hydrogen and helium.”

A colleague in the audience raised his hand. “Rithika, can you explain the choice of membrane material for this shield?”

Rithika nodded. “The shield must block ammonia, sulfur compounds, and phosphines which would cause downstream problems. We are modeling this using a thin silicalite-1 zeolite with high porosity to minimize the pressure drop across the membrane. The relatively fragile zeolite requires strong backing to support it, which we provide with a glass honeycomb.”

She clicked the button again, and a graph appeared on the screen.

“Once ingested, the gas is compressed in an axial flow turbine. The ambient pressure is two bar. We compress it to nearly three bar. Compression also heats the gas. Here it passes through a cone twenty meters long, ten meters in diameter. The cone is lined with a five-micron foil of a palladium-silver alloy over a high-strength inconel mesh. Hydrogen passes through this foil readily. By the end of the cone, the gas mixture contains less than one percent hydrogen.”

“That is an astonishing level of extraction.”

“Yes, palladium grabs the hydrogen quickly. Silver helps the hydrogen pass through the foil quickly and, of course, the high temperature helps.”

Rithika continued. “The hot hydrogen is passed back through a countercurrent flow with the incoming gas, heating up that gas before compression. The retentate is still hot. An intercooler brings it down to ambient temperature, 165K, then it gets refrigerated to about six Kelvin in a cryogenic recuperator. At that point, the helium passes through a nozzle, where a Joule-Thomson expansion drops the pressure and rapidly cools the gas for the final isotopic separation.”

“How cold does it come out?”

She clicked the button again, and a diagram of the fractional distillation tower appeared.

“A little less than five Kelvin. Ready to enter the fractional distillation column. The column is packed with aerogel, and the top is kept at 3.7K. The *⁴He* rains out and goes back to the cryogenic recuperator. The final gas phase is high-purity *³He.*”

Rithika concluded her talk. “Our combined design isolates high-purity *³He* efficiently. We’ve run the numbers, and we believe we can achieve a theoretical yield of approximately ten tons per day per engine, using about a hundred gigajoules per kilogram.”

The audience applauded, and afterwards, several colleagues approached her to ask questions and offer feedback. 

Nikola Savic, a new grad student in orbital mechanics, was dazzled. He came up to talk to her. “Your system is the most efficient system I’ve seen. Off the record, do you have any idea how to get the *³He* off the dirigible?”

“As far as I know, that stage is theoretically impossible. Rockets can’t lift off from Jupiter’s atmosphere. I try not to think about it,” she said with a wink.

Savic’s mind was spinning on the walk home. Real extraction, as much as you please, as long as you want. There just had to be a way to get the *³He* off. When he burst through the door, his roommate, Marco, looked up from his tablet, eyebrows raised at Savic’s unusual energy.

“You should have been there,” Savic said, dropping his bag and heading straight for the kitchen. “Rithika just solved helium extraction. Ten tons per day from a single engine. It’s brilliant. They use membrane separation, fractional distillation, the whole thing integrated into a dirigible that can actually stay aloft in Jupiter’s atmosphere.”

Marco nodded politely. He was an atmospheric chemist, comfortable with theory but not particularly interested in engineering details.

“The problem,” Savic continued, grabbing a drink from the fridge, “is that it’s completely useless.”

“Useless?”

“All that beautiful engineering, and the helium just sits there in the dirigible. There’s no way to get it off Jupiter. The gravity well is too deep.”

Savic slumped into the chair across from Marco, the excitement draining from his face. “She solved the hard part. And it doesn’t matter at all because no one can solve the impossible part.”

Marco looked at him for a moment. “You’re an orbital mechanic, right? That’s what you do. You figure out how to move things through space.”

“This isn’t moving things through space. This is escaping from Jupiter. Do you know what the escape velocity is? Sixty kilometers per second. That’s not engineering, that’s physics saying no.”

“So figure it out,” Marco said, returning to his tablet. “That’s literally your job.”

The challenge hung in the air. Savic stared at the ceiling, then pulled out his slate and started sketching orbital diagrams.

For the next week, he attacked the problem methodically. The tyranny of the rocket equation made chemical rockets hopeless. Planes were better. Electric ramjets. They could lift the helium high in the atmosphere, but there was no way to get it from a plane out of the Jovian gravity well.

Mass drivers came next. Electromagnetic launchers were used on Ceres, the Moon, and Mars. They were very efficient, both in energy and mass. No propellant, just electrical energy converted to kinetic energy through magnetic fields. A theoretical best case.

Savic spent three days modeling a mass driver capable of launching a ten-ton bottle of *³He* from the Jovian atmosphere to escape velocity. The numbers were staggering. The launcher would need to be hundreds of miles long to achieve the necessary acceleration without crushing a steel bottle. The power requirements were even worse. To launch one kilogram of *³He* would require the entire output of a large fission reactor. 

He calculated the infrastructure required: massive superconducting coils, cryogenic cooling systems, power distribution networks that could handle gigawatt pulses. The platform would need to be the size of a small city just to house the equipment. That wouldn’t fit on Rithika’s elegant dirigible. 

Savic pushed back from his desk in frustration. The mass driver was clearly impractical, but maybe it would be theoretically possible if Rithika’s design didn’t cruise at two atmospheres. That left an impossible amount of atmosphere above the rig. The drag crushed all hope. It would vaporize the bottle.

He had another idea, imagining a very light tethered balloon rising high with just this one cargo, and launching from far above all the clouds. But it made too small of a change. 

Like everything else he’d tried, it ran up against the fundamental problem: Jupiter’s gravity well was simply too deep for current technology to climb out of economically.

He was an orbital mechanic, supposedly. That’s what his advisor said. But even orbital mechanics had limits.

At the end of the week, Marco found Savic hunched over calculations, barely moving. “Come with me, my man. You need food. And maybe some perspective. I found a Cuban place up in Baracoa, Café Havana. They have live music tonight. My treat.”

Savic reluctantly agreed. He wasn’t getting anywhere right now. “Alright. I can’t even find a fresh way for the physics to say no. Let’s go have fun. The problem will still be here when I get back.”

“I invited you, not your slate,” Marco said, pulling the slate from Savic’s pocket and putting it face down on the desk.

They took a JRC shuttle bus to Baracoa and walked down to the cafe. The evening was warm, and they grabbed a table on the patio where a guitarist was setting up in the corner. The familiar comfort of black beans and rice, the smell of garlic and cumin, the gentle pulse of música criolla were exactly what Savic needed.

“So,” Marco said, attacking his ropa vieja, “still trying to escape Jupiter?”

“Not tonight,” Savic said, raising his mojito. “Tonight I’m officially not thinking about orbital mechanics.”

The guitarist began playing a slow and melodic tune that mixed with the harbor breeze. Savic let himself sink into the moment and enjoy the food, the music, and the simple pleasure of not calculating mass ratios or power requirements.

Across the patio, two women were seated together, drinking batidas, a thick frozen drink. He could only see the face of one of them, but it was captivating. She was clearly enjoying the music. A young man asked her friend to dance, and the woman he was watching was alone. “I’m gonna enjoy myself tonight,” he told Marco.

Marco followed Savic’s gaze. “Out of your league, Nick. Just enjoy the music.” 

Savic stood and began walking toward her, marveling at the sparkle of her eyes, the bounce of her hair, and the way her cheeks looked when she drew in her batida through the straw. He watched absently as she worked the straw, pulling the drink from the bottom of the glass to her mouth. Such a simple mechanism. Direct connection. No intermediary steps, no complex machinery. Just suction and…

He stopped.

A straw.

He went straight back to the table and started talking to Marco. “You saw that woman drinking a batida through a straw? She isn’t really sucking it up, you know. She reduces the air pressure in her mouth, but it is the air pressure in the room that’s pushing it up through the straw. That is why her drink is flowing.”

Marco hardly knew what to do when Savic talked this way. Anything could suddenly turn into a lesson in physics, and Marco had no idea why it was so fascinating to Savic. 

“You’re thinking about work again,” Marco said.

Savic was lost in thought. He couldn’t even hear Marco. His head was filled with material profiles and energy diagrams.

When they finished eating, Marco paid the bill. On the shuttle back to Jamal, Savic’s mouth kept running. Marco was tired. He was happy to see his friend so animated, but he felt overwhelmed by the discussion. He rolled his eyes and let the words wash over him. When they got home, he went to bed. Savic opened his slate and began doing calculations.

The next morning Savic called together some other grad students who had been at Rithika’s talk and his own advisor, Dr. Miriam Vance, to talk about the idea. “I think we all know that Rithika’s dirigibles are the best way to collect *³He* from Jupiter, but getting the *³He* out of the atmosphere, much less the gravity well, is completely impossible.” Rithika nodded as his friends murmured in agreement. “No ship can fly in, pick up a bottle of *³He* and then leave. The delta V is just too much. So I spent days looking at mass drivers, hoping to send cryocontainers of *³He* out.”

“The gravity well is too deep.”

“Tell me about it. I looked at a five-hundred-mile-long mass driver to get the power down to three hundred megawatts per ton. And it still needs terajoules, even ignoring atmospheric drag. I hoped to use the magnetosphere to help, but I was really just grasping at straws. Fundamentally, it is impossible.” A few heads nodded. 

“Last night, I saw something new. A woman was drinking through a straw. And it struck me. The atmosphere already pushes itself up. With an engineered ‘straw’ the atmosphere would apply most of the force, and we would only need to apply a little extra to accelerate the *³He* and overcome friction, allowing it to rise up through the straw.”

“Do you want to make a *³He* fountain in the exoatmosphere?”

“At first I didn’t know what I wanted. An exoatmospheric fountain would have been a huge step forward. Then I remembered an old space travel concept, space elevators, which allowed you to launch heavy payloads into space without rockets.”

“Space elevators never worked.” 

“They never made practical sense because by the time we could build a cable strong enough, LEO had way too many satellites. For a time, they talked about anchoring it on a ship, and letting the ship move around to avoid satellites, but you could never dodge the satellites fast enough. It can’t work on Earth. But we are talking about Jupiter. No satellites inside the orbit of Callisto, and the planet spins really fast.”

“You realize, of course, that the gravity in the Jovian system is immense.”

“Yes, that’s true. Hence the original problem. So I did a calculation. If Rithika’s dirigible is in the equatorial stream, we can calculate based on equatorial, or System I, rotation. But there is a band above that in which the equatorial jet is five hundred kilometers per hour faster. Geostationary from there is 88,000 km. We will be well inside the Galilean moons, even with runout for a counterbalance. I am calling these split rigs, since there is a dirigible and a space station working together, separated by an 88,000 kilometer straw.”

“No material could possibly handle the stress.”

“I am not a materials guy, but it looks like carbon nanotubes might. The tube would need a strong taper, maybe twenty or thirty times as thick at Jovian stationary orbit as at the dirigible. Then it thins out again as the cable proceeds to the counterbalance.”

Hope seemed to glimmer. If they could pump the *³He* up, they could get around the impossible launch problem. The team grew cautiously excited. This was far from a solved problem, but some of the impossible parts had been converted to very difficult. 

Savic’s advisor, Dr. Vance, called a meeting with him a few days later. “Fascinating talk you gave, Nick. That sounded impromptu.”

“Yes, well, Rithika’s scheduled talk a week ago got me thinking, and last night I had a thought and wanted to run it by some people.”

“It is an intriguing idea. I expect it will not work, but even chasing down a dead end can be a fascinating adventure. Do you think this is a topic you can write your thesis on?”

“Are you kidding? I’d love to.”

“Here is what I will need you to do. I am going to get you a room for this. A small conference room with too many whiteboards. You and your friends are free to use that room whenever you want. What you need to do, though, is divide the problem into a set of smaller problems.”

“That sounds great.”

“Here is how you pay for it. Every week, someone from your group will give a talk on one aspect of this concept. I want clear titles, and deeply detailed talks.”

“Every week? What if I don’t have progress one week?”

“You can talk about any detail of the project that interests you. We learn as much from failure as success. More, actually, since it is so much more common and really makes us examine the research. On my part, I will see to it that there are always snacks at your talks.”

They began working on it, after hours, in locked rooms. The JRC didn’t pry as long as there were regular meetings with titles they could show the world to prove they were working on something. And Savic’s talks drew larger crowds each week. Savic let the others talk, too. Every week he would file the talk with a title, a transcript, the slides, and a count of the attendees. No one read it, but JRC tracked the attendee count.

There were numerous subproblems, and people picked them up as they could. Each problem got its own whiteboard, showing its current state.

The first was how to get the helium up the straw. Rithika herself came up with a plan for this. She proposed a series of simple booster pumps spaced five thousand kilometers apart, each adding about five psi. Not the only choice at all, and subject to optimization, but good enough. It was enough to keep the helium flowing. This was marked solved.

Jason, an electrical engineer from Michigan, attacked the energy problem. The total demand for extraction and pumping was around five hundred megawatts per engine. That would take eight thousand acres of solar panels near Jupiter. Not easy. Not possible. But Jason had an idea. Whisper-thin aluminized mylar formed to concentrate the light, bringing eight thousand acres down to eight hundred acres. Still enormous, and now there were new problems. Alignment had to be precise, and the mylar sheets were fragile. 

Distribution was another issue. They needed power on both ends of the split rig. The mass of an aluminum cable was enormous, and the resistance was too much. Jason split it up. He put a large solar collector on the rig, another large one five thousand kilometers up at the first booster pump, and a small one at each booster pump after that. His ideas drew interest, but were not a solution. No one had improvements. The whiteboard stayed static.

A related problem was maintaining the verticality of the tube. As gas would flow up, it would need to be accelerated laterally. With a little, that was fine, but for tons of gas, the tube would curve. Savic knew this was his problem. Tons of gas going up, all needing a gentle nudge to accelerate laterally. The force came from the sides of the straw in the cable. Savic added a second straw, but for this one he pumped in a propellant gas, and set up a series of ion emitters along the cable. Unprocessed atmosphere made sense and is very light, but it would also cause corrosion and hydrogen embrittlement. Then it struck him that Rithika’s design created enormous quantities of purified *⁴He*. Using helium as the propellant worked well. It did not erode the tube, pumps or ion emitters and had a little more mass reducing the energy requirements. Respecting Rithika’s booster pump plan, he put one ion emitter at each booster pump. Using this active system changed the energy consumption dramatically, requiring an extra four hundred megawatts just for the emitters, and requiring fifteen tons of *⁴He* to be pumped up per day to be used as a propellant, increasing the pumping expense another hundred megawatts. Savic finished his whiteboard and reluctantly restated the problem on Jason’s board, which still had no closure. 

Then there was material degradation. The tube would be bathed in Jupiter’s radiation belts, high-energy protons and electrons eroding the material on timescales measured in months. The atmospheric end would face ammonia crystals and hydrogen sulfide aerosols, each gust stripping away microns. The tether would fray unless wrapped in protective layers that added prohibitive weight and complexity. Savic prevailed on Marco to address this. The obvious solution was a nice, solid coating of gold. The mass was far too high. But Marco looked at only protecting the last thousand miles. This helped enormously. Rather than gold, he put on a coating of boron nitride nanotubes (BNNT) over this region. They were far more stable, but didn’t have quite the strength-to-weight ratio of CNTs. The induced weight penalty hurt everything else, and was made worse because it still eroded, so it had to be made overly thick. Marco outdid himself on that, though. He had crawlers moving up and down the tether inspecting it everywhere. When a defect was found, the crawler immediately did repairs using BNNTs. This allowed a thinner coating, because it would be repaired as it eroded. No one liked the weight penalty or the added complexity of this maintenance machine, but it seemed unavoidable. The team signed off on Marco’s board.

The dirigible skin faced similar problems. Hydrogen seeped through most membranes over time and it embrittled metals. Jupiter’s atmospheric chemistry was relentless. Materials that lasted years in Earth’s stratosphere broke down in weeks here. Each dirigible would be operational for months at best before needing a complete overhaul. Marco worked a separate whiteboard for the dirigible, using aluminized coatings for the ballonetes, BNNT coatings for the skin, and rigorous crew inspection and maintenance. These were not solutions, per se, but tolerable degradation rates. 

This was the furthest anyone had gotten. Better than anything else in the literature. The concept was sound; it was the execution that remained impossible. Materials never really got solved, and power was completely impossible. But every week, Savic or someone else in the group would give another talk, and Savic dutifully filed the reports. The split-rig stayed on whiteboards, a beautiful impossibility. At least it was an interesting topic.
