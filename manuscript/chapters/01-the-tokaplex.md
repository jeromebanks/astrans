# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 1: The Tokaplex**

| Price of *³He* | $20,000,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **20 kg/year** |
| **Current Consumption** | **12 kg/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **0 to 1 day** |

Dr. Elin Sarai braced against the cold wind as she checked the pressure readings on her slate. *Ilha Fusão* sprawled across an artificial island fifty miles off Lisbon. *D* would meet *³He*, releasing clean fusion energy. The tokamak had worked before, but today wasn’t a test. This was a live connection to Portugal’s grid. One hundred megawatts wasn’t enough to change the world. But it would prove fusion could feed a commercial grid.

The wind shifted, blowing her hair across her face. When she turned back to go inside, she saw Gonçalo Silva coming out.

He joined her on the walkway and looked across the island. “*Bom dia, doutora.*” He looked like he had been up all night. “The *³He* tank is installed. We are ready.”

Elin looked out to sea. “That tank nearly bankrupted us. It’s half the world’s production.” She looked back at Gonçalo. “Are we ready?”

“She’s an angry old *senhora* who’s waited a long time to feel important.”

Gonçalo’s remark lingered in her mind. She knew he meant the plant, but his words fit her, too. Today, this plant would show the world what it could do.

She pulled up the system checklist on her slate. Two weeks ago, it had been full of red and yellow flags. Now there was only a single yellow flag: a pressure differential that was outside expectations but within tolerance. She kept her eyes on the slate. A red warning flickered briefly as the deuterium extractor started up, then turned to green when the flow became steady. Another yellow flickered on her slate, then went green again. “*Valha-nos Deus*,” she muttered. Working this long near Lisbon had affected the curses she used.

A soft rumble passed beneath them. Deuterium tanks were hissing. Trace amounts of *³He* were being let out in tiny bursts. Somewhere far below, in the containment torus, the *D-³He* reaction chamber was beginning its warm-up cycle. The corridor curved, descending in a spiral toward the turbine hall. Elin led the way. They passed a crew of techs hunched over an access panel. In front of them, three massive units stood silent, waiting for steam. Elin paused at the observation window. These were conventional technology, superheated steam driving generator rotors, maybe forty percent efficient if they were lucky. Functional, proven, but wasteful.

“This is the bottleneck,” she said. “We’re extracting thermal energy with nineteenth-century technology.”

Gonçalo shrugged. “Efficiency was never the point. It works. And for a hundred megawatts, it’s enough.”

“For now,” Elin replied.

Each level had its own voice, its own smell. Salt and metal. Polymer and heat. And everywhere vibration, small, subsonic, and constant. Like the plant was holding its breath.

By the time they reached the observation ring around the containment torus, Elin was sweating through her collar. Gonçalo keyed the access door.

Inside, the world changed. The torus was not quiet, but it was sacred. A ring of magnet housings loomed around a core of dense shielding. Inside, a reaction chamber poised to hold a plasma at six hundred million degrees. Cameras fed real-time diagnostics to the translucent wall panels.

The field was up. The magnets were humming.

“I never get used to it,” Gonçalo said.

Elin shook her head. “You shouldn’t.”

She took a last look at the checklist. The yellow flag was still there, but the pressure had not moved. She signed off on it. “*Está pronta*.” 

Gonçalo looked down at his slate, now showing her approval. “You know what Raines told me yesterday?”

Elin sighed. “Do I want to know?”

“He said the press has to see the helium loading into the reactor live on camera. He’s planning to let them into the control room.”

She laughed. It was sharp and brief. “I talked to him about that; there is no way the press or anyone else is going into the control room. I mounted a large twin-blade knife switch on the wall so they can all watch him throw it. The engineers will know what to do. I also wired an Edison bulb to the generator, up above the switch. As the turbine spins up, the bulb will glow.”

After the inspection, they found Raines near the conference room, surrounded by investors, dignitaries, and the Minister of Energy from Argentina, who seemed excited and had already begun hinting at the possibility of a second plant, maybe closer to the tropics. Raines was in his element. Laughing, clasping shoulders, turning charm into sales contracts.

He saw Elin and immediately excused himself, cutting the circle short with a gesture and a few well-oiled words. The group didn’t mind. 

“Elin,” he said, voice warm and certain, like he already knew the answer. “She’s ready, isn’t she?”

She didn’t nod. Just looked him in the eye. “She’ll hold.”

He exhaled and grinned, letting the tension slide off him like a coat. “That’s all I need to hear.”

Raines clapped her gently on the shoulder. “I know how much you hate this day. But thank you.”

“Don’t thank me until she makes it through the week.”

“Deal.”

He turned, buoyant again, already slipping back into his public face, back into the performance of selling the dream to the world. 

They came by tiltrotor and by ferries. Premiers and presidents in motorcades threaded the island’s narrow ring road. The King of England, dressed with understated dignity, arrived by helicopter. Billionaires who had staked fortunes were there, and a small press corps trimmed to just twenty, each one combed, curated, and cleared.

They all gathered in the Assembly Hall. Polished steel and glass under a vaulted ceiling. There was seating for two hundred. Everyone had a view of the oversized screen, and beyond it, the observation pane that stared down to the reactor.

Tobias Raines stepped up to the podium, relaxed, with a smile on his lips.

“When I created Helion Dynamics,” he said, stepping out from behind the podium, “fusion was a fantasy told by physicists. I found a few who believed that fantasy. We needed someplace to build, but the fear of unproven nuclear power was strong. Lisbon agreed to let us build off her shores. We built this island with our own hands. Nothing worked. We’ve replaced the tokamak five times. But now we have tamed the reaction. We have made something real. The world is hungry for energy. Today, this marvel will feed the world’s hunger.”

When he finished, the screen retracted into the ceiling, revealing a switch and a bulb. The audience began murmuring and whispering to each other. Raines held up his hand, and the crowd went silent. He grabbed the green glass handle firmly, lifting it. The twin blades of the knife switch were a foot long, heavy, polished copper. Bracing with his legs, he drove it home.

An indicator light came on behind the glass in the control room. The supervisor called out. “Raines is ready.”

A technician set down his *bica* and pulled gently on a lever. Engineers moved like ghosts across the command tier. The reactor hissed. Somewhere deep in her belly, valves opened. *D* met *³He*.

There was no explosion. No flash. Just the warm, unremarkable glow of a single, antique Edison bulb wired to a ceremonial loop.

It came to life slowly, dimly at first, then glowing brilliantly.

The room erupted in applause.

\*\*\*

Hours later, after the reception emptied, Elin and Raines stood in the observation room, watching the Edison bulb glow. The plant hummed steadily beneath them. 

Elin pulled herself onto a chair, resting her arms on the table, bowing her head.

Raines turned, gesturing broadly with his arms. “Argentina’s in,” he said. “They claim fusion is central to their national industrialization plans. They plan to have an artificial island off the coast ready in six months, large enough for a ten-gigawatt tokaplex. How soon can we have it built?”

She slowly turned to him. “That scale would require a ton of *³He* every year. We don’t have that. We’ve talked about this before.”

“Elin, I know it is expensive, but you have no idea how much money they will give, now that it is running. I have already scheduled calls with suppliers. They are charging about twenty thousand dollars per gram.”

“There are no suppliers at the scale you’re talking about. The nuclear industry only produces about twenty kilograms per year. Total. We need tons.”

Her eyes moved back to the numbers on her slate, tracking the plasma and first-wall temperatures.

“That’s fine. That’s what the industry is doing today. But we can breed tritium. It decays to become *³He*.”

“Yes, that’s viable,” Elin answered. “But it’s a few kilograms, and depends on scraping traces from fission systems that were not built for this. And tritium has a half-life of a decade. That trickle is enough to keep this tokaplex running, but not a larger tokaplex.”

“Tritium is only a part of the picture. I know a supplier pulling helium from the Minnesota Topaz site. He can get a thousand tons for the right price.” 

“The Topaz site is very rich in *³He*. But that thousand tons might hold a hundred grams of it,” she said. “The rest is helium ash. That’s not helpful for us. Tobias, *Ilha Fusão* was built to prove fusion can power electric generation. We have proved that. But if we don’t open up more *³He* reserves, this tokaplex is nothing but a curio.”

Tobias Raines said nothing. He watched the Edison bulb powered by their steady reactor, then turned toward the window.

Elin was watching the gibbous Moon rise.
