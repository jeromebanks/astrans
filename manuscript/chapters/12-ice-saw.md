The line went silent.

Pacha dropped his shovel and ran. The printer was still running when Ellie flipped the switch to standby and sprinted. Dust kicked up behind her boots.

When they arrived, Mira had the casing split, revealing the motor’s internals. The coils were scorched black. Red dust clung to the interior walls, packed into seams and corners where it should never have reached. The insulation had frayed and burned, peeled back like scorched fabric. Mira stood beside the motor, arms crossed, eyes locked on the damage.

Jakob didn’t look up. He pointed at the motor guts. “No backups,” he said. “I brought blades, belts, bearings. Not the one thing that matters.”

Tom stared at the open casing. “You didn’t pack a spare motor?”

Jakob didn’t answer.

“I should have,” Jakob said. He didn’t look up. “I knew better. Two is one, one is none. I say it. I believe it. I missed this one.”

Ellie leaned in, frowning. “We can maybe rewind the motor. Depends on how deep the damage goes.”

Mira stayed quiet. Her jaw was tight.

Pacha finally said what everyone else had been circling. “Can we repair it?”

Jakob didn’t answer right away. He exhaled, long and steady. “Not here. Not with what we’ve got. Not in time. We have eight days to cut and process three more blocks. Then the launch window opens. That’s the contract. That’s the water Ceres needs. If we miss it…” He stopped, not finishing the sentence.

Ellie knelt beside the housing, touched it with one gloved hand. “We don’t have another way to cut blocks?”

“Not the way we need to,” Jakob replied. “The sled gave us depth, speed, and control.”

He looked at each of them. He had no answers.

Mira finally spoke. “So what do we do?”

Jakob looked down at the wrecked saw. Then out the airlock window, where the sky bled into dust.

“We figure it out,” he said. “There is no backup here. We figure it out.”

No one spoke. 

Dinner was hot stew, thick with lentils and dried vegetables. No one said much. Forks scraped quietly. 

Mira sat stiff, back straight, eyes forward. She hadn’t touched her food. Tom pushed things around on his plate with slow, absent motions. Ellie sat, not talking, not eating much either.

Jakob didn’t join them. He sat at the end of the main corridor, near the airlock. Helmet off, still in his dusty suit, elbows on knees, staring at the saw motor on the floor in front of him. The casing was cracked open, the blackened copper glinting under the overhead lights. He hadn’t spoken since dinner started.

They finished processing the last two ice blocks quietly. The filtration system hummed in the background, water melting in the chamber, trickling through screens and mesh filters into storage. Steam rose in thin threads, caught the lights.

When they finished, Ellie stood up. “I’m going to take care of the printer.” She then wordlessly put on her gear. Her boots thudded in the corridor. The printer had to be cleaned before they went to bed, before the polymer inside started to break down. If it did, it was a loss they couldn’t afford.

Back in the corridor, Jakob hadn’t moved.

He sat alone with the motor at his feet, the weight of the entire mission heavy on his shoulders. They had planned for everything. Redundancies, backups, contingencies. Two is one, one is none. That had been the rule. And now here they were. One saw. None left. No launch. No contract. No way forward. Jakob didn’t say it out loud.

The hum of the water processors. The clink of utensils left in bowls. Jakob composed the message with mechanical fingers. No grand speech, no dramatics. Just the facts, numbers, part codes. Three sled saws. Ten motors. Redundant systems for a world that didn’t forgive mistakes.

*To. Max Juno*  
*Subject. Equipment requisition – Priority*

*Three sled saws. Ten compatible drive motors. Full specs attached.*  
*Ship ASAP. No substitutions.*

*\-Jakob*

He stared at the screen for a while after it sent. He didn’t expect a reply. He had no idea what time it was in Lagos. Max would get the message when he got it. But no matter what Max did, no help would come soon enough. Years, if everything aligned perfectly. He needed it in days.

He closed the message. Then stood. There was still a job to do.

Back in Nigeria, Max Juno was already halfway through his second cup of coffee when the message arrived. Max sat for a long moment, staring at his own reflection in the glass. He opened the procurement system, out of habit more than hope. Three sled saws. Ten motors. Full specs. He filled in the form and stepped through the menus.

Estimated delivery: thirty-four months. He sat back. By then, it wouldn’t matter. The cost was hundreds of millions. 

Max stared at the screen, then reached for the cancel key. He didn’t place the order.

There was no point pretending he could help. 

Back on Mars, Ellie dragged a power conduit across the red dirt. It snaked from the ship’s auxiliary port to the half-finished dome. The doorway stood open, rough-edged, unsealed.

She moved deliberately. Not quickly, but without allowing hesitation where despair might settle. 

Pacha joined her at the printer a few minutes later, ready to help with the shutdown. She looked at him oddly.

Then she spoke. “Pacha, I can’t do anything for that motor. I wish I could. But there is something I can do, if you’ll help me. Let’s finish this dome.”

Pacha nodded. They had worked hard all day and deserved rest, but the whole team needed someplace better to live than the ship. 

Ellie watched the controls while Pacha shoveled regolith into the hopper. Then she joined him. Shovel after shovel. The printer extruded each layer in a slow, molten spiral. The curve of the dome was closing now. More overhang. They had to print slower, wait between passes for the material to cool and hold shape. Ellie adjusted the print speed manually.

Four hours in, they had said nothing, but looked at each other, complicit in their transgression.

Pacha pumped his fist when the printer stopped. Ellie didn’t smile, but she looked up at the finished dome and exhaled. Dust already clung to the surface. The polymer looked dulled, aged, like it had weathered years instead of a sol.

She ran the electrical hookup and initiated the systems check. Ellie watched the green light blink on the control panel, steady and soft. The pressure test held. Airflow was stable. The dome was sealed. It would hold. The compressor started pumping in the Martian atmosphere, and the recycler struggled to keep up, converting the *CO₂* into oxygen and soot.

She stood for a moment, one gloved hand resting on the junction box. Then she turned and walked back to the ship.

Inside, she passed Tom and Jakob without a word, ducked into storage, grabbed her sleeping roll, and cycled through the airlock again.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to.

Mira guessed what was going on. She came next, pack slung over one shoulder. Then Pacha, carrying the cavies in a pressure box. “The floor will be warmer than my bunk,” he said.

That was as close to an announcement as there was.

They were going to sleep in the ag dome tonight. It wasn’t fully ready. It had air and heat, though, and space. And they built it. It was theirs.

Tom and Jakob brought their gear last.

Even if it all failed tomorrow, even if they missed the launch window, they would spend this one night in the world they were trying to build. A night in a home they built. A claim on a distant planet staked in sleep.

They put their cots near each other, on the side opposite the cavy pen. As they all prepared to sleep, Jakob took a moment with Ellie. 

“The setting here is much calmer. I still don’t know what we will do tomorrow, but we will sleep better tonight. Thank you.” 

In the morning, the dome was dimly lit. Pacha programmed the lights to start low, build toward midday, then fade again at the end of the sol. The air inside still held a hush, like a cathedral at dawn, the natural reddish hues of the regolith lending a regal feel.

They stirred from their sleep slowly. It had been the best night since launch. The sleeping pads were soft, the heat even, the air dry but warm. There was space to stretch out. No clanging bulkheads, no humming walls a meter from their heads. Just quiet. 

When Mira sat up and rubbed her eyes, she didn’t bother to put a shirt on. She looked around the peaceful dome. She could smell the polymer faintly, but mostly she could smell the soil and the cavies and the people. It was calming.

Pacha was the first one moving with purpose. He checked the cavy pen. They all looked warm, nestled together like furry little coals. He topped up their water and scattered feed, murmuring to them in Spanish like they were his children. If the mission had any hope of surviving long-term, it ran on cavies and calories.

After the animals, he turned to the stove. It was just a hotplate and a pot, but it was enough. Oatcakes, protein mash, dried fruit. He called them to the table, and they all gathered. Some of them had put on light cotton thermals as they got out of bed. No one wore shoes. This was home.

They ate together, quietly at first. A few soft laughs. It felt like something new had started. The motor was still broken, but the world hadn’t ended last night.

When Jakob came by the table, he had shaved. Just a rough pass with the clippers, but it was the first time since Earth. It made him look less ragged. He watched the others as they ate, not with concern, but with clarity.

When the dishes were cleared, he stood. 

“Six blocks,” he said. “Mira?”

She nodded. “Done. All filtered. All refrozen. Sixty tons are ready.”

He looked around the dome. “We lost the saw. We lost time. We can’t afford either. But we’re not finished. Ceres still needs water. That’s why we’re here.”

He met their eyes, one by one. “Here’s the plan.”

He stepped forward into the light. “We are not in danger of dying today. Let’s be clear. We have power. We have heat. We have clean water. The dome holds pressure. The cavies are fed. Pacha’s crops are germinating.”

A pause. “But no one’s coming to get us. Not because they don’t care, but because they can’t. Max said he can get us the parts in three years. The only ship that brings us back to Earth is the one we pay for. We pay with water.”

He gestured to the ceiling above them.

“This dome, it’s better than the ship. It’s warm. It smells like soil. We made it. We can live here.”

Ellie, quietly, pulled on a thin undershirt. Tom put on boxers.

“We’ve got sixty tons of clean water ready to go. If we ship that now, Ceres gets a week’s supply. Longer, if they ration, and they will.”

He exhaled once, steady.

“Last night I thought we were done. But now I’ve got a plan. It’s brutal, maybe stupid. But it will work.”

He let the silence stretch. 

“We brought three adzes. They’re meant for smoothing the surface, not cutting. We’re going to hand-cut the next forty tons. Muscle, breath, repetition. I don’t know if we can cut even a ton at a time, but whatever size we can cut, we stack it up on the sled.”

He looked to Mira. Then Tom. Then Pacha. Then Ellie.

“You swing until your grip gives out. Then you rotate. Fix a filter. Feed the cavies. Make a shipping container for the ice. Then back to the adze. We keep going.”

No one moved. 

“We’ll burn a lot of calories. So we eat more. I’m not rationing. If we run short, we wait on Pacha’s harvest. That’s the gamble. We move forward. If we succeed, we launch on time. If we fail, we lose the window. We launch a partial load, or we launch late. But we don’t stop.”

He met their eyes again.

“I’m not asking for volunteers. There’s no committee. This is what we will do.”

Silence. He looked around at a team, people who knew what they were doing.

When Jakob got to the ice field, he looked around.

Pacha had used a pole to chip out the final cut on the seventh block. His arms hung heavy, shoulders low, but the job was done. Tom and Mira were guiding the ice onto the sled with ropes and winches, careful, methodical. Jakob watched them work.

Ellie was starting an adze cut. Lots of chips, but chips are also ice. She was cutting a long line. They would break off one-ton chunks later. 

They weren’t moving fast. But they were moving. And that was enough for now.

Tom’s voice came over Jakob’s radio, a direct call. “Jakob, we still need to calibrate the launcher. I need your help.”

Jakob started walking out to the Mass driver. He caught up with Tom, who was carrying a few boulders on a sled.

Tom began speaking. “I need to walk you through something.” He pulled up a slate layered with elliptical arcs, transfer windows, and annotated times. “How do you see this project right now?”

Jakob frowned. “Twelve sols until Day Zero. First launch. Then one shipment per week for ten weeks. Then we wait for the next window.”

Tom shook his head. “That’s the wrong perspective.” He turned the slate. “Ceres doesn’t care about launch dates. They care about delivery dates. If we want a shipment to land there every week, we have to hit a moving target.”

Lines flickered across the screen: curved orbits from Mars to Ceres. Tom tapped the first. “Day Zero is the fastest transfer. But to get them a shipment one week later, we can’t launch one week later. Ceres has moved. The second shipment takes two days longer to arrive, so it launches five days after the first. Then three days later. The curve compresses.”

Jakob leaned closer. “We’re overlapping launches?”

“It inverts,” Tom said. “Week eight ships before week seven. Week nine before week six. The curve bends backwards.” He changed windows. Four early orbits glowed green. “These launches happen before Day Zero but arrive at Ceres in weeks eleven through fourteen. Four extra shipments. Eighty million we didn’t count on.”

Jakob stared. “Send ice early; it gets there late.”

“Exactly.” Tom swiped again. Longer curves swept the far side of the solar system. “And here \- if we launch the other direction before blackout, we hit weeks sixty-three and sixty-four. Nine extra deliveries. That cuts the blackout from sixty-one weeks to forty-eight.”

They got to the mass driver, and Tom loaded up a boulder. He programmed the machine and asked Jakob to reload every time there was a launch. Tom hiked down range a couple of miles and set up a monitor. The first boulder flew, landing ten miles downrange. 

“Hook, two minutes off,” Tom called out, adjusting the parameters. 

Jakob loaded another. He reflected on Tom’s plan. “Some of these blocks will go halfway around the solar system.”

“That’s how you hit a moving target.”

The second boulder launched fast. It also landed about ten miles downrange. “Perfect. Dialed in.

Jakob loaded the third boulder. He looked down at the slate again. This was an opportunity. A narrow one. Exciting, if they had a saw. But now it meant nothing. They would be lucky to make their first shipment. Hitting extra shipments was not possible. 

Tom spoke, “Look, Jakob, I know we have a lot of stress right now, and I am not helping with this. I wanted to explain this to you before, back when we were making ice. Now it seems pointless. But I think you should know. It changes all our scheduling.”

The third boulder launched faster. It flew over a hundred miles down range. “Still perfect. We are dialed in,” Tom shouted. He walked back to Jakob, and together they returned to the dome.

“We’re burning out,” Jakob said. “Everyone’s trying, but the adzes are killing us. Shoulders, wrists, lower backs. Another week like this and someone’s going to tear something.”

Tom didn’t argue. He’d seen Mira wrap her wrist at lunch. Ellie’s gait had shifted. Everyone was compensating.

Jakob kicked at a rock. “What if we reforge the adze heads? Hammer them out into axe blades. Something that swings straighter.”

Tom gave it a second. “I can do the work. Might help with bite depth. But it won’t solve the strain. Still heavy tools, still tough ice.”

Jakob nodded. “I know. I just don’t see a good option.”

Tom didn’t answer right away. He kept hauling the empty sled back over uneven rock. Then he said, “We’ve still got the cargo bikes. Both of them. The ones we used for cardio.”

Jakob frowned. “You want us biking across the ice?”

“No. Mount a bike frame on a rig, use the drive wheel to turn a belt. I’d make a smaller saw blade. No tungsten carbide. Not eighteen feet in diameter. Maybe six feet in diameter. The bike spins that. It won’t be fast, but faster than hand-chipping. And we’d shift the strain to legs instead of arms.”

Jakob turned that over. “You think the bikes can handle that kind of load?”

“They’re built for cargo. I’ll weld a stand to a sled to hold the frame steady, and fix a belt to a pulley. Add a guide system for the blade. Maybe a screw mechanism to control cut depth.”

“We’d still wear ourselves out.”

Tom shrugged. “We’d be cutting ice, not cartilage. It’s an upgrade. Overall, it is much less effort, smoother, and uses stronger muscles than cutting ice with the adzes.”

Jakob exhaled through his nose. “Make two. We’ll rotate crews through them.”

“I’ll start tonight.”

Jakob looked at Tom seriously. “Let’s do it.”

Tom looked quizzically at Jakob. “Yeah, I just said I would.”

Jakob shook his head. “No, the other thing. Let’s launch tomorrow. Let’s hit every extra launch date you suggested. We are depending on your bike saws, you realize, but let’s do it.”

Tom’s pace picked up. “Yes. I mean, uh. Yes, I’ll get on it. Mira is good at this stuff. She and I will deliver two bikes by tomorrow.”

Jakob didn’t know if he was a fool or a genius. But right now he felt excited.

When they got to camp, Jakob went in the ship. He pulled on a headset and took a seat at the comms board. The air felt thinner here, despite the pressure seals. He stared through the window, scanning the empty horizon like Knox might somehow materialize from it.

Calling Ceres was always strange, like shouting into a canyon and waiting half an hour for the echo.

“Dr. Knox,” he began, voice steady, measured. “I’ll be direct. We lost our saw. We’re cutting with adzes now. I don’t know how much ice we’ll be able to harvest.”

He paused, then continued.

“But here’s the thing. We’re prepping the shipment for week 14, launching tomorrow. We believe we can still reach weeks 11 through 13 if we push. I’ve attached the full launch schedule and estimated arrivals. It’s aggressive. Maybe too aggressive. But we’re going to try.”

He ended the message and queued the transmission.

The red sky hadn’t changed. The sun burned low on the horizon, indifferent. Dr. Knox didn’t yet know the message had been sent.

When Jakob got down to the icefield, Tom was hard at work. Jakob took Pacha’s adze and started cutting. Pacha exhaled, exhausted. He gave Jakob a status update. “Jakob, I gotta tell you, the ag dome’s working. I’m washing and breaking down regolith, building soil. I added some carbon powder from the air recycler and some liquid fertilizer I brought up. So sweet. Sprouts are growing. It’s like a dream. The cavies aren’t cramped anymore, and they’re actually happy.”

Then he paused.

Jakob heard it before it came. “But?”

“I need a greenhouse,” Pacha said. “Not just grow lights. Real surface exposure. I’m doing what I can under LEDs, but if you want real food, we need sun-grown plants.”

Jakob closed his eyes. “You need space. Light. Water. Air.”

“Water’s not the hold-up right now,” Pacha said, gently. “It’s space.”

Jakob nodded, but didn’t answer. The truth was starting to tighten around him. Water for Ceres. Food for Mars. There was no room for both to be first.

Then his radio crackled. A long delay, followed by the calm, even voice of Dr. Knox, the kind of tone you develop when every decision has consequences.

“Jakob, good to hear from you. I want you to know we see what you’re doing. It’s dangerous work, and we’re grateful. What you’re giving us, we can’t get any other way.”

There was a pause. Knox continued.

“We’d been planning around ten shipments. That’s what the window looked like. But if you can really hit fourteen, we’ll make it worth your while. I’m not offering charity. We’re rationing already. Your ice lets us keep the mines open longer, keep the factories warm, keep people working. It buys us time.”

Jakob stood for a moment longer, processing.

Then he started swinging his adze again.

In a few minutes, the comm pinged again.

“Jakob, one more thing,” Knox said, almost sheepish. “We’ve got iron. Thousands of tons of it. Comes out with the molybdenum. But we’ve got no carbon. We can’t make steel. If you ever stumble across graphite out there, we’d be happy to pay for that, too.”

Jakob let out a short laugh, low and sharp.

Iron with no carbon. Ice saws with no motors. Plants with no space.

Everywhere, a piece missing.

Jakob looked over the ice. The others were already swinging. Mira was steady and rhythmic, while Ellie leaned into each strike with the quiet focus of someone burning calories just to stay warm and functional. The blocks glinted under the low sun, their edges still sharp from the adze work. They would pile up about five tons on the sled, now, a mixture of smaller blocks and chips from the adzes. They had processed eighty tons, and the next load was almost ready. 

Tom spelled Ellie, and she came over to talk to Jakob.

Jakob was direct. “Pacha needs a big greenhouse.”

That stopped her. She exhaled sharply, not quite a laugh. She tilted her head. “You want that printed now?”

Jakob shook his head. “I want it, yeah. But not yet. We can’t live on cavies and algae powder. Pacha’s right. We need real space for things with roots. He says five acres. That was always the plan. But that plan went out with the motor. For the next ten weeks, we are all ice cutters first. Everything else waits.”

Ellie understood. There was nothing to debate. She stepped back, caught her breath, and watched him swing. “I’ll start planning it, though. We get through these ten weeks, I will build it fast.”

They hauled another sled up to the processor. Only five tons at a time, to keep it from falling off. Three more loads to go. Jakob hoped to get another one loaded before dinner time, but he was going to be strict about no cutting after dinner. They could get two loads tomorrow. 

Pacha came back from processing the ice. He called them all to dinner. “It’s seven O’Clock according to the ship. Time to eat.”

Jakob looked at the load, only half full. Whatever they loaded would be enough. 

After dinner, Tom caught Mira. The two of them suited up and went to the ship. Jakob knew why. No one else even thought about it.

Morning came quietly. The warmth inside was a minor miracle, and no one wanted to leave it. Everyone ached from the work the day before. 

Pacha got up first. He moved silently past the others, pulling on a light shirt as he made his way to the cavies. They chattered softly at him from their enclosure, little noses twitching. He fed them from a bin of pellets, checked their water, then turned to set the table. No one had said it was his job, but it had become one of those things that simply happened because he did it.

Ellie woke slowly, stretching in the warmth of the dome. After six months of cramped ship quarters, the cot and gravity felt luxurious. She stood, rolled her shoulders, felt her body move properly, then went to find her base layer, letting it slide on slowly. Mars was supposed to be harsh, but right now? Right now, it felt like freedom.

Pacha glanced over, then back to his work.

“Morning,” she said, grinning.

“Morning,” he replied, not looking up. “There’s hot water if you want it.”

She sat at the table and sipped. As the others woke, 

The others drifted in, rubbing at sore shoulders, flexing their hands. The work left its mark, even if no one complained. Tom came in from outside. The first rig was standing, a cargo bike locked into a welded frame, a belt looped down from the rim to a saw spindle, rough depth screw bolted in. It wasn’t elegant, but it spun.

At breakfast, Mira couldn’t stop. “Guys, we built a pedal-powered saw,” she announced, arms wide. “Not a toy, either. I mean, it’s smaller than the old one. We had to make blades for it. Six foot, not eighteen foot. But that’s still real. You can feel the bite. Smooth cut, no chatter. Way faster than the adze.”

Ellie looked up. “Seriously?”

“Seriously, seriously. No shoulder pain, either. You use your legs. I could ride that thing all day.”

Pacha raised an eyebrow. “That’s a promise?”

Mira grinned. “You want a turn? We’re making a second one today.”

Jakob leaned in. “Is it stable? Will it hold alignment?”

Tom nodded. “Good enough. And if it isn’t, we will fix it.”

The mood shifted. Not giddy, but lighter. Bodies still ached, the math hadn’t changed, but the path forward suddenly seemed a lot better.

Jakob looked around the room at tired faces, calloused hands, too-thin bodies made lean by too much work and not enough food. No one had given up, but this couldn’t last. 

They all left together to try it out. Tom popped it up on a sled to get it down there. The sled didn’t roll. Tom just put it on another sled, and they all went down except Mira and Jakob. Mira crouched beside the sled that didn’t roll, her gloved hands steady. Jakob brought her a tarp. Soon, it was dotted with tools. mallet, wrenches, a grease gun, and a box of spare parts. She didn’t look up. Her focus was on the left front wheel housing, already disassembled and resting on its side like a tired limb.

The sleds were simple machines, flat aluminum frames mounted on twelve aluminum wheels, six to a side, with minimal suspension and no steering. They were built low to the ground, squat and stable, each one rated to carry a hundred tons. Their only concession to technology was the electric assist built into the hubs, small motors connected to an onboard battery, just enough to take the edge off pushing.

“This wheel’s been dragging,” Mira explained to Jakob. “Bearings are shot.”

She tapped the old bearing out with a soft mallet. It dropped onto the tarp with a dry metallic click. The outer ring was scored, and the ball cage looked warped, with too much grit in the housing, too much wear.

“Dust gets in,” she said, mostly to herself. “Regolith fines. Starts grinding everything down. Same as the motor.”

From a foil-lined pouch, she took a fresh bearing. She expected this before they left. She had maybe a hundred in the supply locker. She pressed it into place with slow care, aligning the motor housing before bolting it shut. The work was quiet and methodical, practiced. When she was done, she spun the wheel, then cleaned up. 

“These motors aren’t for pulling,” she added, almost offhand. “They’re too weak. They are just there to keep you from wrecking your spine. Light motors mean I could pack more replacements. Looks like we are going to need them.”

By lunch, both rigs were running. The bikes weren’t comfortable, and no one rode them for fun, but the saws cut faster. They were cutting one-ton triangular blocks, but these stacked up well. They would load up eight tons at a time. Soon they had finished a hundred tons, and Tom and Jakob loaded it into a fiberglass carrier. They loaded more ice into the processor. 

Tom grabbed some food at lunch, then He and Jakob left to load up the first hundred-ton block of ice in the mass driver. Together, they slid the block into place. The box it rode in was shaped like a giant lemon, made out of glass fiber spun from molten regolith. Ellie had made four before the saw disaster. Tom pulled up his slate, checked the programming, and triggered the sequence. The launcher responded with a hum, its alignment shifting minutely. Jakob watched the arc plot stabilize. It didn’t launch. The driver would wait for the right microsecond and launch then.

Without another word, they turned back toward camp. Inside the dome, the others were wrapping up lunch. Mira looked up as they stepped through the airlock.

“Launch is set?”

Tom nodded. “Automated. The clock handles the microsecond window. We’ll know in an hour if the launch worked.”

“Like hell,” Ellie grinned, putting on her gear. “I’m going to watch the launch.” 

Pacha started putting on his gear. “Dudes, no way I am missing this.”

The rest followed. Tom and Jakob looked at each other. Tom spoke up. “Jakob, there is no reason to watch. If it doesn’t launch correctly, there is nothing we can do. I will program in a new launch tomorrow.”

“No, Tom,” Jakob answered. “We are not going to fix it if it doesn’t work. We are going because we want to watch the inaugural flight of our business. Everything may look kind of hard right now, but we are still launching a hundred tons of ice to Ceres on a launch window we didn’t know existed. Let’s go.”

The team got to the mass driver about ten minutes before the launch. Tom propped his slate up with a rock, showing a countdown timer. They chattered excitedly, the pains from the day’s work receding, replaced with excitement and a sense of accomplishment. 

They all counted the last ten seconds together. “Ten. Nine. Eight. Seven. Six. Five. Four. Three. Two. O…”

The railgun clicked, and the package started moving before they finished “one.” It was faster than they could believe. Even Tom, who designed it and knew exactly how it worked, was amazed by the launch. They could see a tail of red dust coming up behind where the ice had already passed. The Martian horizon is only two miles away, so they couldn’t watch it go down the entire path, but they did see it when it started across the sky. A glowing red ball, faster than they could believe. Everyone cheered. 

The team was excited on the way home. Mira spoke up. “We did it. We actually did it. And the ice saws Tom and I built. Are they great or what?”

Pacha laughed. “They are great, Mira. I still finish exhausted, but not all beaten up as the adzes left me. And they are so much faster. So, does that mean Ellie might have some free time to build for me?”

Ellie thought about it. “Well, we only have two saws. If we are keeping up with the schedule, I will start your greenhouse. If we fall behind, I stop. Deal?”

“¡Trato\!” Pacha shouted. “Let’s do it.”

The math had changed. They could load multiple shipments a day. They returned to the ice fields. No time to celebrate. They kept cutting. But if they kept working, they would be able to keep Ceres well stocked.

Their third morning in the dome was quiet but not slow. People moved with purpose. The sleds were loaded. The ice was pure. The mass driver hummed and launched again, without an audience this time. They were actually cutting faster than they needed to. Tom had found a way to send more water than they had ever planned.

For now, it was working.
