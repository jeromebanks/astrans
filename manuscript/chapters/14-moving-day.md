Mira and Pacha had been out cutting ice all morning.

Jakob had pulled up insolation charts. “Tom, looking at our current solar power output, do you believe we can still make the trek north on our own?”

“It’s a race against decreasing sunlit hours and how far we can travel in a day. By delaying until now, the decision to wait has been made.”

Jakob knew that when he had not pushed back during the call from Paris, there was no other possible outcome.

Ellie sighed. “Yes, we need to go north, but not to Olympus Mons. The ice is at Planum Boreum. We need the water, and, to be frank, so does Callum. He just doesn’t know it.”

“When we first arrived, we already knew we needed a way to get to Planum Boreum for this season,” Jakob reminded them. “Callum is that way. We stop a month or so where he is, get his team operational, then he will take us north to Planum Boreum, and we will be back in business sending ice to Ceres. It’s a shorter flight for Callum, too, so he can supply his own camp.”

“As long as no one gets attached to the miner’s site,” Ellie cautioned. “We have to have water. We have to move on.”

Jakob looked back at Ellie. “OK. Now, what I need you to do is get ready for the move. And be excited about it. Agreed?”

As Ellie nodded, the airlock began cycling, and Mira and Pacha came in. 

Mira was excited. “Tom, we dropped ten blocks in the cycler. Another launch is ready.”

“Good. I’ll slow launch that load tomorrow. That will give us one more week before we need to resume shipments.” 

Callum hadn’t arrived in ten hours or twenty. The hope of a daylight landing was gone. When it got dark, Jakob climbed atop the ag dome and began slowly waving a beacon light northward, scanning the horizon. After a few hours, he saw a new light near the horizon, too bright to be a star. Jakob froze, afraid to move in case the light was some trick of dust or fatigue.

The radio crackled a moment later. “G’day, mates. Callum’s my name.” 

Jakob’s shoulders relaxed. The calm message erased a mountain of worries. As the gyroplane aligned with the makeshift runway, its huge coaxial rotors set it down softly. Callum unlatched his suit from the ship’s oxygen and electrical feeds and stepped out. Tom stepped forward, wordlessly hooking up the hydrolox lines to refuel.

“Welcome to Magazine Beach\!” Mira called out, her voice warm and inviting. “Come on in.”

They all went through the airlock into the ag dome. When Callum removed his helmet, he was hit by the sudden, thick scent of damp soil, growing things, and the musk of the cavies. It felt earthy and alive. 

“My crew’s livin’ in our ship for the last four months. Rank doesn’t even cover it. Your place smells sweet and full of life. Is this what you’re gonna put together for us?” Callum asked.

Ellie explained her priorities. “More or less. When I get up there, building an ag dome is my first priority. That lets us raise livestock and gives Pacha a place to start crops and mess with the dirt. We will all sleep there until food production is stable.”

Pacha gave him a tour, showing him the plant beds and trays under light. Callum leaned closer, smiling when he saw the cavies in their pen. After months of algae and supplements, the sight of anything fresh made his mouth water.

“So you’re Pacha,” Callum whispered. “Max sent me these, said to hand ’em over quietly.” Callum pulled out two baggies of soil, one dark, one more multicolored. “One is Andean, one is Himalayan, but they both just look like dirt to me. What’s the go with ’em?”

Pacha moved between Callum and the rest of the crew. “Thanks, man. Keep it down. The rest of the team doesn’t need to know. My legal mycorrhiza cultures are failing, hurting crop yields. I need a healthy soil biome. The robust, wild fungi and bacteria in the dark Andean soil and the more colorful Tibetan soil are promising for our low-pressure environment. But, you know, we aren’t supposed to do it this way. I’ve just run out of options.”

Callum gave him a wink, and no more was said. Pacha put the soil away in a drawer, and they rejoined the rest. 

Jakob was sitting at the table. “Hey Callum, you must be famished after that flight. Pacha is a first-rate cook. Let’s have some dinner.”

Callum took a seat. “Nothing could sound better. I’m starved.”

Mira set the plates and cups of water. Pacha first served up a simple salad of microgreens with freshly baked dinner rolls. 

Callum devoured it. “It’s been too long since I ate real food. These rolls are amazing. They have an earthy flavor I love, and they are actually chewy. After months of paste, it is great to have something to sink my teeth into. What are they?”

“Amaranth is our staple here. It grows well at low pressure, even without mycorrhiza, and is more nutritious than other grains. I’m glad you like it, because you’ll be eating a lot of it.”

Pacha then served the main course, roast cavy with sliced oca. The aroma of roast meat enveloped Callum. It was mouthwatering. He picked it up and began eating, letting the fats roll down his chin. Then he looked around at the rest of them, using knives and forks. “Sorry, mates. Reckon I got a bit carried away. That meat is delicious.”

Pacha was amused and threw Callum a napkin. “I’m glad you like it. It’s the only sort of meat we have here, but we have it with every meal. How’s the oca?”

“Is that what these little potato things are? Yeah, like lemon zest. Their crunch goes great with the meat. My boys are going to love this food.”

After dinner, Callum excused himself to get cleaned up. Mira showed him the restroom.

Pacha smiled. The Magazine Beach crew had always appreciated the meals, but never so viscerally as Callum. 

When Callum returned, Jakob brought up the plan. “We’ll send Tom and Ellie with you first to get started on a new ag dome.”

“That’ll work,” Callum said. “We’d love a dome like this.”

Jakob nodded. “It’s Pacha’s dome, really. Agriculture trumps everything else. We can stay as long as we are not in his way. We need to get everyone under cover before the move from here is final.”

Tom and Ellie began loading the sleds. Ellie also needed a glassmaker for plumbing. Most people looked at a dome and thought the walls were what mattered. Ellie knew the real work was waste management, lighting, power, air, laundry, and running water. Even in the ag dome, that was what counted.

Callum checked the straps himself. “That’s all you need to get started?”

Ellie pulled her visor down. “It’ll do.”

The gyroplane had been designed for this trip. It needed a full tank to make it back. Tom checked the fuel level before disconnecting the hoses. “All filled. Let’s go.”

Ellie stopped to hug the people staying behind before leaving. “Mira, you remember how to clear it if the sewer backs up, right?”

Mira’s eyes were wet with tears. She didn’t answer. 

Jakob cleared his throat. “We will keep things running here.”

With that, the three of them got in the gyroplane. They plugged their pressure suits into the gyroplane’s oxygen and electricity. Callum checked everyone’s connections, then started off. 

Jakob was fascinated watching the gyroplane ascend. The six-bladed tractor fan was large by Earth standards, twelve feet in diameter, but in Mars’ thin air, it didn’t have enough bite to get the machine rolling. The electric landing gear provided most of the acceleration, with the tractor fan maintaining speed as it lifted off. Then, at fifty feet, the enormous pusher prop extended and unfolded, its thirty-foot radius delivering efficient thrust that carried them steadily north. Jakob watched the bird slip past the horizon, then turned back to the dome. 

The gyroplane kept a steady pace. Callum had set the autopilot almost as soon as they leveled out. “We should arrive around three. I need some shuteye.”

After Callum nodded off, Tom followed suit. Ellie stayed up. The surface of Mars didn’t even look black; it just looked like she was travelling in space, and there were no stars below. The stars above were spectacular. Earth was visible, and Jupiter. Ellie felt at peace among the wandering planets. The gyroplane hummed evenly, a mix of the electric drive whine of the tractor motor and the deeper pulse of the huge pusher prop.

It was past 3 a.m. when Olympus Mons came up on the horizon. She couldn’t see it, but it began blotting out the stars. The gyroplane descended in a long glide. Callum woke and retracted the pusher prop. The basal scarp soon blotted out everything forward except a small light on the landing strip. They gently autorotated down with the tractor fan still pulling, landing on the pediment, a mile south of the scarp. Ellie imagined how this would look when it was built. Larger, stronger, better domes. Enormous greenhouses. Mines. It needed a name that fit both the miners and the Magazine Beach crew. 

When they got out, she grabbed a shovel and started digging. Twenty minutes later, she stood back to admire the word carved deep into the red soil. “Bounty.”

Callum’s transit ship stood a hundred yards away, legs planted deep into the regolith. The hull showed streaks of re‑entry heating and red dust accumulation. Around the base, the three solid rocket boosters looked like battered iron pillars. Their bottom ten feet were crushed and splayed outward where they had absorbed the final impact.

“That ship is enormous,” Tom commented. “It looks beat up.”

“Yeah, nah. You blokes ordered some pretty massive gear, and my crew’s got extensive mining kit. They assembled her in high Earth orbit. She never felt a lick of Earth’s pull, so size wasn’t as much of a problem. Landing her was dicey. None of us thought we would live. It was all autopilot. The mass injection rate increased first. We could really feel the thrust increase after months of barely any gravity. Then we started the skip, bringing us down to subsonic. The parachutes came out, getting our speed low. We dropped just below the shield that way, then the candles lit. Those solid rockets have the raw thrust we needed to avoid making a crater. They stuck out, so when they hit, they crumpled, giving us a soft stop.”

Callum, Tom, and Ellie were all in the airlock. As soon as the pressure normalized, Callum made a sweeping gesture. “Welcome aboard.”

The smell hit them as soon as the inner door opened: lithium grease, warm metal, and the stale tang of long‑cycled air. The interior, for its size, was still close‑packed, crates strapped into the passageways, personal kits lashed to bulkheads. Tom and Ellie remembered their own trip. The Mars transit is rough. They took off their pressure suits and slept on the floor until morning.

In the morning, Ellie got up first. She nudged Tom, and they began cooking pancakes with some amaranth flour from Magazine Beach. The miners appreciated the break from standard rations. As they finished eating, Ellie spoke. “First task is to build the ag dome. Can you guys dig the foundation for me?”

“We’ll get that dug out in no time,” Callum replied. “One thing us boys can do is dig.”

They suited up after breakfast and began unloading. Tom was already at the hatch, helping unstrap the sleds. The miners hauled down the excavator, a tracked backhoe with a frontloader. They had a slew of other equipment for mining, from jackhammers to sizing screens.

Tom and Ellie hauled out the Sabatier unit for polyethylene production. It was small, capable of a ton an hour, matched neatly to the output of the Bosch carbon powder line. The water inlet was underneath, and had an *H₂* pipe coming up from it. Ellie studied it to find the hydrogen bypass. She found the water intake and looked to where the electrolysis section broke off into hydrogen and oxygen. There was the inlet where she could inject hydrogen directly, as long as she kept a couple of gallons of water in the cracker to keep the gas contained.

Ellie started talking to Callum. “This ship was a nuclear thermal rocket,” she said. “Hydrogen propellant?”

“That’s right. Fastest way out here.”

“Making polymer can eat water,” Ellie said. “Back at Magazine Beach, we didn’t care, because we had plenty of water. Here you are parched. The plan is that I am going to use your hydrogen. Is that OK?”

Callum thought for a moment. “We used most of it. Maybe thirty tons are still in the tanks. Take all you want. It will boil off soon, anyway.”

Ellie did the math, then nodded. “That’s two hundred tons of polymer. We can make a larger dome and a few greenhouses.”

Callum smiled. “That sounds great. There is a hydrogen takeoff in the cabin and another one near the door. Now, see that place over there where Petey is standing? Is that where you want the dome?”

Ellie nodded. “Roughly, yeah. Let me mark it out. I am going to need graded regolith, so please move it over by the sizing screens.”

Ellie drew a circle on the ground showing where she wanted the dome, and the excavator came alive, putting a low, electric vibration in the ground. Ellie watched the first cut into the regolith, the loader bucket peeling away the surface in broad, light scoops. Dust slipped through the teeth like dry powder, curling into the thin air before falling back. They fed the first load through the shaker box, separating the dense minerals that block radiation from the light silicates used for glassmaking. 

Callum gave a rowdy wave as he headed back to the gyroplane. “Righto, listen up, you lot\! Be good to Ellie and don’t go actin’ like a bunch of drongos. We’re in dire need of a decent home. I’m flyin’ south now to grab Pacha and bring him up.”

The miners waved as Callum left, then got to work helping Ellie. They understood processing dirt. Sorting and grading went fast. On the pediment, the regolith was shallow. They pulled up five feet of regolith, then started on the bedrock with drills and jackhammers, digging three more feet to finish the basement. 

While they chipped away, Ellie started making polymer with the hydrogen takeoff near the door. That ran by itself while she took the silicate tailings and started up the glassworks. By the time excavation was complete, she was ready to line the basement with glass wool and set up rebar to support the domes.

The miners were fascinated when she pulled out one of the 3D printers and began laying in polymer/dust foam. The foam insulated the basement floor and walls, locking the rebar in place. They were running out of daylight when Ellie switched over to using ninety percent regolith to make the stronger surface walls. She barely got the double wall structure started, sealing in the airlock and the utilities block before dinner time. The rest of the dome would have to wait.

While Ellie was working on the ag dome, Tom was working on getting power. The ship had come in on a small nuclear thermal rocket. The reactor made two hundred megawatts of thermal energy. But for electricity, they used a thermocouple that only managed two hundred kilowatts. That was plenty for running the ship in transit, but not enough for operations on Mars. Tom and Petey had stayed in the ship, rooting through the gear to find the generator.

“Have a squiz at this, mate,” Petey said, grinning as he hauled out a large crate. “They reckon this little beauty can pump out a hundred megawatts of spark if you give it some welly. Got a fair few pipes and bits and bobs with it. Reckon we can piece it together, or are we dreamin’?”

Tom’s eyes widened. He opened the crate and started inspecting it. Simple design, built to interface with the General Atomics reactor. He just needed to send supercritical *CO₂* through the pipes instead of hydrogen.

The system needed forty tons of liquid *CO₂* for the working fluid. Tom pulled out his slate and did the math. Callum’s big twin screw compressor could make the *CO₂* in two days. He set the pressure relief valve to eighty bar to drain off the nitrogen and other unwanted gases. Tom started the motor, then checked with Petey. “You think we can get this together in two days?”

“Rockets aren’t exactly built to be pulled apart, are they? But once we’ve yanked that nozzle out, I reckon we’ll be right as rain. I’ll get the jacks and blocks sorted and get stuck in straight away.”

Tom helped him set up the blocks and jacks on a couple of sleds. As soon as the nozzle was supported, Petey worked his way up into the nozzle connection and rows of nuts. His pneumatic wrench strained with each one. 

Tom was impressed with how quickly he got to work. “Need a hand?”

“Bit of a squeeze in here for the both of us, mate, but if you can worm your way over to the hydrogen tank connection, you can turn the thrust shutoff valve and disconnect the reactor intake pipe. Once that’s done, we can get this show on the road.”

Tom looked at where he needed to get to and thought about how much better Mira would be at getting there. He squared his shoulders, grabbed a nut driver, and started winding his way in.

By evening, Tom had the tank shut off, and the reactor intake pipe was connected to the turbine return pipe. Petey called out as he got the last bolt off the nozzle. Together, they lowered the bell until it cleared the hot pipe and trucked it out from under the ship. They slid the turbine into the space. With no nozzle, this was no longer a rocket; it was a power plant.

Tom and Ellie ate with the miners on the ship that night. 

At dinner, Ellie began updating people on construction. “We have finished the subgrade work. Tomorrow, the dome should go together pretty easily. We have one more night with the smell of lithium grease and recycled air. Tomorrow we’ll move into the dome.”

Tom’s fork stopped mid-motion. “You’ll finish tomorrow?”

“Just the structure, and air. Pipes, wires, and furnishing have not begun.”

“Well done. Me, I don’t have good news. Just work. I need a one-acre section of bedrock scraped clean. All the regolith piled up near the ship. And I need a dozen six-inch holes about four feet deep. Does anyone here know how to do that?”

Luca gave a bit of a sheepish grin. “I’m your man for that, then. The regolith’s only about five feet deep in these parts. No worries, mate. I’ll have it knocked over in a couple of hours.”

“Hours? Perfect. That space will be our primary heat sink. The reactor makes the *CO₂* hot, but I need something to make it cold.”

The next day went very smoothly. Ellie and Angus worked on the inner dome first, while Luca cleared away regolith and Petey drilled the holes. Tom followed Petey, using a three-foot wrench to twist each heat sink, expanding the diameter and locking them into the bedrock. He set up the spider connections to and from the heat sinks, and piped the return back to the impeller. Tom and Petey worked together to attach the intake of the turbine where the nozzle had been. When everything was in place, Petey slowly opened the valve to feed *CO₂* into the turbine until the liquid reached the fill line on the sight glass, about halfway up. Tom opened the intake valve coming from the reactor and hit the starter. The system bogged, trying to move the liquid up into the reactor, but after a minute, there was no liquid, just a hot, supercritical fluid, putting out real power for Bounty.

When Luca came back with the excavator, dome construction sped up. He could keep the hoppers of both printers full as well as dump regolith in the space between the walls. By lunch, the dome was sealed. Ellie started compressing the air inside it and set the recycler to keep the *CO₂* under a thousand ppm.

Callum and Pacha arrived just as they were all sitting down to lunch. Pacha brought all the cavies into the ship and opened the air valves on their flats. 

“Those guys hate the flats. I’ll let them breathe regular air again.”

Ellie looked around the ship. “I am not sure this is any improvement, Pacha, but you do you.”

Angus looked at Callum. “Hey, we finished the dome. Want to check it out?”

Callum looked back at Ellie. “Is that true, lass?”

“We have to wait for air. The recycler is running hard, but the *CO₂* level is still too high. When we have enough oxygen in there, a green light will come up on the utilities block. Give it another hour. Just so you understand, the dome walls are done, but it is not yet plumbed or fully wired.”

Callum smiled. “When it’s ready, let’s unload all Pacha’s stuff to the dome, and you can give me a quick tour before I go.” 

After lunch, they brought a couple of sleds over to the gyroplane and started unloading his terra pretta, plants, seeds, and two baggies of dirt. When Callum saw those, he looked over at Pacha, but Pacha said nothing.

They brought it all back to the dome, but the light wasn’t green yet.

Ellie took the opportunity to remind them of the rules. “Now, listen. Since it is not plumbed yet, for the next few days, if you need to go to the bathroom, you will need to use the head on the ship. I’ll get the plumbing sorted soon, and then we can use the restrooms in the dome.”

They nodded. Petey looked baffled. “So, if I need to hit the loo in the night, you want me to chuck on my pressure suit, head through the airlock, stroll over to the ship, go through its airlock, pull off my pressure suit, hit the loo, then put the pressure suit back on, head through the ship’s airlock again, come back to the dome, go through the dome’s airlock, take off my pressure suit again, and then finally head to bed? I’d totally miss brekkie doing all that\!”

“Yeah, mates, no worries. I’ve got a better idea,” Angus whispered.

When the green light finally came on, they all helped move Pacha’s gear inside. Pacha set his cavies loose in the dome and began setting up soil regions for the plants. Everyone moved their own gear, too. They brought cots, blankets, and a few buckets with clothes and personal items.

This time, he was planting a sunflower relative called goldeneye. “It’s good for the soil biome,” he said, looking meaningfully at Callum. 

Callum couldn’t stay. After a brief look at the dome, he was back in the air, heading down to pick up Jakob and Mira.

Ellie continued, bringing Pacha downstairs. He was glad to have someone to talk to. “Ellie, I missed you guys. Magazine Beach feels empty, now. Callum landed while I was asleep. I hope I got everything. It was so dark the whole ride up here, so I couldn’t see anything. It was like our transit to Mars. Just stars and blackness. Callum just got in the air and went straight to sleep. I didn’t even have anyone to talk to. But it was dawn before we got close to Olympus Mons. It is so huge. The scarp is just everything when you come in. Sorry if I am talking too much.”

Ellie laughed. “Well, I’m glad you’re here. What do you think of the vertical garden space? The basement gives us more flexibility.”

“Hey, Ellie, this dome is totally beautiful. Exactly what I need. Thanks,” he gushed. “This wall will be so much easier on my back. Love it.” 

Ellie showed him his quarters. “I also set up a private bedroom down here for you, when the upstairs is overrun with plants and livestock.”

“Perf, Ellie. You always were looking out for me. But I gotta know, what about the greenhouses? For five of us, we just needed a few acres, but if I am feeding hundreds, which sounds like the plan, I am going to need a lot more space.”

“Yes. I am aware. I’ve laid out plans for a greenhouse district east of here, a ten-by-ten grid of ten-acre structures. We aren’t planning to build them all right away. One at a time. But we’ll get you all the space you need.”

Pacha looked at the plans on Ellie’s slate. “That’s cool. That is space for crops, pastures for cavies, and land where I can grow moss and lichen just to build soil. Fantastic.”

That night, dinner included cavy meat. This was the first meat the miners had had since they left Earth. “Cheers, Magazine Beach\! Meat, vegetables, and bread. I haven’t eaten this well since we left Earth\!” Petey called out, to a round of cheers from the other miners. 

Pacha did a fake bow. The teams were all exhausted. After dinner, they talked a little before turning in. 

In the middle of the night, Ellie woke to the sound of water spraying onto metal. It made no sense. She hadn’t attached a water supply to the dome yet; it was completely dry. She sat up, alarmed. Then the pungent odor hit her. “Miners are going to be interesting to live with,” she thought, and went back to sleep.

A few hours later, Ellie heard the sound again. “Miners,” Ellie thought and rolled her eyes. As she listened, the footsteps came back over by where she was sleeping. “Pacha. What are you doing?” she whispered. “I expect that from the miners, but not you\!” 

“Ellie, it’s so convenient,” Pacha replied, sheepishly. “I was about to suit up when I realized they’re right.”

Ellie didn’t fight it, but when she woke the next morning, she pulled him aside. “I am not sleeping in a room that smells like an outhouse. You and I are completing the sewage system now.”

Pacha nodded without argument. They gathered their tools and began the work. Ellie showed him how to heat the glass stock and draw it over the mandrels to make the tubes they needed. Once he understood the technique, she let him handle most of the forming while she assembled the anaerobic digester, the photobioreactor where they grew algae, and the distiller that gave them back pure water. Pacha used the dry minerals left by distillation as soil amendments. The system they built was four times larger than what Ellie had built at Magazine Beach, with capacity for twenty people. With Pacha’s help, the ag dome bathroom was operational by noon.

That same morning, Angus got on their cargo trike and rode out to the southwest slope and then up to the shield. He set an anchor ten yards back from the edge and tied himself off, then set another one a yard away and laid a polymer trough over the edge. Angus looked out, almost three miles above Bounty.

Petey was watching him through binoculars. Local Band couldn’t reach that far, so they were on FM. “Angus, Petey here. We see you on the edge. Are you tied off? Over.”

Angus pulled the jacketed spectra line from the cargo bike. “Tied off and solid, Petey. The view’s a ripper. Volcanic chaos to flat sand. I’m ready to send down the cable. You lot ready? Over.”

Angus started feeding the cable down slowly, then realized he could never hold the weight. After a couple of hundred yards, it pulled out of his hands. He called out, “Cable released. It’s running\! Take cover\! Over.”

The last two hundred yards hit the talus apron hard. Petey and Luca anchored the base. Angus attached the electric seat and took the first ride down the cable. After that, they could move easily up and down the scarp. They spent most of the day at the mine site, coming back down before dinner with rock chips and core samples from the mining site. The miners were talking to each other.

“Looks like that vent shrivelled while it was still pulling out beryllium. We have both Bertrandite and Phenakite.”

“Two minerals? That’ll complicate the processing. Did ya give the gangue an assay, then?”

“Bang-on, mate. I checked that first thing. A few iron-rich bits and some feldspar. The feldspar will need to be knocked out chemically.”

“No wazza. Callum was dead set on that during the trip, wasn’t he? Reckon he’ll be wrapped.”

During dinner, Petey was talking to Tom. “Look, mate, you’ve got all this power now. I need to start making steel bits. You mind if I wind up the power? I have an FFC Cambridge refiner that can drink the whole hundred megawatts if I let her, and we need the steel.”

“Sure, knock yourself out. We aren’t doing that much right now. Just the glassworks and the Sabatier machine.”

To make a cableway, they needed metal, both for the towers and the cable. They had planned for that and set up their FFC Cambridge refiner, using a magnet to pull iron ore from the regolith. After an hour, they had a large pot of sponge iron. They alloyed in carbon from the Bosch recyclers and made simple steel. 

Ellie’s next concern was equipment maintenance. Cleaning was impossible in the wind, on the regolith that was causing trouble in the first place. Electrostatic attraction meant there was always more dust coming in. She knew they needed a spacious building to use as a garage. She planned to add more later, but the first one was built toward the cableway. No airlocks, just oversized doors in and out. It had thin walls supported by glass rebar. The garage gave them a place to work, sheltered from the sun and dust, well lit, with a clean floor and tools handy. The digger and rovers could be kept there when not in use, out of the dust and sun. She moved three sleds in, too.

With the garage in place, Ellie started defining Bounty. All traffic would go in and out through the garage. She began marking the borders of the settlement. She used a pole to draw a line around five acres. The ag dome was the center. There was also space for apartment domes, the sewer system, a metal shop, and glassworks. The ship and generator were a few hundred yards outside the border, and the Sabatier machine was kept still farther away because Ellie deemed it too dangerous. She then built a small wall along the boundary, a foot high and a couple of inches wide. Not straight, it looked like corrugated tin on edge. This made it stiffer and allowed her to save material. When the entire wall had been built, she got Tom and Pacha to help her pull out rolls of *LDPE* sheeting. They used tape from the ship to quickly assemble a bubble cover while she anchored it on the wall using fitted caps. 

When it was ready, she called everyone over to watch. “Are you guys ready to see Bounty become your home?” she asked, as she hit the air pump switch.

Slowly, the bubble inflated. No real pressure, less than a millibar, but enough to give it shape. They would still wear pressure suits inside, but there was now a space with no dust. Pacha cheered, and Tom gave a thumbs up. The miners loved their home.

Polymer accumulated slowly. Pacha understood that, but he didn’t like it. When they had enough for the base, he and Ellie went down to where the greenhouses would go. They were not as fast moving dirt as the miners, but they excavated the first greenhouse site down to bedrock. Ellie laid down an inch of foam and set the glass rebar. Pacha started the hard work of converting regolith into soil. Using the miners’ ten-ton ball mill, he added five tons of regolith and one ton of precious ice. He pumped up the pressure to three psi and began tumbling. As the ice tumbled, it melted and washed out the perchlorates and rounded the grains of regolith into something more like sand and clay. As it processed, he would regularly draw out half the water and vacuum distill it to leave the perchlorate dust behind, recycling the clean water back into the mill. When the water ran clean, he pulled out the sand and laid it in the middle of the base. The Herculean task was going to take several days, but he didn’t stop until Ellie came by and told him it was dinner time.

He and Ellie set to work preparing dinner. Jakob and Mira should have arrived by now. Radio would only give them a five or ten-minute notice of their arrival. Outside, the temperature in the covered yard was dropping to the night’s cold, but inside, the smells of the night’s meal were intoxicating. The miners had never eaten oca. Pacha was roasting it with herbs and algal oil, and it smelled intoxicating. Just before dinner began, Callum arrived with Jakob and Mira, bringing one ton of frozen food, another ton of ice, and a small pressure box. Before anyone even said hi, Mira blurted out in a singsong, mocking tone, “Hey, Pacha, you forgot one of your cavies.”

Pacha was beside himself with guilt and quickly took charge of the animal, soothing it and giving it some amaranth leaves to nibble, then returning it to the herd. 

Mira and Jakob were pulling off their gear, hanging up their outer suits and pressure suits. Mira sighed when she got her thermal regulator off. As she began removing the base layer, Ellie jumped up and nudged her. All the miners were staring. “Uh, Mira, we have guests. Just step behind that screen.” Mira and Jakob went behind a changing screen to change into soft clothes and slippers. It was the first privacy they had had since the transit to Mars. It felt awkward.

Callum entered. He had gone back to the ship to get a bottle. It was nothing special, but it loosened the dust in their throats.

Jakob began. “It was bittersweet closing up Magazine Beach. Mira and I stacked four hundred tons of ice in the ice shed. We couldn’t fit another snowball in there. When we were packing, Mira heard a squeak, and that’s how she found the missing cavy. After that, we turned the place upside down to make sure there weren’t any more.”

Mira cut in. “Pacha, we were thorough. Really, it was just the one.”

Pacha smiled, though he was still upset he had left one. 

Jakob continued, “We pulled out all the frozen meat and vegetables. I could not believe it added up to a full ton. We also got a ton of ice and waited for Callum. I left the hydrolox generator running for future trips. The days are getting short, so it might take two or three days to generate a full load, but it will let us go down again to get ice or pick up anything we left behind.”

Callum nodded. “Yeah, we need more ice. No question. The thing is, it takes all day. I hope to get in another three trips, though. We really need the water.”

Jakob nodded. “Thanks, Callum. I’d like to pull a lot more than that, but the trek is too long for that.” He continued with his story. “It felt bittersweet turning off the recycler and the heater. Once we lifted off, I looked down, watching the words ‘Magazine Beach’ fade in the distance as we headed north.” 

Mira jumped in, excited. “You guys flew at night. Jakob and I flew during the day. Mars isn’t as interesting or varied as Earth, but it was still fascinating to watch the features go by. And coming up to Olympus Mons was incredible. It reaches out forever and up to space.”

Jakob finished his story. “When we came in, I saw the bubble and their enormous ship, so I knew where we were. But, as we landed, I saw the sign ‘Bounty.’ That touched me. We walked in through the garage, where everything was so tidy and organized, then came into the bubble and saw your huge ag dome. This is really something, Ellie. It’s going to be hard to go to Planum Boreum.”

Ellie nodded. She had built exactly what was needed. Leaving would be hard.

It had been a long time since the Magazine Beach crew all sat together. Ellie brought everyone up to speed. “We have a lot to build. We have food to last for maybe three months. We can extend that with algae, but, as always, we need to get Pacha what he needs.”

Jakob nodded. “Of course. Food is always number one.”

Ellie paused a moment, then continued, “I would say maybe, because we have another shortage. Water. The plan has always been that we need to move up and set up a second ice camp on Planum Boreum. I am not sure how long we can stay before we move.”

Tom sat with a slate propped against his tray. “We slow-shipped six months’ supply on the old contract. If we don’t have new ice established in those six months, there will be a crisis on Ceres. Planum Boreum has a huge ice cap and is closer than the Magazine Beach, but it is still a five-hour flight. We have time to work this out, but we need to use that time to establish a new ice supply.”

Ellie’s face clouded. “I need to build them an ice shed before we leave.” She turned away before they could see her eyes wet.

“Hey, mates,” Callum said. “You lot looking for water? Surface ice is nice and all, but as an old miner, I can tell you Mars has loads of buried ice. The Medusae Fossae Formation’s got as much as you could ever want. Problem is, it’s a fair bit south of here.”

Jakob sat up. He knew about the ice caps. Not this.

“Yeah, it can be miles thick. I have shallow radar. We can fly around with it and see if there are any good deposits.”

“That sounds like a lot of ice. I didn’t see any coming in.”

“Naw, it’s not on the surface. You need to dig down a ways. It’s like permafrost. I bet we can find some rich deposits. You game?”

Ellie started. “Jakob, this sounds promising. I told you guys not to get attached, but from the time I named this place Bounty, I have not wanted to leave.”

Jakob responded, “Look, we have time. Tom said we have six months. We can try some ideas. This ice sounds like a totally different beast, though. I understand saws. I don’t understand underground permafrost ice. And our sleds are meant to go a mile or two in a day. I don’t know if this can work, but I’ll check it out. Callum, can you help me scout out these deposits?”

“Nothing easier. Let’s get in the gyro at dawn and take a look, see if we find some.”

Jakob was satisfied they had a direction.

Callum tore a piece of bread and dipped it into the stew. He was eating real food again. Life was grand. Max had set him up with this crew that kept him fed and housed. But now he understood he would also need to work to help them.

For a moment, no one spoke. They could hear the soft hiss of the recycler in the corner, the low murmur of water moving through the new plumbing, and quiet chewing.
