# The Astrans

# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 7: Salty Metal**

| Price of *³He* | $6,500,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **75 tons/month** |
| **Current Consumption** | **70 tons/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **33 months** |

José Torres was glassing the sky at night when he saw the fireball. He cranked *Peces de la Luna* up to forty knots, watching it disappear beyond the horizon. Seven minutes later, a second fireball appeared.

He glanced at a picture of his kids above the wheel, riding their bicycles.

*Peces de la Luna* was built for a specific task: ten rapid deep-sea recovery operations at a time. The hull design was borrowed from a high-speed Italian ferry, the MDV 3000 Jupiter, allowing it to reach forty knots when fully laden. The ship was painted bright yellow, with a dark blue racing stripe, the fastest ship out of Lázaro Cárdenas. Tonight, it was prepped for eight recoveries.

A second fireball showed as the first one hit the water. The sound of the splashdown took eight minutes to arrive.

Lunar librations gave a one-hour launch window each month. Tseng had launched eight targets during the window, three days ago. As they arrived, the Earth's rotation was stretching those impacts into a thousand-mile line.

When the target struck the seabed, the thud played on the ship's loudspeakers. The acoustic analyst triangulated the signals, calculating precise coordinates. Precision didn't always mean accuracy. Five miles out, José called for the crew to shut off the engines. The sea around *Peces de la Luna* went still. He released a UUV, which swam a search spiral around the calculated impact site and found the target less than a thousand yards away.

José ordered a recovery glider prepared. An air compressor kicked on, inflating six support pylons on the fantail. When they were filled, the stern crane lowered the glider down onto them. The ropes, hawsers, and lift bag had all been packed in the tail with the recovery kit.

Once the glider was secured, a chute laid three hundred tons of construction rubble evenly across the ballast box. Liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen, each in corrugated thin-walled cans, insulated by syntactic foam, were strapped in place, completing the recovery kit.

José held off on launch. It took ten minutes to get a clear image from the UUV over the acoustic modem. When he was satisfied that the target's shackle — threaded through the eye Tseng had cast into the back of every bowl, for exactly this — was undamaged, he signaled for the drop. The pontoons deflated, and the glider slipped off sideways. In a few seconds, it was gone from view. He dropped an acoustic transponder buoy at the site so he could keep talking to the UUV, and *Peces de la Luna* sprinted to the next destination, two and a half hours away, to do it all over again.

The glider dove fast. Every minute spent in the water meant more heat transfer into the cryoliquids and more boil-off. As the pressure rose, the pressure-relief bubbles became more of a shimmer than individual gas pockets. In thirty minutes, the glider flared and settled beside the target, seventeen thousand feet below the waves.

The hawser off the lift bag was fourteen inches in diameter. The UUV guided the latching end to the shackle and locked it in place.

Secured, the lifting bag was spread over the hydrogen tank, and the valve was cracked open, letting gas vent slowly into the bag. At this pressure it was not so much a gas as a supercritical fluid, but it still rose in seawater. The seawater that touched the hydrogen flash-froze and sank to the bottom of the bag. As the bag took shape, it centered itself over the bowl. The feed rate increased, dumping tons of hydrogen into the bag. The UUV sprayed seawater into the bag at high pressure, warming it. Snow fell softly through the supercritical hydrogen, collecting at the base of the bag. This continued until the hydrogen had warmed to ambient, around 3°C. The bag was forty percent full, pulling five hundred tons.

The UUV fed oxygen in through a catalytic combustor, heating the hydrogen from abyssal cold to 400°C. The heat expanded the hydrogen until the bag was filled, reaching eleven hundred tons of buoyancy. The thousand-ton bowl weighed less than eight hundred in seawater, but the lift wasn't enough. Nothing moved.

José relayed another command to the UUV, trying to rock the bowl. It didn't budge. He had it circle instead, using its thrusters to blow the silt away.

Watching the UUV work, he looked at the size of the bowl on the screen. "A billion pesos for just that one, enough to keep the bank from seizing my company."

Forty minutes passed before the bowl shifted. The lift of the bag overcame the suction of the silt. A snap sounded over the ship's loudspeakers as the seal let go and the bowl lifted free. The crew on *Peces de la Luna* cheered.

The bowl rose fast, spilling gas from the base of the lift bag as pressure dropped. It fluttered, jerking the hawser violently. José had the UUV pull the vent line, reefing the base of the Kapton bag. At five hundred feet, the vent was reefed further, slowing the ascent to six inches per second.

When the bag breached, the bowl's momentum kept it rising, slackening the hawser. With no lifting force, gravity won. The bowl fell, snapped the hawser taut, and pulled the bag back under. It bobbed dangerously for a minute before hanging dead in the water. José made a note: *next time, slow to four inches per second.*

In two hours, *Pleamar* arrived, well deck already awash. Divers pulled a rubber donut under the bowl and inflated it until the rim rose above the waterline. The gear went up onto the dry half-deck, washed and fogged with deionized water. The ropes, hawsers, and lift bag were transferred into a freshwater soak tank with Salt-Away.

*Pleamar*'s heavy three-hundred-ton crane couldn't lift the bowl, but it had enough authority to position it precisely over a pedestal. Once in place, the donut was vented until the bowl made solid contact with the wooden shoes underneath.

Up close, the bowl looked older than its week of age — the ablative shield burned black and pitted, salt already drying white in every seam, a rime of frost sweating off cold metal into the tropical air. José ran a bare hand along the rim. A week ago it had been sitting on the Moon.

*Pleamar* purged its tanks. When the cargo deck was dry, they retrieved the donut, washed it, and stowed it for the next recovery. The bowl was rinsed with soap and deionized water. Recovery had taken four hours from arrival to having the bowl strapped down. *Pleamar* was underway again, headed to the second target.

After the last drop, José called Felipe. "Every bowl intact. A couple of shackles bent, nothing serious. How's the mill?"

"Mano, good to hear from you. I have the crew scrubbing walls and ceilings now. Surgical cleanroom by the time you're back. How far out are you?"

"Not enough fuel to sprint. We dropped to thirty knots. Two days."

"Deploy the grillage?"

"After I'm back. Cargo won't arrive for another six days."

"We'll scrub the dock anyway. Carnal, someone here wants to talk to you."

Felipe handed the phone to Maria. "José, the bank called again. They're starting foreclosure."

"It's going to be okay, vieja. Once this load sells, we'll have enough. They won't take the villa. Where are the kids?"

"I brought them to help Felipe clean the factory. Mateo's scrubbing the lunchroom floor. Works very hard for a ten-year-old man."

"And Sofía?"

"Sofía is why Mateo needs to scrub the lunchroom floor."

*Pleamar* picked up the five nearest bowls over the next two days, and *Bajamar* got the three farthest. José stayed on the phone with both captains through every recovery. There was no time for more than short naps on the trip back, and when he got in, there was more work waiting.

*Bajamar* made port first. José and Felipe had already laid out the plywood and two-inch steel-plate grillage. When *Bajamar* backed into its slot, they extended it to cover the ramp and the ship's deck. Then the SPMTs came like an army of ants, lifting the bowls off their pedestals and carrying them slowly to the fabrication room.

The alpha case was milled off under argon. The clean bowls were melted under vacuum and poured into ten-ton molds. The outer three millimeters of each ingot were milled away, remelted, and cast into industrial titanium. Each bowl took thirty hours. When it was done, the aerospace-grade titanium went onto different boxcars than the industrial grade. Felipe swept up the spatter and alpha-case swarf and sold it separately to ArcelorMittal.

José signed the shipping documents; the rest was CPKC Railroad's problem. One week until the next run.

Maria put the bills away when she heard him at the door. "José, we haven't seen you in so long."

He opened his arms, pulling her and the kids in together. "Maria, I'll be home more this week. What's for dinner?"

He fell asleep at the table. Maria and Mateo moved him to bed, where he slept sixteen hours straight. When he woke, the kids were at school and his wife was out for groceries. He sat in his chair, drank a Coke, and stared at the clouds.

The next day, *Pleamar* and *Bajamar* prepared to leave. He helped scrub the decks. Each ship took four hundred tons of MGO. When he paid the fourteen-million-peso fuel bill, he wished they ran on LNG. Or at least were painted more brightly.

Over the following days, the gear was inspected and prepped for the next operation. One hawser showed internal fraying under X-ray and was discarded. Two lifting bags needed patching, and one gas bag had torn and needed replacing. Every eye thimble had been hammered and needed swapping out. The marine supply shop behind his mill had woven all his bags and ropes in the first place; they were happy to handle the repairs.

The crew was loading *Peces de la Luna* when José got a call from Elin. "You sent me seven thousand tons of aerospace-grade titanium and seven hundred tons of industrial grade. Metallurgy's report is back. The quality is outstanding. I can pay seventy dollars a kilo for the aerospace grade, thirty for the rest."

José did the math in his head — dollars from Elin, converted back to pesos at the company's books. Twenty percent would come to $1.8 billion pesos. Suddenly his mill's two-hundred-million-peso debt didn't seem so bad. "Yes, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am."

"Do you plan on sending more?"

"I'd like to. I'm about to go pick some up."

"I'm behind on my targets, and your titanium is helping. You run a steel mill. Do you know how to fabricate high-purity titanium sheet?"

"Sure," he lied.

"If you could send me twelve-gauge sheet, I'd pay double. I can send you the specs. It would really help."

"Of course, señora."

"Thank you, José."

"De nada."

He called Felipe. "I just got off the phone with Dr. Elin Sarai."

"From Biloxi? She bought it?"

"She loved it. Money's already in the account. She wants more."

"Then she'll get more. You're shipping out tonight, right?"

"Yes, but she wants sheet metal too. Double pay for twelve gauge. Can we do that?"

"I'm on it, carnal. You go get the next load."

José put to sea with twelve hours to the first deployment. He glassed the sky. Nothing yet.

He was in the mess hall when Li called. "I saw the deposit. Four hundred million dollars."

"Yes. How's that sit with you?"

"That's fifty million a bowl, my cut. We just launched ten more."

"Tan, she said if I can roll it into sheets, she'll pay double."

"Whatever you want to do is fine with me. But if you botch the sheets and Elin won't buy them, I still expect my five hundred million."

"Yes, ma'am. I've been wondering — why me? There are bigger names in metal and shipping. Acero del Pacífico is small fry."

"Your location is convenient. I could have asked ArcelorMittal instead, and I'm sure they'd have agreed. It would be one of their most important product lines. I'd sit in the front row. That's nice. But with you, I don't sit in the front row. I sit alone. I told you I wanted ships, a new dock, cogeneration, ready in a year. ArcelorMittal would agree, and there would be analyses and meetings, and two years later they still wouldn't be done. I lent you the money. You did the impossible. I told you I wanted to get paid, regardless of any other problem. You said, 'Yes, ma'am.' I trust you — not because I know you, or because you're trustworthy, but because you have no choice. That takes the stress out of it, for me."

"I understand. But Tan, you lent me ten billion pesos for the ships and the dock. It's so much."

"The loan is real, and I expect to be paid back. But don't hurt your business to do it. When you're comfortable, let Elin route me part of your share. The first months are hard. It won't be hard forever."

The run had ten targets. A couple of shackles bent, as before, but this time one sheared off completely, and the bowl was unrecoverable — for now. José called on the way back. "Felipe, I'm coming in. The third bowl's unrecoverable. Shackle sheared clean off."

"Tan's going to take that out of our cut."

"Not if I get it back. I don't have a plan yet. Set me up with two packs and extra hawsers."

"If you're shipping out again as soon as you're in, that means I'm handling the offload and processing myself this time."

"You okay with that, mano? You only need *Bajamar*'s load. *Pleamar*'s holding station for the rescue."

"I'll take care of it. Truck's already going to Liquide Air for the oxygen. I'll have two hydrogen containers and the rest of the gear ready when you get here."

"Gracias, carnal. Two days out."

*Pleamar* had held station for three days by the time José got back to the lost bowl. The captain had already sent two UUVs down to search for the shackle and found nothing. José dropped a fresh UUV and the first glider.

Once they were down, he had them wrap a long hawser around the bowl and cinch it tight with a mechanical cinch. When the lift bag started pulling, he saw the problem. The bowl had been settling since it hit bottom. Another week and it would have been buried in silt. The stiction force was beyond anything he'd planned for. He kept the lift bag hot at eleven hundred tons of buoyancy while he sent down the second glider.

This time he used a net of one-inch rope. Two UUVs pulled it under the bowl, working it through the silt. Progress was imperceptible. José had them saw it back and forth. Still slow, but visible now, a few inches at a stroke. Four hours later, the net was secured. He connected it to the second lift bag and put in just enough hydrogen for tension. The net could only hold about two hundred tons safely.

The first bag was the new problem. It was losing lift fast. The first oxygen tank was empty, the hydrogen was cooling and slowly permeating the Kapton inner bag. The second glider still had cryogens, but its syntactic insulation was built for a fifteen-minute dive, not four hours of loitering.

José put another five tons of hydrogen in the second bag without heating it, then moved what was left of the first tank's hydrogen over and used the oxygen to heat that instead. While it warmed and expanded, the UUVs roped the two gliders and the empty cryotanks to the net so nothing would drift off and get lost.

When the temperature hit three hundred degrees, less than a thousand tons of lift, José thought he saw the bowl move. There was no pop. He asked the crew if they saw it too. While they were still deciding, it sped up. No pop — the bowl was rising.

The net had broken the stiction. Pulling through had opened a hundred channels for water to flood in behind the bowl as it lifted from the mud. Everyone cheered. The *Pleamar* captain blew the foghorn. José loved that, and answered with *Peces de la Luna*'s dual-tone horn.

Because of the noise, he almost missed it. A metallic crashing sound rose with the bowl. The UUV showed the cause: the two gliders were smashing into each other and into the cryotanks, now full of nothing but water, banging like drums.

He reefed the hem of the first bag, but by then the hydrogen in the second had warmed to ambient and, as the pressure dropped, expanded. A loud pop. The UUV flew under and showed him a snapped link in the net. He was past the safety margins. He gave the order to reef the second bag, but as it started up, more segments popped, then it went like tearing fabric. The second bag was now lifting a torn, empty net and a tangle of gear. It shot up like a rocket, slammed into the first bag, and forced it to burp its lifting gas. One of the gliders hooked the first bag and dragged it up a ways before letting go. The gear on the second net was going far too fast to be stable, kiting back and forth, smashing into itself.

It hurt to watch the equipment tear itself apart, but all of it could be replaced for a million pesos. The bowl was worth nearly a billion. He tried to keep it in perspective.

The screen showed rate of rise. The bowl had good momentum and the lift bag was still pulling, but the numbers were dropping. Twenty meters a second, then fifteen, then ten. If it stopped rising, there was nothing more he could do for it. As long as it kept climbing, the gas kept expanding and the lift kept building. He didn't know what speed meant failure. He was just watching.

The second bag breached. His crew hauled it and the wrecked gear up onto *Peces de la Luna*'s deck. José kept his eyes on the rise rate, which had dropped below a meter a second. He remembered he'd need to slow it more than that before it breached. If the problem was buoyancy, he was fine. If it was momentum —

At seven thousand feet, the number climbed back to a meter a second. He let his breath out and realized he hadn't taken one in over a minute.

The rise kept accelerating, but José kept it reefed tight — no extra strain on the gear. *Peces de la Luna* and *Pleamar* held a half-mile clearance for the surface. This time *Peces de la Luna* took the lift bag and hawsers, and once the bowl was secured on *Pleamar*, both crews gathered on deck for a minute before turning for home.

José called Felipe on the way back. "We got the bowl."

"Fantastic. How'd it go?"

"Smooth and easy. Sort of."

"José, you need to talk to Tseng. He can fix this."

"It wasn't a problem. I just had to go out again."

"Carnal, this was a problem. Talk to them."

He called Li, Darrin, and Tseng together. "On the first run, a few bent shackles. Didn't worry about it. But this time one sheared off completely, probably on a sideways splashdown. Never found the piece."

"How bad was it?" Tseng asked.

"We got it back. Took an extra week."

"You lost one of my bowls?" Li asked.

"No, ma'am. Recovered it. It's on a pedestal now, heading to port."

"Then what's the problem?"

"Felipe's been saying it for a while, and I agree. We need something sturdier."

Darrin was already sketching on his slate. "Seen this before, North Sea. A shackle holds fine in port. At sea, a crossed shackle is a lot stronger."

Tseng agreed. "Of course. Next shipment, cross shackles, heavier bar stock."

"Gracias. Tseng, send Felipe the geometry — he'll cast the connector hardware himself. What I've got now is too weak anyway."

When José got back, Felipe had already processed *Bajamar*'s five bowls. They found a minute to talk.

"José, the sheet metal isn't simple. I've ordered the equipment, but it's not only equipment. We need more staff."

"Of course."

"The men we need are specialists. Harder to place. And it can't happen in this building. High-purity sheet needs its own space. I made an offer on the marine supply shop behind us. I think they'll take it. Then a new building, and a line run over to it."

"I like that supply shop. They make our gear."

"They still will. Just from a new storefront."

"I wanted sheet metal this month, or next month at the worst. Now it sounds like six."

"No, carnal. I won't leave you waiting six months. But you need to know the constraints."

"Gracias."

"How's Maria?"

"Bills to pay and nothing to pay them with. And I'm gone more than ever."

"It's not easy."

"How's Lupita?"

"Doing well. Speaking of Lupita — I need you to come to dinner at her father's house."

"Of course. Bring Maria?"

"Yes. And the kids."

"The kids too? Special occasion?"

"La petición de mano."

"¡Felicidades, carnal! Ya era hora, ¿no?"

"Sí. Should have done it a year ago. But, mano, I need to show you something."

They walked back to the office.

"When we processed last month's load, we ended up with a pile of alpha-case millings and copper-contaminated swarf."

"We were pretty efficient. How much was there?"

"Less than half a percent. Still thirty tons."

"What did you do with it?"

"Talked to a buyer at ArcelorMittal. They want titanium — Elin's buying it all up. They gave me a finder's fee off the books."

"How much are we talking about?"

"See those two duffel bags?"

José opened one. Mostly five-hundred and two-hundred peso notes. "Well done, carnal."

"Bring one to Maria, see if it brightens her day. Mine's enough to start a family, I think."

José ate dinner at home that night. "Maria, work isn't going to get easier. But last month's load sold, and Acero del Pacífico is solvent."

Maria looked up at him, eyes wet. "José, they set a date. We pay in a week or they force us out."

He dragged the duffel bag out and set it beside her. "This is for you. Enough to keep the villa."

She opened it, cried, and hugged him. "We still miss you."

He fell asleep in front of the TV.

The next day, his secretary stopped him at the door. "José, you have a visitor."

"Can he wait until after lunch?"

"Not this one. I already sent him into your office. He's been there half an hour. You'll want to see him."

"Marcia, we've talked about this. No one goes into my office unannounced."

"I think this was the right time to make an exception."

José walked in. A tall, blond man stood at the window, looking out over the Acero del Pacífico docks. "Buenos días, señor."

The man turned. Tobias Raines. José dropped his coffee.

"I was just admiring your dock. You import steel from Asia?"

"Yes. That's the business. Some small fabrication too."

"I talked to Dr. Sarai last night. She mentioned you'd just sent her over fifteen thousand tons of titanium. Nice work for a steel importer."

"Thank you, sir. Elin's been very generous. I hope to do more for her."

"I hope so too. But I'm curious. Where's it coming from? I'm looking at your fleet. Marine salvage?"

"Yes, señor. Salvage. I find the titanium on the ocean floor."

Raines glanced away. "Most of the money goes to an offshore account, and the rest to Acero del Pacífico's account at Banorte."

"Yes. I only get a portion."

"That offshore account — I know it. I buy *³He* through it. The most prolific miner on the Moon. I never knew the name behind it."

"It's not a man. A woman. Tan. She drops high-purity titanium on the ocean floor, I pick it up. She gets eighty percent of ingot fair value, I cover expenses and losses, and keep the rest."

José walked him through the details — Tseng's bowl design, the recovery process, the whole chain — then took him out on a tour of *Peces de la Luna*.

"Why LNG?"

"Six hundred tons lighter than MGO, which helps. Cuts engine maintenance too. But mostly it's half the price. This is a steel mill — I already had the gas mains. That's also why I have a cogen plant and a hydrogen reformer."

"Rubble ballast?"

"Three hundred tons a recovery. I wanted it cheap. I have a barge collecting construction rubble — people pay me to take it off their hands."

"Doesn't that wreck your hull?"

"I bed it in silt from the channel dredges. Works fine. I rake it out when I'm back in port."

"You've built a very nice system." Raines studied him. "What would you think about expanding the operation?"

"Well, señor, *Peces de la Luna* is built for ten recoveries. I could run more, but Tseng only launches once a month."

"What if it wasn't Tseng launching? And the metal wasn't titanium? Tungsten. Tantalum. The metals Earth's running out of."

"I'm ready for any metal, if it follows Tseng's design."

Something in Raines's face said he already knew otherwise.

"I'm not ready to ship yet. Won't be for a couple of years, at least. But when I am, we can do good business — if you don't mind growing."

"Yes, sir. If I need to expand the fleet, can you give me something in writing? Something I can take to a bank?"

"We can do that." Raines put out his hand. "We can do a lot better than that."

They shook on it, and Raines walked back down the dock toward his car, already on his phone before he reached it. José stood at the window a while longer, looking out at *Peces de la Luna* riding light and empty at her berth, and thought about what a couple of years might mean for Mateo and Sofía.
