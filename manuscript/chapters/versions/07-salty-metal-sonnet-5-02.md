# The Astrans

# Part I The Spark

## **Chapter 7: Salty Metal**

| Price of *³He* | $7,500,000/kg |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Current Production** | **35 tons/month** |
| **Current Consumption** | **32 tons/year** |
| **Time Since Start** | **24 months** |

José Torres was glassing the night sky when the fireball came down. He cranked *Peces de la Luna* up to forty knots and watched the streak vanish below the horizon. Seven minutes later, a second one lit the clouds behind it.

He glanced at the photo taped above the wheel, his kids on their bicycles, and put both hands back on the controls.

*Peces de la Luna* was built for one job: ten rapid deep-sea recoveries, back to back, no wasted hours. Her hull came off the lines of an Italian ferry, the *MDV 3000 Jupiter*, and at forty knots laden she was the fastest thing working out of Lázaro Cárdenas. Bright yellow, a dark blue stripe down each side, so nobody mistook her for a fishing boat. Tonight she was loaded for eight.

A third streak fell as the first one hit the water. The sound took eight minutes to arrive, a soft thud rolling across open ocean.

Lunar librations gave Tseng a one-hour window each month to launch. Three days ago he'd put ten targets on that window. Now Earth's rotation was stretching the impacts into a line a thousand miles long, and José's job was to be under each one before the sea swallowed it.

The acoustic array triangulated the splashdown, calling out coordinates that were precise and not necessarily accurate. Five miles out, José cut the engines. The deck went still. He dropped a UUV, and it swam a spiral out from the calculated point until it found the target, less than a thousand yards off.

"Prep the glider," he said.

The compressor kicked on, filling six pylons bolted to the fantail. When they were taut, the stern crane lowered the glider onto them. Ropes, hawser, lift bag — all of it packed into the glider's tail with the rest of the recovery kit. A chute dumped three hundred tons of construction rubble evenly across the ballast box, and the crew strapped down the cryogen cans, liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, wrapped in corrugated foil and syntactic foam.

José held the launch. It took ten minutes to get a clean image off the UUV, relayed through the acoustic modem, grainy and slow. He wanted to see the shackle before he committed a bowl-shaped hole in the ocean to it. When he was satisfied, he gave the signal. The pylons deflated, and the glider slid off sideways and was gone.

He dropped a transponder buoy to keep talking to the UUV, and *Peces de la Luna* sprinted for the next site, two and a half hours out, to do it all again.

***

The glider fell fast, and every minute in the water cost heat, boiling a little more of the cryogens away. Pressure built as it dropped, until the relief bubbles stopped looking like bubbles at all and started shimmering like heat off a road. Thirty minutes down, the glider flared and settled beside the target, seventeen thousand feet under the surface.

The hawser was fourteen inches through, thick as a man's thigh. The UUV walked the latching end across the seabed and locked it into the shackle. José watched the feed, waiting for the green light that meant it had taken.

The lift bag spread out over the hydrogen tank. He cracked the valve, and gas started venting in, slow. At that pressure it wasn't gas so much as a supercritical fluid, dense enough to rise through seawater anyway. Where it touched the water it flash-froze a skin of ice. The ice sank straight through it. As the bag filled, it centered itself over the bowl. José opened the valve wider, dumping tons of hydrogen a minute. The UUV sprayed seawater into the bag at pressure, warming the gas from the inside. Snow fell soundlessly through the supercritical cloud, piling at the base like the world's coldest blizzard. It kept warming until it reached the ambient three degrees. Forty percent full. Five hundred tons of lift.

He fed oxygen through a catalytic combustor next, and the hydrogen jumped from abyssal cold to four hundred degrees. It expanded, filling the bag until the gauge read eleven hundred tons of buoyancy — more than enough to lift a bowl that weighed eight hundred in seawater.

Nothing moved.

José watched the screen a long moment, then had the UUV rock the bowl, gentle at first, then harder. Nothing. He circled the thrusters around the base instead. Silt rose in slow clouds, drifted, resettled. It never quite cleared.

"Been sitting a while," he said, to no one. On the screen, the bowl looked exactly the size it was: a billion pesos of aerospace titanium, enough to make the bank stop calling. Enough to keep Maria in the villa. He watched the silt swirl and thought about the balance on the loan and made himself stop thinking about it.

Forty minutes in, the bowl shifted. A snap came over the loudspeaker, loud enough that half the crew flinched, and the suction let go all at once. The bowl came up off the bottom, and the crew on deck cheered before José had time to feel relieved.

It didn't stay smooth. As the bag rose, the pressure around it fell and the gas inside kept expanding. It vented from the base in bursts, jerking the whole assembly on the hawser. Two hundred feet up, the bag surged sideways. For a second the readout showed the shackle taking a load past anything Tseng had rated it for. José watched the number climb. Hold. Drop back. Only then did he let his breath out.

"Reef the vent," he told the UUV. At five hundred feet he had it reef further, cutting the ascent to six inches a second, patient, controlled.

Then the bag broached, and the bowl's own momentum kept carrying it up after the bag had already gone slack. For a few seconds there was no lift at all. Just eight hundred tons of gravity, winning. The hawser snapped taut, hard enough to yank the bag back under. The whole rig bobbed like that, ugly and violent, for the better part of a minute before it finally hung dead in the water, exhausted.

José exhaled and made a note on his slate. *Next time, four inches a second, not six.*

Two hours later, *Pleamar* arrived, well deck already awash. Divers wrestled a rubber donut under the bowl and inflated it until the rim cleared the waterline. The crew hauled the whole assembly onto the dry half of the deck, fogged it with deionized water, and dropped the ropes and lift bag into a freshwater soak tank laced with Salt-Away. *Pleamar's* crane didn't have the muscle to lift the bowl outright. It had the precision to walk it sideways onto a pedestal instead. The donut deflated, and the bowl settled onto its wooden shoes with a groan that carried through the hull.

They purged the tanks, rinsed the bowl with soap and deionized water, stowed the donut for the next one. Four hours, splashdown to secured. *Pleamar* was already turning for the second target before the wash water had finished draining off the deck.

José called Felipe once the last drop was down for the night. "Every bowl intact. A couple of bent shackles, nothing worse. How's the mill?"

"Mano, good to hear you. I've got the crew scrubbing walls and ceiling right now. Surgical cleanroom by the time you're back. How far out?"

"Not enough fuel to sprint. We're at thirty knots. Two days."

"Deploy the grillage?"

"After I'm back. Cargo's still six days out."

"We'll scrub the dock anyway." A pause, and José could hear Felipe covering the mic before he came back. "Carnal, I've got someone here who wants you."

Maria's voice replaced his. "José. The bank called again. They're starting foreclosure."

"It's going to be fine, vieja. Once this load sells, we'll have enough. They are not taking the villa. Where are the kids?"

"Here with me, helping Felipe scrub the lunchroom. Mateo works very hard for a ten-year-old man."

"And Sofía?"

"Sofía is why Mateo has to scrub the lunchroom floor."

***

*Pleamar* took the five nearest bowls over the next two days; *Bajamar* the three farthest. José stayed on the line with both captains through every recovery, catching sleep in twenty-minute pieces between calls. When he finally made port, there was no rest waiting, only more to do.

Felipe had the plywood and two-inch steel grillage laid out before *Bajamar* even backed into her slip. They extended it to cover the ramp and the ship's deck, and then the SPMTs came in like a column of ants, lifting bowls off their pedestals and walking them, slow and level, into the fabrication hall.

The alpha case came off under argon. What was left went into the vacuum furnace, melted clean, poured into ten-ton molds. The outer skin of each ingot, three millimeters of contamination, was milled away, remelted, and cast again as plain industrial titanium — nothing wasted, just sorted into what would fly and what would weld a hull. Thirty hours a bowl. Felipe swept the spatter and the swarf into drums and sold it to ArcelorMittal on the side.

José signed the shipping manifests. After that it was the CPKC Railroad's problem.

Maria was putting away bills when she heard him at the door. She didn't get up right away.

"José, we haven't seen you."

He opened his arms, gathering her and the kids into one motion. "I'll be home more this week. What's for dinner?"

He fell asleep at the table before he found out. Maria and Mateo walked him to bed between them, and he slept sixteen hours straight, waking to an empty house, kids at school, Maria out for groceries. He sat in his chair with a Coke and watched the clouds move for a while, not thinking about anything.

***

The crew was loading *Peces de la Luna* for the next run when Elin called.

"You sent seven thousand tons of aerospace-grade and seven hundred industrial," she said, no preamble. "Metallurgy's report is in. The quality's outstanding. Seventy dollars a kilogram for the aerospace grade. Thirty for the rest."

José did the math twice before he trusted it. Twenty percent of that was more than the mill's entire debt.

"Yes, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am."

"Will you be sending more?"

"I'd like to. I'm about to go get some."

"Good, because I'm behind my targets and your titanium is helping more than you know. You run a steel mill — do you know how to fabricate high-purity sheet?"

"Sure," he lied.

"If you can send twelve-gauge, I'll pay double. I'll send you the specs."

"Of course, Señora."

"Thank you, José."

"De nada."

He called Felipe before the ship cleared the breakwater. "Elin bought it. Loved it. She wants more, and she wants us rolling sheet — twelve gauge, double the price."

"I'm on it, carnal. You go get the next load."

José put to sea with twelve hours to the first drop. He glassed the sky and found nothing yet.

He was in the mess when Li called. "I saw the deposit. Four hundred million."

"Yes, ma'am. How's that landing for you?"

"Fifty million a bowl, my cut. We just launched ten more." A pause, unhurried, the kind she never wasted. "I've been wondering. Why me? There are bigger names in metal and shipping. Acero del Pacífico's small fry."

"Your location was convenient. I could've asked ArcelorMittal. They'd have said yes. Then meetings, and analyses, and two years of nothing to show for it. I'd sit in the front row for that. With you, I don't sit in the front row. I sit alone. I told you what I wanted — ships, a dock, cogeneration, done in a year. You did the impossible. I trust you, José. Not because I know you. Because you have no choice. That's easier, for me."

"I understand. But you've lent me ten billion pesos for the ships and the dock. It's a great deal of money."

"The loan is real. I expect it back. But don't hurt your business getting there. When you're comfortable, let Elin route part of your share to me. The first months are hard. They will not stay hard forever."

***

The next run had ten targets. On the third, a shackle sheared clean through where it should have only bent — one seam of weld doing the work of two, and the load found it. José spent an extra week finding the bowl and lifting it by hand, a net instead of the shackle. Patient work. Careful work. When it finally came free and rose true, he stood on deck a long time before he trusted it enough to look away.

He called Tseng that night. "The shackles are too weak for a sideways splashdown. This is the second one that's failed."

"How badly?" Tseng asked.

"Recoverable. But it cost me a week and nearly cost me the bowl."

Darrin was on the line too, tracing something on his slate. "Seen the same failure mode in the North Sea. A straight shackle works fine dockside. At sea, under shock load, you want it crossed. Much stronger."

"Of course," Tseng said. "Next shipment, crossed shackles and heavier bar stock."

"Gracias. Send Felipe the geometry — he'll cast the connector hardware himself. What I've got now is too weak anyway."

When José made port, Felipe had already processed *Bajamar's* load without him, five bowls down while José was still fighting the sixth.

"How's Maria?" Felipe asked, once the numbers were settled.

"Bills, and nothing to pay them with, and I'm gone more than ever."

"It's not easy."

"No." José looked at the mill floor, clean now, humming. "But last month's load sold. Acero del Pacífico is solvent." He didn't say the rest — that solvent didn't mean safe, not yet, not with the bank still calling.

***

His secretary caught him in the hallway two mornings later. "José, you have a visitor. I put him in your office."

"Marcia, we've talked about this."

"I know. This was the right time to make an exception."

The man in his office was tall, blond, and looking out the window at the docks like he already owned the view. José recognized him before the man turned around, and his coffee hit the floor.

"Buenos días, Señor Raines."

Tobias Raines turned, unbothered, hands in his pockets. "I was just admiring your dock. You import steel from Asia."

"Yes. And some fabrication."

"I talked to Dr. Sarai last night. She mentioned you'd sent her over fifteen thousand tons of titanium. Not bad, for a steel importer."

"Elin's been generous. I hope to send her more."

"I hope you do. I'm curious where it's coming from, though. I've been looking at your fleet." Raines nodded toward the window. "That's marine salvage out there, not steel hauling."

"Salvage, yes. I find it on the ocean floor."

Raines's eyes stayed on him a beat too long. "Most of the money from your account routes offshore before it ever touches Banorte."

"I only keep a portion."

"That offshore account. I know it — I buy *³He* through it. The most productive miner on the Moon, and I've never had a name for her."

"It's not a him. Tan. She drops high-purity titanium into the Pacific, I pick it up. Eighty percent of fair value goes to her, I cover expenses and losses, keep the rest."

José walked him through it — Tseng's bowl geometry, the splashdown math, the recovery kit, all of it — and then took him down to the dock for the tour.

"LNG," Raines said, looking at the fuel manifold. "Why?"

"Six hundred tons lighter than marine gas oil, laden. Cuts my engine maintenance. The fuel itself is half the price. I already had gas mains — this is a steel mill. That's also why I run a cogen plant and a hydrogen reformer."

"The ballast. Construction rubble?"

"Three hundred tons a recovery. Cheap. I've got a barge that collects it — people pay me to take it away."

"Doesn't that wreck the hull?"

"I bed it in dredge silt. Rake it out when I get back. Works fine."

Raines walked the length of the fantail, taking in the pylons, the crane, the coiled hawser thick as a man's leg. "You've built something good here. What would you think about expanding it?"

"*Peces de la Luna's* built for ten a run. I could go out more often, but Tseng only launches once a month."

"What if it wasn't Tseng launching? And what if the metal wasn't titanium?"

"I'm ready for any metal that comes in Tseng's shape."

Raines said nothing to that, and something in his face told José the shape wouldn't be the same at all.

"I'm not ready to ship," Raines said. "Not for a couple of years, at least. But when I am, we can do good business — if you don't mind growing."

"If I need to expand the fleet, can you give me paper a bank will accept?"

"We can do better than that."

They shook hands on the dock, the crane idling behind them, the tide going out.
