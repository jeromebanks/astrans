Catherine Dahl was watching the Seine when the croissants arrived. Le Puits Perdu made them from amaranth now, Bolivian amaranth, the kind that only ever reached her table because the restaurant held its own reserve back from the export orders. She broke one open anyway. It never tasted like anything but Mars.

Her slate lit with an incoming call, a restaurant number in Baracoa. She didn't recognize it and let it ring through to voicemail.

"Miss Dahl, this is Terrence Halden. I need help. Elin suggested I call you."

She called Elin instead of the number he'd left.

"Hi, Catherine. How are you doing?"

"Doing well. Ceres is ahead of projections. But I just had a message from a man named Halden, and he says it was your idea."

"You've seen the news? They're doing it. Building the split rigs."

"I heard. Launching every twenty minutes, they tell me. Nothing like it's ever happened."

"They'd be launching more if I hadn't talked Halden into giving the CNT contract to Lafevre. Mars has a mass driver that can reach Callisto now. The whole thing is working, Catherine. What it isn't doing is staffing. They need a couple hundred people willing to go to Jupiter and run rigs that JRI itself isn't sure will survive."

"And you think I can recruit them."

"Halden can't. If no one signs on, the project fails before it starts. I hoped you might."

After the call, Catherine looked up the job ads herself.

*Job opportunities on Jupiter. Chemical engineers preferred. Pay DOE.*

"He does need help," she thought.

She took the Gulfstream out of Orly. Somewhere over the Atlantic her slate lit again. "Hello, Catherine Dahl speaking."

"Miss Dahl, I am so glad to reach you."

"Why are you calling me from a restaurant?"

"That's just how we do things around here. Bastos doesn't like us talking to Raines' people."

"I don't hide. I'm on my way to Baracoa. I'll be there by three. You, Bastos, and I will talk then."

She ate on the flight and landed at 2:30. Halden met her on the tarmac with a JRC car, and it was obvious someone had chewed him out recently and thoroughly. "Our meeting with Bastos is at four. She may not be in a great mood."

"Perfect. There's my bag. Take me to the hotel — I'd like to freshen up."

Jet lag didn't touch her. By four she was sharp and ready.

Bastos welcomed them in. The marina view from her office had a calming effect on most visitors. Catherine started talking before she took a seat.

"This project can't succeed. You thought you needed to solve the engineering problem. All the engineering in the world won't help you if you can't hire."

"Halden is perfectly capable of hiring—" Bastos started.

Catherine cut her off, projecting the job ad onto the wall. "No, he's not. How many have you hired so far? This ad screams incompetence."

"I've really just gotten started, Miss Dahl. I'm trying a few different ideas. The first split rig isn't even complete yet."

"Halden, I respect your work at Punta de Maisí, and how you wrangled this design out of JRI. But right now you're making excuses because you don't know what to do. Let me lay this out. You need trainers, a training facility, a program, and — mostly — people willing to risk their lives. The trip to Jupiter takes a year right now, so you need to be sending people a year before the first rig is finished. I'll ask again. Are you ready?"

"No," he whispered.

"If he's a little slow to hire, that's not a crisis," Bastos said. "This is JRC. We've hired a thousand people. I can put him in touch with HR and start filling the reqs. Who are you to tell us how to run our business?"

"How many of the people you hired knew there was a good chance they'd be killed? How many agreed to live isolated from friends and family for up to a decade? The project is failing, and I came to help. If you'd rather do this yourselves, I'll see myself out." Catherine shut off the projector, stood, and walked out.

Halden turned to Bastos. "Please, Amira. You and I deal in bureaucracy — managers reporting to managers, someone making spreadsheets. She runs mining operations. It's not the same thing."

"She disrespected you."

"She told the truth."

"Fine. Run after her. You hire her. I don't like her."

"But you know we need her."

Bastos looked pained. "Yes. We need her. Go get her."

***

Halden found her in her hotel room. "You impressed Bastos. She wants to hire you."

"I'd hate to see what unimpressed looks like."

"I could tell you. She would have been sweet the whole time, pitying you. She wouldn't have told me to go hire you."

"All right. If we work together, I bill corp-to-corp, and I pull one percent of the gross for as long as my hires are up there. I take the lead on hiring, and I build the training facilities and the curriculum."

"Corp-to-corp is fine. The percent will need board approval."

"It's necessary. I want our goals aligned. If I'm paid on how effective the team is, it changes my mindset. And you need board approval either way — I need to be able to make decisions without begging Bastos for one every time."

***

A week later Catherine was back in Bastos's office, and Bastos was in rare form. "You asked for a percent. Outrageous — but I got it for you. I called in favors with the board and they agreed." She threw up a projection: the salary range for Jovian workers. "One million dollars base for an engineer? That's more than I make."

"I notice you haven't signed up," Catherine said. "We need top engineers. People who are fastidious and flexible. Those two traits don't often travel together, and such people are already expensive before you ship them five hundred million miles to work on machines that aren't safe."

Bastos killed the projection. Catherine tapped the blank wall where it had been.

"And if they die, that salary keeps going to their beneficiaries for twenty years. You talk about the number like it's going to hurt your bottom line, but you spent more than that in the last hour on launches. Each split rig is half a trillion dollars, and you're begrudging the people who run it their pay. You need a few hundred bodies to operate the entire fleet. Pay them."

***

The new ads got attention. Catherine offered paid internships in place of interviews. Halden, Dr. Savic, and Dr. Patel volunteered to sort the applications for her.

"I asked JRC's HR to filter the resumes and confirm everyone is who they claim to be. That's all they check," Catherine told the three of them. "I need you to pick people who are technically sharp and work well on teams."

"So we each score a resume and pass it along, and you take the highest totals?" Halden asked.

"Don't try to make it fair or perfect. Pick up two resumes at a time and choose the better one. Do that enough times and you'll know what you're looking for. I need three hundred from each of you."

***

She went to see Dr. Ji-Wu Kim next, in his basement office at the Institute — austere, functional, and rarely visited. Bastos had warned him she was coming.

"Dr. Kim, I need space. I'm bringing in five hundred applicants for screening and orientation before I send the finalists on to training."

"I want to help. We have lecture halls, but nowhere near enough beds for five hundred."

"I don't need beds here. I need classrooms, a physical, and two days to run them through basic screening — health, aptitude, whether they flinch when I raise my voice. After that, they're someone else's problem for six months."

Kim considered it. "Why here, and not just fly them straight to training?"

"Because half of them will wash out before they're worth the airfare. I built a camp years ago for a different client, and it's already standing. No sense letting it sit empty. But your people will spot the ones worth sending there faster than a JRC recruiter ever will."

"You're building a filter, not a school."

"That's the idea."

***

Six hundred showed up over three weeks, culled from the scored resumes. Kim's staff ran physicals and aptitude batteries in the lecture halls while Halden, Savic, and Patel worked the stack behind them, two resumes at a time, best of two, over and over, until they had their three hundred apiece. Catherine merged the lists herself, cutting duplicates and anyone Kim's doctors had flagged. Five hundred and change cleared screening and boarded two C-130J-30s in Baracoa, the same worn cargo birds Catherine had used once before for a different rock.

They refueled at Dutch Harbor. The plane parked on an incline, nose two feet higher than the tail. A firehose came up to the front door; the rear ramp opened onto a blast of cold that turned the whole interior to fog. Catherine walked the aisle. "Feet up." Seawater sluiced down the deck and out the back until it ran clear.

An hour past the Kuril Islands they went into a toboggan maneuver, the plane pitching down and speeding up to pace a tanker for a mid-air fuel transfer. Sixty thousand pounds went across in twenty minutes. Stomachs went with it.

They landed on April first, during the Karaburan, wind over forty miles an hour and the sun a pale disk behind blowing dust. To five hundred people who'd just spent a day in a windowless cargo hold, it looked like heaven. They hauled their duffels to the barracks, a low, circular building of rammed earth that Catherine had built for a different company on a different rock, six years and one continent ago.

Day one, in the old training hall, Catherine gave the same speech she'd given the first class. "I'm looking for a hundred people to go to Jupiter, with a reserve behind them. A couple of you may end up as trainers for the next group. For the rest, thank you for coming out. While you're here you will become an expert with every piece of gear we use up there. If you can't master it, a truck takes you back to town and JRC flies you home. Four of your classmates didn't enjoy the flight in and have already left."

Rows of survey lasers, dummy mass specs, and anchor cams sat on the tables in front of them. Beyond the hall, mockups filled a canopied hangar the size of a jet bay: an elevator car on rails, a dirigible gondola with its envelope draped beside it like a shed skin, and a segment of the space platform itself, ten replicas of each.

Every morning began with drills. Klaxon, breathing gear, get to the elevator. Same drill, flashing light instead of a klaxon. Same drill, something deliberately wrong with the mockup. During lectures, instructors hit them with tear gas without warning — grab the mask, get it on, find the door.

Theory was rigorous and the failures were not gentle. Early tests came with warnings. After two months, an F came with a bus ticket home and severance.

Some days the class went down to Punta de Maisí to watch the Babbal Tube work, a rocket emerging from the tube every twenty minutes with a thud that carried five miles and barely a sound from the rocket itself. "It was built for heavier payloads," an instructor told them. "They lightened the rockets instead and push them at ten g out of the tube. Mach five and max Q on exit. Fine for resin. It would kill a person or shatter delicate equipment." They watched the descenders too — twin parafoils threading a suspended net with a precision that looked scripted, the heat shield base coming off after to hand over whatever had ridden it down, usually nothing more delicate than a canister of *³He*.

The class thinned from five hundred to three hundred, then, in the final week of drills, to two hundred. On graduation day, Catherine kept it short. "All of you have done exceptionally well. A few of you will stay on as instructors for the next class. The rest of your files go to Dr. Knox on Ceres. He'll make the final call on who ships to Jupiter."

They partied through the night. In the morning, two hundred people made their way to Punta de Maisí. It took Knox two weeks to answer, reviewing scores and interview footage across the light delay, sending his verdict back in a single message: a hundred to Jupiter, a hundred home, no explanation beyond the numbers next to each name.

Catherine read the list once, then called Halden. "Your hundred are ready. Get me a launch window."

The rocket held twenty at a time. Every thirty minutes from eight to twelve-thirty, another group went into the tube, bound for the LEO hub and the six-month burn to Callisto.

Halden watched the last group go from the observation rail, Liora two steps behind him with her slate. He said nothing about the note he'd left on the table in Jamal, and neither did anyone else.

Catherine found him there afterward. "You'll need another five hundred behind these, and soon. This was the easy batch."

"I know."

"Then get started." She turned and walked back toward the hangar, already thinking about the next six hundred resumes.
