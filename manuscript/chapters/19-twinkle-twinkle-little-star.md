Max Juno was the first to hear about it. Sitting in his Lagos office in the newly completed tower, he was on a routine call with a contact at Shibata Capital in Tokyo. They drifted through the usual topics: metals contracts, new alloys, logistics, until a small aside broke the rhythm.

“There’s a reactor program. Kagutsuchi Vortex. Early runs show stability at two, maybe three gigawatts. Machine about the size of a city bus. They claim it is very stable.”

Max noted the words, asked no follow-up, and let the talk slide back into routine. Only after the call ended did he allow the weight of it to settle. Small fusion, if real, could be very disruptive of the fusion industry Helion Dynamics was building.

He composed a message to Tobias Raines:

 “From a source at Shibata Capital. New reactor: Kagutsuchi Vortex. Two to three gigawatts. 20m footprint. Early data promising. Sharing because you should know.”

He hesitated over the last sentence, aware it revealed more than he liked. After all these years he still did not know whether Raines’ driving aim was to remain the richest man alive or to see fusion carried into every corner of civilization. 

In Geneva, Raines read the note at his desk overlooking Lake Geneva. He pressed a button and gave three instructions without pause.

 “Get Max Juno on the line. Clear my schedule. Ready the jet for Tokyo.”

Max Juno was still in his office when Raines’ call came through. He excused himself smoothly and answered the call.

“Hello, this is Max speaking.”

“Max, how soon can you get to the airport? A jet is waiting for you there in Lagos. We’re meeting them in person.”

Max hesitated, just for a moment. “You’re sure?”

“I’m sure,” Raines said, and in that certainty there was something larger than business. something closer to faith. “You found this. You belong in the room.”

The line went dead, leaving Max staring at the polished surface of the conference table, his own reflection looking back at him, eyes sharpened by the weight of what was coming.

Max was already in a limo when he called his Shibata Capital liaison. “Secure a slot at your headquarters. Tonight. Raines is coming personally. He wants Kagutsuchi Vortex present; CEO and chief engineer, no substitutions.”

The liaison swallowed hard, bowed reflexively though it was over the phone, and set to work.

Raines next called Dr. Elin Sarai. “Elin, listen, I am going to need your technical expertise. Have you heard of Max Juno?”

“Max Juno. Maybe. Is he the guy who financed the Mars operation?”

“The same. Smart man. You’ll like him. He and I will meet you in Biloxi. We’re going to Japan.”

They converged in Biloxi, where Sarai’s manufacturing plant dominated the Back Bay shoreline. She was already waiting on the tarmac, helmet under her arm, coveralls marked with graphite dust from the shop floor.

Raines stepped down first. She studied his expression before speaking.

 “Tobias, what is it?”

“Another fusion plant,” he said simply. “Smaller than ours. If the reports are true, it’s quite amazing. We are going to visit the company that made it.”

She had brought her crash bag, the things she needed if she was suddenly called away on a business trip. Together they boarded the jet bound for Tokyo. Max filled in the details he had. 

In her excitement, Elin’s words spilled out fast. “They have a self sustaining stellarator? That’s amazing. We must see it.” As the flight continued, Elin gave an impromptu lecture on the technology, bringing Max and Raines up to speed on what to look for when they arrived. “Stellarators are generally more stable than a tokamak, but none have been able to generate the confinement needed to sustain fusion.”

“We sell tokaplexes, not tokamaks. Why did you say ‘tokamak’?” Raines asked.

“The fusion core itself is a tokamak. It is also fairly small, though larger than the Kagutsuchi Vortex reactor. The process of using that and keeping it fed and stable and extracting electricity from it requires many more buildings and structures. That complex of buildings is our tokaplex. It is the turbines, cooling towers, emergency systems, and controls that take up so many square miles, not the reactor. 

Max sat back, unsettled, staring at the darkening horizon. He had delivered the message because Raines deserved to know. Max had made his first fortune selling lunar helium to Helion Dynamics. Raines had backed Magazine Beach when no one else would. The loyalty ran deep. But even now, uncertainty gnawed at Max. Would Raines see the Kagutsuchi Vortex as . threat or an opportunity? Would he crush them, absorb them, or enable them?

At 35,000 feet, Max spoke briefly with Shibata Capital’s liaison, confirming the attendance of Kagutsuchi Vortex’s two senior figures: Kaito Ishikawa, the reserved CEO, and Dr. Naomi Fujimoto, whose early work on vortex confinement had become a foundation for the field.

“They are honored to meet with you,” the liaison said with practiced formality. “And eager.”

“Good,” Max replied, ending the call without ceremony.

The Gulfstream touched down at Haneda under a velvet blue sky. A black sedan waited, its driver and attendant already in position. Elin stepped out after Raines, a small carrycase in her hand, posture sharp but restrained. She glanced at Max, as though reading uncertain data, withholding judgment.

Raines turned to Max, understanding his uncertainty. “Max, you did the right thing. If this is real, it changes everything. If it isn’t, we lose a day in Tokyo.”

The car carried them through Tokyo’s arteries of neon and glass before arriving at Kagutsuchi Vortex headquarters, a low, modernist complex on the edge of the bay. Security was discreet but thorough. Waiting at the entrance was Nakamura, the director from Shibata Capital. His bow was precise, his English polished.

“Welcome. Ishikawa-san and Dr. Fujimoto are inside.”

Nakamura led them into a large hall where the reactor dominated the space, coils gleaming under hard white light. Around it, consoles and scaffolding suggested both research and ongoing construction. By the machine stood two figures waiting for them.

Kaito Ishikawa, tall and angular, carried a stillness that suggested calculation as much as patience. His suit was flawless, his hands folded behind him.

Beside Ishikawa stood Dr. Naomi Fujimoto, slight and sharp-eyed, a slate in her hand. Her posture was alert rather than ceremonial, the stance of someone who lived between theory and machine. Her eyes were already on Elin, measuring.

As Raines, Elin, and Max entered, Ishikawa bowed lightly.

 “Welcome to Tokyo,” he said, voice calm and deliberate. “We have much to show you.”

Raines returned the bow, not as deeply, then gestured toward the machine dominating the hall.

 “Let’s see what you have built.”

They moved closer. The stellarator loomed, coils curving in elaborate arcs, a twisted cage of superconducting fields surrounding the core chamber. Technicians stood back as Fujimoto touched a console.

“The central challenge, as you know, is maintaining density and temperature while keeping the plasma stable. We have achieved this with a field geometry of our own design.”

Lights across the console shifted. Nothing changed in the room. No noise, no light, no vibration. Inside the stellarator, the plasma kindled, a deep blue glow held in its magnetic cage. The confinement lines shimmered faintly against the chamber walls. Elin watched with fascination as the needles on the reaction gauges moved up and remained steady.

“It is stable,” Fujimoto said. “We have sustained uninterrupted operation for seventy hours.”

Max raised an eyebrow at the figure. Raines showed no outward change, but his attention fixed.

“There is some waste,” Fujimoto continued. “Deuterium and *³He* near the boundary must be vented to maintain stability. We replenish continuously. The venting also removes *⁴He* ash.”

Elin leaned in slightly, studying the graphs that appeared on the console. As it continued, the outer shell of the stellarator began to glow dull red from residual heat.

“Our present limitation is not fusion,” Fujimoto said, her tone matter-of-fact. “It is recovery. Our steam cycle cannot absorb the generated energy. Turbines saturate, and over ninety percent of output is discarded.”

Elin spoke without hesitation.

 “Mechanical bottleneck. Not thermonuclear.”

“Correct,” Fujimoto answered, her eyes sharpening in acknowledgment.

While Ishikawa and Raines spoke quietly at the main table, Elin and Dr. Naomi Fujimoto stepped aside. The hum of the reactor hall softened behind them. A small cocktail table stood between them, forgotten by the others.

“Your machine is beautiful,” Elin said, her tone precise but sincere. “Can you make it larger?”

“There is a limitation in size,” Fujimoto explained. “At larger sizes, the field cannot contain the plasma once fusion begins. At smaller sizes, edge effects dominate, and ash sweeping removes too much reactant. We found this sweet spot at two gigawatts thermal.” 

“How do you keep heat from destroying your magnets?” Elin asked.

Fujimoto gave a small smile. “This project is less about fusion and more about materials and heat exchange. Ishikawa sees the small footprint and calls it revolutionary. He compares it to your facilities without realizing most of your acreage is turbines, cooling, and power distribution.” She paused, then continued in a quieter voice. “The real advances are in the walls. We use silicon carbide for both the first wall and the containment shell. Then a spiral of magnesium oxide, so helium can enter cold, absorb heat as it winds through, and exit hot enough for generation. We’ve reached outlet temperatures over twenty-five hundred degrees Celsius. Different ceramics might allow us to boost that temperature, if needed.”

Elin’s eyes sharpened at the detail. “That’s impressive. You know, electricity isn’t always the primary use-case. Offworld, they need steady process heat for smelting. Something like this could serve directly, without turbines at all.”

For a moment both women stood silent, each considering how the other’s work reframed her own. When they looked up again, it was with the recognition that they had learned something neither had walked in knowing.

Elin nodded. “What you have built here is phenomenal.”

Elin talked to Raines briefly afterwards, explaining her thoughts. There was a pause, a silence where fortunes are decided. Raines nodded once to Elin, then broke the silence, his voice deliberate, cutting cleanly across the room.

 “How scalable is production?”

Ishikawa lifted a hand. “I regret to say that development has exhausted our capital. We have no manufacturing facility. Even if we did, each reactor requires months of precision assembly. Hundreds would be impossible. We could perhaps produce a handful, each essentially handmade.”

Raines did not look away from Ishikawa.

 “You’re telling me you can’t produce a hundred. Fine. Can you produce ten, and can you commit to pushing output as far as you can take it?”

Ishikawa’s reply came without hesitation. “Yes. We would love to. As much as we can, as fast as we can, but we have no manufacturing facilities.”

“Then I have a proposal,” Raines said. “I’ll build you a facility in Gulfport, not far from Elin’s base in Biloxi. Helion Dynamics has infrastructure there already. And when you start producing, I will buy everything you can make.”

The room absorbed his words. For a moment, no one spoke. Then Nakamura exhaled slowly, the relief unmistakable. “He means to build with us,” he murmured to himself. “Not against us.”

Raines continued, “The Kagutsuchi Vortex will have many uses on Earth, particularly in remote areas, and we will help you find those markets. But right now the UN is blocking the export of fissile materials to space. The claim is safety, but the real goal is to stop fusion power. The reactors currently powering operations in space won’t need refueling for years, maybe a decade if they’re careful.”

Ishikawa’s eyes narrowed slightly, understanding.

“But your Kagutsuchi Vortex can replace those fission reactors and provide more power. Much more. That will expand lunar production, increase metal flow from Ceres. We need this technology, and we need it fast. These reactors aren’t banned, yet. If they’re in orbit before anyone moves to regulate them, we control space power. But I’d like this kept quiet until that happens. Agreed?”

Ishikawa’s composure cracked slightly, relief visible in his expression. “Of course. We would be honored to help, and we will keep this to ourselves.” He bowed, deeper than before. “We are very grateful for your faith in us.”

Raines then stood, ending the meeting with the same clarity with which he had begun it. “Good. Then we are aligned. Build. Scale. Deliver.”

The group rose. There were bows, brief handshakes, but no ceremony. The air carried the quiet charge of a compact sealed.

On the jet that night, Tokyo receded into a scatter of lights. Inside the cabin, the atmosphere had shifted. Raines sat with a drink untouched in his hand, silent. After a long pause, he turned to Max.

“This will affect you too. I want you scouting Martian ventures. Risk more. Even ventures that fail may help get the right expertise to Mars, and an entirely new venture may work. Mars is the natural core of operations off Earth.”

Max nodded once, already thinking.

The silence returned. Max glanced at Elin. “He just guaranteed the market before the machines even exist.” He paused, still processing. “I wasn’t sure what he’d do. He could have bought them out, buried the technology. Instead he’s building them a factory.”

“You thought he might crush them?” Elin asked.

“The thought crossed my mind,” Max admitted.

Elin’s tone was more contemplative than surprised. “He doesn’t see fusion as his. He sees it as humanity’s. He doesn’t want to own the light, just see it shine. The rest of us have to keep up.”

The engines hummed, steady and low, as the three of them sat with the weight of what had just begun.
