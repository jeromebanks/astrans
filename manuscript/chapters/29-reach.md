Darrin loaded up a rover. Extra batteries, food, water, and a *CO₂* cylinder. Li noticed him packing. “You’re packing the high-wheel? Planning on driving somewhere?” she asked.

“Not sure yet, but I am ready for it. I am getting new QR codes. These claims aren’t on our map. I don’t know where they are coming from. What I do know is they are sending their bags to Faraday Crater, which is at the easternmost edge of the cableway. I’m going there to take a look, see what happens to the bags. I am bringing the high-wheel because the terrain might be tough.”

She looked at his survey laser. Having it didn’t mean anything. He always had it. But it brought a question to mind. “Are you planning on stopping them?”

“Doubt it, but maybe. I want to see what is happening.”

Darrin scanned the QR code on the high-wheel rover and punched in the destination: Faraday Crater. He hooked up and was off. The uptake was smooth. The crater floor dropped away as he climbed over the rim.

The lunar highlands unfolded beneath him punctuated by bright crater scars, illuminated only by blue earthlight. The cable carried him at fifty miles per hour. Below he could see the mining camps, sweeping, bagging, sending the regolith to Tycho. Even though the power was capped, they could still sweep slowly straight through the lunar night.

An hour and a half out he passed Henderson & Associates, eight domes clustered around a central processing hub. Power lines gleamed on ceramic insulators. Nearly a dozen bags queued at their transfer station. 

Thirty minutes from Faraday Crater he noticed a rover loaded with pressure bottles. A lone figure was at the tower, filling each bottle with oxygen. Darkside deaths had ended under the cableway.

After four hours, he came to the end of his cableway. There he found another cable, lighter, extending further east. Darrin had some of the new bags with him. He hooked them up to see where they would go. The platform switched them onto the minor line. Darrin hooked up and followed them out. 

This line couldn’t handle the thirty ton bags that had become the standard under the cableway. But three ten-ton bags held just as much. Darrin kept following the empties another fifty miles. Here, there were no more lines. The bags just sat on the platform, waiting to be claimed.

Darrin dropped down and parked his high-wheel a ways away where it wouldn’t be seen, then walked back to watch. He buried himself in the regolith, leaving a gap so he could see the platform. He waited.

While he watched, other bags were coming to the end and staying. A man came up, hauling ten bags. He hooked them all on. Darrin pulled out his slate and took pictures of them. The QR code checked out. He knew that man. He stayed partly buried, watching the bags. 

Another man drove up in a crawler, hauling a trailer with twenty bags. As he hung them on the cableway, Darrin noticed immediately that the bags had different codes, and none of them were people he knew. He watched the man take the empty bags and a few packages that had come in, toss them in the trailer, and leave the way he came. 

As soon as he was over the horizon, Darrin got up and ran back to his crawler. He followed the crawler’s tracks. They were easy to see. There were ruts from them being followed so many times. Darrin kept his distance. If the man was paranoid, he’d be seen.

The driver stopped. Darrin kept his high-wheel out of sight and crept up, staying low. As he watched, the man got off the crawler and put everything in a very large cup, then sealed that with another cup. The cup was huge compare to the load.

The man rolled the cup about ten feet. Then backed off. He walked to a table and tapped it a few times. Without warning, the cup started moving fast, then flew into the sky. Darrin recorded it on his slate. The slate estimated where it would land.

Far from the cableway, there was no way to get a message back to Li. Darrin got back to his high-wheel. He drove five miles north, staying out of sight, then let the slate take over driving. It was heading three hundred miles away. Darrin set a stop point fifty miles from the destination. He pressurized the interior and took his helmet off. While the slate drove he boiled water and dropped in an algae biscuit. He ate it with a daily multivitamin. 

He couldn’t send it, but he compiled a note, anyway. “Li, found where the bags are coming from. Going to investigate.” 

Darrin was watching as he drove through. Camps looked different out here. Light were off, since it was night. No one had power to burn. He saw K bottles stacked next to buried huts, nothing happening. He remembered living through lunar night, the biocore turned low, trying to rest and breathe less.

His lights were the only ones on, other than the Earthshine. Darrin started to doze when he saw a man running. He took control of the rover and turned towards the man. Looking at his right hand on the steering wheel, he was surprised to see the survey laser.

The man was waving his arms and running toward Darrin. There was a heterodyne squeal when the man got in range. “Hi, stranger\! Can you help? My wife and I are going to darkside\!”

Darrin sped up, driving straight toward the man. He got his helmet back on, and recompressed the air into storage. “On the way. Just two of you? Is she still alive?”

“She is in her suit in the hut. Regolith cut a seal on the airlock. We are leaking air. I saw your lights. I’m sorry, but we don’t have any other hope.”

“No worries.” Darrin pulled up beside the man. “Get in.”

The stranger got in. “My name’s Bill. My wife is Helen.” 

“Plug in to the rover, and call your wife.”

Bill plugged his suit into the electrical outlet. “Helen, get out here. Do you see the lights? Come this way.” 

Darrin kept driving to the pile of regolith the man had pointed out. Buried, it just looked like a hill. Then he saw a woman come around the corner walking slowly towards the rover. 

“My air’s thin. I don’t have enough battery to recycle it.”

Darrin pulled to a stop next to her and got her inside. Bill plugged her in, and Darrin gave her suit a charge of *CO₂*. The recycler started processing it, releasing pure oxygen into her helmet. He put a new destination on his slate, Faraday Crater. He overrode the speed controls. When Bill and Helen had both recharged and brought their oxygen levels up, Darrin remembered to repressurized the rover. 

Three minutes later, they reached four psi. He took off his own helmet. “You can take your helmets off if you want. We’re OK in here. Are you hungry?”

They nodded in unison as they worked their helmets off. “My cup is dirty, but the water is hot.” 

He dropped another algae biscuit in the cup and added water. They used his fork to eat. “Since we couldn’t take our suits off, all we could eat was tube paste. We ran out days ago.”

“Help yourself to another biscuit.” Darrin pointed to a cabinet with twenty biscuits, and some flavor packets.

The high-wheel handled terrain smoothly, even at speed. After a couple hours, the Faraday Crater tower showed. Darrin’s slate chirped, surprising him. It had just sent his message to Li.

They pulled up next to the crater. “We need to suit up again. I’m going to open the door soon.” He hit the compressor while they were getting their helmets on. Sound disappeared, except over Local Band. “I am going to recharge and refill my oxygen tanks. What are you two going to do?”

“We can’t go back. Our hut is unlivable. I guess we just stay here at the tower, taking oxygen and power until dawn.”

“Come dawn, what are you going to do?”

There was a long pause. Helen answered. “I have no idea.”

“Have you heard of Tan’s Cantina?”

They nodded.

“That’s where you are going. She will feed you, and there is a hotel.”

“We can pay. We have twenty grams back at our hut.”

“You’re not at your hut. You stay on this line for four hours, then you will see the cantina. Tan will take care of you. Can you wash dishes?”

Darrin waited until his rover was fully charged and their suits were charged. He programmed their destination and scanned their QR codes. He sent a note to Li to expect them.

They hooked on and waved as they left.

Darrin reset his destination to the package’s expected impact site. Three hundred fifty miles. Three hundred to his stopping point. The rover started driving. He fell asleep. 

\*\*\*

Eight hours later, an alarm woke him. He had reached the stopping point. Darrin started driving himself towards the calculated impact point. Another one flashed overhead, coming toward him. His slate calculated the launch and impact points. The new calculations were with a half mile of his original calculations. He continued along slowly. The high-wheel was good for driving in rough areas, but not so good for hiding. Ten miles from the calculated site, he got out and started walking.

He walked about two miles when multiple spotlights lit up all around him. An open-top rover came up to him. The driver turned toward him. “You must be Darrin Brooks. We’ve been waiting for you. John Fletcher. I appreciate you making the trip. Get in.”

Darrin got in the rover. John continued. “My guy watched you over in Faraday Crater, Watched you leave, watched you come back, watched you leave again. You aren’t Tan, so he figured You must be Darrin Brooks.”

“How did he tell you? Messages in the containers?”

“No, we’ve got a fiber optic cable under the regolith. It’s for timing the launches, but it keeps us in touch. Do you want to see our setup?” 

“Sure.”

Fletcher drove over to the launch site. Four steel cups were nestled together. Another pair was out, and each half was partially loaded. The mass driver was a mile long. “This is Riccius, half a mile from an Odd Point Rendezvous site. Three hundred miles from Faraday Crater. Everyone within twenty miles of here trucks in their regolith, and I launch it to Faraday. The mass driver on that end catches it.”

“How can you be so precise?”

“It’s the moon. No coriolis force, no wind. So we can get pretty close. And the vessels have some controls.”

“Where do you get the power?”

“We need less than you might think. Launches use a lot, but landings are regenerative. We have solar panels and batteries, and since we don’t sweep, it’s easier. Still, we send less during lunar night.”

He took out his slate and showed a diagram. “I’m thinking of building my own cableway. We would start with a fifty miles radius, move up as demand and income allows. I have not decided on a cross or an asterisk. Either way, it will make it easier for them to get to rendezvous, and, more importantly, it will serve as a collection system for their regolith, which we send you.”

Darrin brought out his own slate, showing their design. “I found that a loo out here with a single line coming in from the loop is efficient. It makes the cableway next to your operations less complex. Then an asterisk spreading out from that. Miners regularly add infill lines. It has worked pretty well for us.”

“That looks nice. I will tell the team on Earth. For us, though, it is a question of ROI. All those decisions are made downstairs,” Fletcher said, pointing at Earth.

Fletcher drove to where the container was being loaded. “Quarter inch steel cups. Cost a fortune to ship these up from Earth. They sent me six pairs. Tough. When we were starting, I put a few of them into the pebble berm. They came out slightly dented, but fine. We hammered them out with sledgehammers.”

Darrin ran his hand along the steel. 

Fletcher brought him to a cup that was on its side, being loaded. “At each end is a guidance module. This small, tubular mass driver ejects regolith pellets at a thousand meters per second. The pellets are just sintered regolith. I put a ton in the front and in the back. They typically fire off a couple hundred pounds. The launch crushes them, so it is dust flying out. No matter. Probably safer. The pellets just make loading easier.”

“You see them loading in the bags. When both sides are full, they will join the cups and roll the vessel over to the launcher. My man on this side opens the vessel, takes the bags out and hangs them on your cableway. He picks up anything headed this way, such as empty bags, food, air, tools, parts, anything that can take high G forces. He then reseals the vessel and launches it back. Two-way flow of material. Regolith going toward you, material and supplies coming back.”

Darrin stared at Fletcher for a long moment. The concept was audacious. Ballistic cargo transport across three hundred miles of lunar surface but here it was, working. “Your mass driver must be amazing.”

“It’s great. Lets go look at the muzzle.” They got back in the rover and drove up to the end, which looked like a huge mound of pebbles.

“You can see our tube poking out through the hill there. It launches at a forty-five degree angle. You see that set of rings? They are packed with electromagnets allowing fine tuning on launch and final corrections on catch. The hill itself is pebbles. If a vessel misses, it runs straight into that pile, absorbing the energy quickly. The mass driver is only a mile long, and only starts curving to forty five degrees in the last third of the launch. Same on the other end.”

“Only a mile? That would need over twenty G. You would crush your cargo.”

“Twenty-five. We don’t ship cargo that would be crushed. Once a month we make the drive back and forth, carrying people and delicates.”

As they drove around the site, Darrin saw a sign.

			 *Send your regolith to Tycho*  
				 *30%*

“What’s that?”

“We never did any marketing or advertising except for those signs. We have six of them around here.”

“How is that working out?”

“Very well. We are making money and people keep bringing us their regolith.”

Darrin pulled up a chart showing mining growth under the cableway. “You can see it is growing everywhere. The fastest growth is under the six main lines. But lets just look at Faraday Crater.” He shifted the chart to show growth at that tower. “This includes the miners near Faraday Crater, those a little further out for whom Faraday is the closest tower, and you.”

“I can see the bump when we started shipping.”

“Me, too, but look at the growth. People near the tower are growing fast and are the wealthiest. Fifty percent growth since the tower was installed. Next are the people east of the crater that need to drive a long ways to get to it. Solid growth. Thirty percent. But look at the unknowns. Those are your people. Essentially flat.”

“I should advertise more.”

“Your problem isn’t awareness. Your customers aren’t thriving.”

“They’re doing fine. Churn is under ten percent.”

“Do you know why the miners who are leaving are doing so?”

“We are not worried about those low numbers. All businesses have churn.”

“Not all. We don’t. Let’s look at miner growth.” Darrin brought up a chart showing the change over time of productivity by QR code.

“Miners under the cableway are making money fast. They’re buying better equipment, eating better, working safer. Every month they produce more. Your customers aren’t making enough to upgrade. The churn you see is miners darksiding or getting so scared of darksiding they can’t stay.” 

“No one asked your opinion. We are doing alright.”

“When you send the regolith to Tycho, you asked my opinion. If we agree to keep processing these bags, it will be because we believe you are helping these miners thrive.”

Fletcher took a moment to breathe. He got his pulse back down. “Darrin, it’s not just about them. I have to thrive, too. We plan to extend this model all around your cableway and, eventually, all over the Moon. The more I make, the faster I can grow.”

“Your model can’t grow right now. You are hauling steel vessels up from Earth. You are trying to live on solar. And miners aren’t excited to work with you. Instead, you work with me.”

“What do you mean?”

“You need to build a cableway. I sell you what you need for that. Drop it off at Faraday Crater. I send you light titanium cable. Now you can make a cableway that doesn’t flex in the sun. You need vessels? I can stamp them. I can even run electric lines out here. I’ll charge you for it, but it will be steady power, day and night. My one requirement is you cut the miner’s fees to ten percent. Let them thrive, too. That is what I will agree to.”

“I’ll have to talk to the owners downstairs.”

“OK, you do that. And, when they sign, we will start processing your bags again.”

Fletcher jumped out of the rover. “Wait right here.” 

He turned and ran into an office. When he took his helmet off, he didn’t turn off Local Band. Darrin couldn’t hear everything, just the shouted parts. “Then wake him up\!” “He cut us off\! Zero revenue\!” “Ten Percent\!” “No Choice\!” The mumbling got fainter. 

Darrin sat in the rover for hours. He wrote a note to Li, including all the details while they were fresh. He knew the message would not get sent until he was under the cableway.

Two hours later, Fletcher returned. “The board was delighted with your ideas. They are very excited about the possibilities of our cooperation. I am authorized to sign a contract with your terms.”

“I am less interested in you signing a contract than I am with you following the terms. There aren’t any lawyers up here, anyway. Start by changing your signs. Next, go talk to the miners. Talk to one every day, get to know them and their needs.”

Fletcher brought Darrin back to the high-wheeler. Darrin set the destination to Faraday Crater and went back to sleep. He slept through the entire trip until his slate chirped. His message to Li had been sent. He hooked his rover to the line and slept four more hours until he arrived in Tycho. 

The terminal platform rose to meet him, friction dampers engaging smoothly. He unclipped and dropped onto familiar ground, feeling the vibration of the cableway system through his boots.

He cycled through the airlock, stripped off his pressure suit, and found Li in the cantina. “Welcome home, Darrin. Do you remember these two?” She pointed to Bill and Helen. “They didn’t just have a bad seal. All their equipment was cheap. I threw away their suits and sold them new ones. They could afford them. They had probably ten grams of *³He*.”

“Li, are you nuts? Those suits cost…”

Li looked at him sharply.

“I forget how much they cost. How much did you charge them?”

“A gram.”

“They look nice.”

“While you were off playing outside the cableway, Bill and Luke went back and pulled everything they had: helium, sweepers, solar panels, batteries, their rover, even their hut. Helen was a CPA before she came up here. She agreed to stay on. You know I need an accountant. Luke has Bill working on the ovens, for now. Point is, they agreed to stay.”

“I got your note. Thirty percent was too much. How did it go?” she asked without looking up.

“I got him down to ten percent.” Darrin pulled up a chair. “But we need to talk about our extraction fees.”

She looked up sharply. “What about my fee?”

“I told Fletcher we’d consider dropping to fifteen percent for his incoming cargo. From twenty.”

Li’s expression went flat. “You told him what?”

“Hear me out.”

“Fifteen percent? Darrin, do you understand what we provide?” She gestured at the window overlooking the extraction facility. “We maintain all of that. We staff all that. And now you want me to cut our fee by a quarter?”

“I know what we’ve built. But I also know what we haven’t built. Ninety-eight percent of the Moon is waiting for you. Bill and Helen? Luck. Darksides happen daily.”

“If they had stopped here first, I’d have checked their gear.”

“You can’t check everyone’s gear. But you can expand. Fletcher thinks he is making money in transportation. I think he is extending Tycho Crater across the Moon. Help him build it, for us.”

Li was quiet for a moment, studying the schematic. “We still lose five percent on extraction fees.”

“Do we?” Darrin zoomed out on the map, showing the full lunar sphere. “Right now we cover two percent of the Moon’s surface and generates sixty percent of lunar *³He*. The other ninety eight percent of the Moon? It’s just sitting there. Billions of tons of regolith that’s barely being mined.”

Li studied the map in silence. She heard him. This was huge. Still, she felt he hadn’t heard her. “Darrin, the Moon is not London or DC. We don’t have lawyers. We need simple agreements. Twenty percent is simple. I change that, everyone will start looking for a deal. My fee stands.”

Darrin started to object, but she held up a hand.

“But,” she continued, “I’ll rebate five percent to Fletcher on all regolith that comes through his network during the growth phase. He charges the miners ten percent for transport, gets five percent rebate from us on their extraction fee, we charge twenty percent. Net cost to the miners is still thirty percent, Fletcher makes fifteen percent during construction, and we keep our nominal rate.”

“You sure?”

“I’m sure.” She turned back to her workstation. “Now go get some sleep. You look exhausted.”

He started to protest, but she was right. The adrenaline was wearing off, leaving him hollow and tired.

“Wake me if anything comes up,” he said.

“Nothing’s going to come up. Go.”

He made his way to the hut, stripped off his clothes, and collapsed into the bed. Sleep came immediately.
