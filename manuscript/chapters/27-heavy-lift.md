Amira Bastos stood at the floor-to-ceiling windows of her corner office, watching the afternoon light play across Baracoa’s harbor. Behind her, Dr. Ji-Wu Kim settled into one of the leather chairs arranged around a small conference table. “Amira, you sounded upset when you called,” Kim said carefully.

Bastos turned from the window. “Did you see Raines’ interview in the Financial Times yesterday?”

Kim shook her head.

“I can tell you the board has. He was talking about the lunar *³He* project six years ago, how he solved it by offering loans and stable price guarantees, creating a rush of miners. Six months later, *³He* was flowing. Same with refractory metals. He funded asteroid mining operations, and within three years, asteroids were producing more tungsten than from all terrestrial mining combined.”

“Those were logistical problems,” Kim said quietly. “What we’re working on is fundamental.”

“Don’t tell me that, Ji-Wu. I have a board meeting in two weeks and I need to show them progress, not excuses. It’s no secret Raines wants new leadership at the JRC, and now he is making his case to the public.”

Kim was quiet for a moment, studying Bastos’s face. “Getting *³He* from Jupiter does not lend itself to quick solutions. I know just the group for you to talk to. The Heavy Lift group is tasked with improving the cost and cadence of launches from Earth to LEO. It is the most visible part of our charter.”

The next day, Kim brought Bastos to JRI. They went to a two-story structure labeled “Heavy Lift”. 

Kim brought her to visit one of the chief researchers. “Amira, may I introduce you to Dr. Marcus Webb, a propulsion specialist who joined us from SpaceX. He has been looking at a set of ideas that I think will suit your needs very well.”

Webb stood and shook Bastos’s hand. “A pleasure to meet you. Ji-Wu tells me you want to build a new launch facility.”

“The launch facility is Kim’s idea. I need something that makes a statement about the value of JRC,” said Bastos. “What is your plan?”

Webb turned to the whiteboard. “The fundamental problem with chemical rockets is that the fuel that accelerates the rocket also has to be accelerated. You need more fuel to accelerate that fuel, which adds to the weight, which, in turn, requires more fuel. This vicious cycle is called the rocket equation. Each increment in speed requires exponentially more fuel. While it looks grim, it has a bright side. Each decrement in speed requires exponentially less fuel. That is where we in the Heavy Lift group live.”

“What do you propose?” Bastos asked.

Webb began drawing towers, tubes, and dotted lines in sweeping arcs. “The system my team has been working on involves a six mile pneumatic tube supported by a titanium lattice. The tube accelerates the rocket to Mach 2.3 at the top, at which point a ramjet stage takes over, driving the rocket to Mach five at ninety thousand feet. The result is we move from three percent payload to twenty percent payload, eight times as much.”

Bastos looked blankly at Kim. 

“Marcus, that is a very nice system, but we are on a deadline. Tell us about needed research, construction time tables, and costs,” Kim said. “Your research is directed to finding the ideal system, but today what we need is expedience.”

“There is another system we can use, designed to minimize dynamic load. It still uses a pneumatic tube, but rises a half a mile. In six seconds, the rocket exits at Mach 0.75. It stays subsonic up to the stratosphere, allowing for a lighter rocket body. If we combine that with air augmentation, the system can launch twenty percent more payload from the same fuel.” 

“Twenty percent doesn’t sound so impressive.”

“This system allows a high cadence. The rocket goes into the launch tube, gets fueled, and launches. Six seconds later, you are ready to load another rocket. A typical launch pad requires weeks to months of inspection and reconditioning after every launch.”

Bastos made a note on her slate. Launch cadence had overwhelmed US and Chinese launch facilities. This could be sold to the board.

“Another advantage is reduced sonic loading. Takeoff typically has the engine noise reflecting off the ground into the payloads, which must be adapted to deal with that destructive force. With this, there is no launch noise. By the time the rockets are on, the ground is fading away fast. Tons of sound-absorbing blankets are no longer needed.”

“Coming back to your comments on cadence, did you say we could have a launch every six seconds?”

“No, that is just the launch itself. There are several other steps, but we could probably launch two or three times per hour.”

“What would it cost?” she asked.

“No matter what we build, it will cost around ten billion dollars. You always need a seaport, airport, and hangars.” Webb said. “The launch tower itself would cost ten billion beyond that.”

“Put together a full proposal,” she said finally. “Cost estimates, timeline, technical specifications. Everything. If I’m going to show this to the board, it needs to be bulletproof.”

“How long do we have?” Webb asked.

Bastos checked her watch. “The board meets in two weeks. You have three days to put it together, then I’ll practice my presentation with you until that meeting.”

“We’ll have something ready,” Kim promised.

Kim and Webb went to meet with other researchers. Bastos remained in Webb’s office, looking at the whiteboard covered in sketches of towers reaching toward space. She didn’t understand the thermodynamics or the structural engineering, but she understood opportunity.

The day before the meeting, Kim, Webb, and Bastos sat in her office. The images on the whiteboard had all been drawn by Bastos this time. “I feel I really got it this time.”

Webb walked up to the board and erased a four and wrote a three. “Try not to get too technical in the meeting. You got the key points.”

The board meeting started at eight A.M. Dr. Emile Carson spoke first. “Amira, JRC was chartered to do an enormous, maybe impossible job. That you have not accomplished your goal is no surprise. My concern, however, is that I don’t see any action.”

Bastos looked around the room. None of them would meet her gaze. Okafor had a large envelope in front of him, with her name on it. 

She went directly into her rehearsed talk. “I have the top scientists in the field working on extracting helium from Jupiter. In the meantime, there is one element of that problem that has global importance, and that is heavy lift. I have provided documents on your slates.”

Despite feeling weak in the knees, she stood and moved to a whiteboard, as rehearsed. “Our primary limitation right now is heavy lift, from the surface to LEO. Canaveral is backed up for three months. Boca Chica is backed up six. Wenchang only takes reservations three months in advance and is completely booked. The lunar population is capped not by demand or technology, but by heavy lift capacity. The world needs more.” She drew a diagram of the kilometer launch tower and the cavernous warehouse. She graphed the rocket equation beside that, drawing a dotted line from the top of the tower to the graph. “While this tower doesn’t boost height or speed much, as you can see, it has an enormous impact right where the most fuel is being used. It can reduce fuel costs by twenty percent.” 

Bastos stopped looking at the whiteboard and looked at the people. “Six months ago, I would have said fuel savings were paramount. But today, I see that as secondary. The goal is volume.” She continued drawing, showing a large arched roof connected to the tower. “The hangar has space to load payloads and stage several rockets at the same time. When ready, they are moved into a low-humidity pipeline.” She drew five rockets in a row. “The rocket spends twenty minutes in each station. First, we dehumidify the entire rocket body and all the internal bits to prevent frost formation. Bone-dry air is blown into every crevice of the rocket. After it is dry, we chill the entire rocket body to nearly freezing. In the third station, the body of the rocket stays close to freezing, the fuel tanks are cooled with refrigerated air to \-60°C, then, in the fourth station, with nitrogen vapor, leaving the tanks colder than they need to be to prevent boil-off when the rocket is fuelled. The fuelling takes place in the launch tube. When ready, the tube is sealed. The launch itself only takes six seconds.”

Carson was calculating on his slate. “Do I understand you can launch three rockets per hour with pipelining, which would be seventy-two per day?”

“Theoretically, yes. However, we don’t expect to reach that cadence. The research team expects to launch forty rockets per day.”

Professor Liu Wei, the sustainable development theorist on the board, was recognized. “Fuel expenditure remains my primary concern. I feel that a twenty percent improvement is too small.”

“Professor Wei, there are many at JRI who agree with your perspective. They have suggested an alternative project. With the board’s indulgence, I will describe that process.” Bastos moved to another whiteboard. “Mostly, the project is the same. We still need seaports, airports, barracks, and hangars. But they propose an enormous tower six miles high. This will take several years to build and will cost twenty times as much, but the rocket will exit near the stratosphere at Mach 2.3. This resolves several rocket problems very cleanly. The rocket would not need to handle the transsonic domain; it would be through seventy percent of the atmosphere, and it would have saved…” She drew another dotted line from the tower top to the graph of the rocket equation, showing a third of the fuel consumed at that point. Bastos noticed the directors were engaged. 

“At this altitude and velocity, JRI proposes a ramjet stage. While my first proposal uses a shroud for air augmentation, this ramjet is more complex and will require years of research before it can be viable. When it is viable, it will be able to accelerate the entire rocket to Mach 5.0 and release it at ninety thousand feet. At that point, the rocket lights. Much of the work has already been done. Even the nozzles that work at that altitude are ten percent more efficient. Net result, this system can launch around ten times as much payload from the same rocket. JRI exists to research academic ideas such as this. I don’t bring this to you at this time because I believe we need action now.”

The board dismissed her briefly and deliberated amongst themselves. When she was escorted back into the boardroom, she noticed the envelope in front of Okafor had been torn in half. 

Carson spoke. “Amira, we have reviewed your documents. Twenty billion is steep, but we all knew a spaceport would need to be built. We are authorizing that operation.”

Bastos was about to speak when Carson held up his hand. “Professor Wei and Ambassador Okafor are both insistent that progress continue on the larger project, as well. While it will take time and money, the board believes that it is consistent with the chartered purpose of JRC. We have allocated an additional fifty billion per year for that project. Will you be able to fit both projects in Punta de Maisi?”

Bastos looked at each of them. “Yes, they will fit together well. Following your plan, we will be operational sooner, and eventually, we will be efficient, too. The shorter tower will remain useful for non-standard launches. I will start immediately on the proposal I gave you, and get you a combined field proposal by next week.” 

That night, Bastos brought Kim and Webb to Restaurante La Colina. The waiter asked what they would be having. 

“Lobster in coconut milk and blue lagoons for everyone. And keep them coming.”

Kim asked first. “It looks like things went well. Spill.”

Bastos said nothing until she had taken a sip. “The board was prepared to let me go. It started off tense, but our prep worked. It didn’t even seem stressful. I just went through the motions we rehearsed. Carson understood cadence. But Wei hated it.”

“Of course she did. She hates combustion. And agriculture, too, if that came up,” Kim said.

“I felt that. But one thing she doesn’t hate is spending more money. The board approved the kilometer tower, but stipulated that we must also build the super tower.”

Webb dropped his drink. “Both\! You’re a genius, Amira.”

The busboy came over to clean up the spill while the waitress brought him another drink.

“Speaking of genius, Marcus, you are going to have to help me build a new proposal.”

The board approved the updated proposal online, and she sent out an RFP. She got one of the younger new managers to oversee the process, Terence Halden. Not many companies could operate at this level, and preparing a bid took time. A month later, Bastos checked in on him.

“Seventeen responses so far,” Halden reported, scrolling through his slate in Bastos’s office. “Most of them are consortium bids. This is too big for any single company to handle alone.”

“Any standouts in the bidding?” she asked.

“A few interesting approaches,” Halden said. “There’s a European consortium led by Bechtel that’s proposing a more conservative timeline but very robust engineering. A joint venture between Japanese and Korean firms that’s promising some innovative materials science.” He paused. “And there’s one Brazilian company, Construtora Atlântico, that submitted a bid about thirty percent lower than anyone else.”

Bastos looked up sharply. “Are they credible?”

“I’m having our procurement team investigate them now. Their proposal is surprisingly detailed.”

Three thousand miles away, Tobias Raines called Henrique Cavalcanti, the CEO of Construtora Atlântico. “Henrique, have you put in the bid?”

“I have been working on that for weeks with my team. The RFP was written by academics, but we worked it out. When I calculated the cost, I cut fifteen billion from that, as you asked. It is a sealed bid process, but I am pretty sure we will be the standout for low price.”

“That’s good, we need to get the bid. What I need to be sure of is you can get this built quickly. Every day it is not built is costing me money.”

Henrique looked up from the structural calculations. “Our current design is eighteen months. As you know, projects like this face more delays than advances. This may take two years or more.”

“That won’t do. Let’s target fourteen months. You let me know what we can do to speed this along.”

“Yes, sir. Can I ask a question? They say their plans are speculative. Are you concerned it might not work well?”

“We build infrastructure for them. That will definitely be useful, whether the tower works as advertised or not,” Raines said. “Even if the pneumatic launch system is a complete failure, we’ll have built the first step we will need to support Jupiter operations. And if it fails, I believe they will figure out how to fix it.”

“Understood. We will continue to press for ideas to get this done faster.”

“Fantastic, Henrique. Let me give you a number of a metal supplier. Felipe runs a large mill in Mobile, Alabama called Planetary Metals. He can get you what you need fast. Let him know I sent you.”

“Will do.”

The call came three days later. Bastos was reviewing quarterly reports when Halden entered, with rolls of blueprints under his arm.

“Construtora Atlântico is a legitimate contractor. They have done a lot of work for the Brazilian government.”

Bastos set down her reports. “You’ve finished the background check?”

“Yes. They have a long and successful history. And a fascinating proposal.”

Halden spread out the Brazilian proposal on Bastos’s desk. “They made several changes. Twinned towers don’t just double launch cadence. It creates fault tolerance. If something goes wrong with tube A, you can use tube B until it is fixed. The tubes are designed to be built by jacking the top and inserting a new segment underneath. That process can also run in reverse, bringing it down for needed repairs or when there is a hurricane warning.”

“They are using the local limestone to run their own cement plant on site, which I think is smart, but they have also requested mineral rights to sell off the limestone they excavate. I wrote a note rejecting this idea, but I haven’t sent it yet. I wanted to talk to you.”

“Don’t look a gift horse in the mouth. Selling limestone may be why they are cheaper. The worst thing for us would be a huge pile of broken limestone and no place to send it.”

“OK, I will approve it. Another variance is the eliminated rail.”

“No, we need that to connect Baracoa to Punta de Maisi.”

“Instead, they want to use Mexican Modutram. The top speed on the intercity route will be about forty-five miles per hour, which is slower than the spec you and Webb put together, but they do much more. Yours had three stops: Baracoa, the airport, and the hangar. Theirs has unlimited stops and routes. They let it run all over the construction site, depending on where the work is, and all they have to do is paint some lines. Here this week, two hundred yards away next week. And the cars are very flexible. They can carry passengers, freight, and food trucks that can roll right up to the work sites. They even have yellow steel modules for scrapers and graders.”

“That’s close enough to rail for my needs,” Bastos said, scanning the timeline. “They are going to finish the smaller project in eighteen months? That’s half what the European consortium was promising.”

“And they’re guaranteeing performance. If the system doesn’t achieve the specified cadence, we don’t pay the final thirty percent.”

Bastos studied the technical drawings, trying to process the scale of what she was looking at. “I want this started now.”

“Yes, ma’am,” Halden answered. “I will get them started today.”

Nine months later, Bastos stood on the observation platform that had been built specifically for this moment. The concrete pad stretched for nearly a hundred yards, positioned a mile from the construction site.

Around her, a small crowd had gathered. Kim and Webb from JRI, and Carson from the board of directors. The hangar was still just a skeleton, but the twin towers were complete and guyed in place. They watched as the first pneumatic tube was jacked into place, a hundred meters at a time. They started with the crown, which looked like a pepper pot muzzle brake. In less than three hours, the tube was ready. They didn’t yet have the entire pneumatic system ready, but they could run a low-pressure test. A stacked wooden slug, six meters across and ten meters high, stood where the rocket would go. Technicians began the countdown. It used a clock face with only a second hand. When it got close to the end, everyone on the platform was cheering, “Five, four, three, two, one.” Nothing. It had launched, but it was still travelling. Six seconds later, they saw the slug exit at about six hundred miles per hour straight up. Five seconds after that, the report struck, shaking them right through their bodies. The slug continued up for another twenty seconds, before beginning its descent. Vanes on board directed it to land safely at sea. 

“Linda,” Henrique murmured.

Bastos looked out at the impossibly tall structure, the first piece of humanity’s most ambitious attempt to reach space cheaply and routinely.

She thought about her desperate board meeting ten months ago, when she’d been scrambling for anything that might show progress. Now she had seen it launch a slug two and a half miles into the Cuban sky.

For the first time since coming to Baracoa, she felt like they were going to change the world.
