Chapter 2

Helium Fever

3,316 words · about 14 minutes

Price of ³He$8,000,000/kg
Current Production1 ton/year
Current Consumption12 kg/year
Time Since Start1 week to 2 months

Li Yun Tan was alone in her apartment, watching the news. Locally, a traffic accident in downtown Phoenix resulted in multiple injuries. The ambulances moved freely to the intersection, lights flashing, traffic moving aside. In Kuala Lumpur, when she was twelve, the streets had been gridlocked for hours.

In financial news, the story opened with canned footage of the Edison bulb in Ilha Fusão. Helion Dynamics was offering a two-year price guarantee of eight million dollars per kilogram for lunar ³He, and a loan of up to one million dollars for transit.

She looked around her apartment; no one to call, no debt, no lease, just a photo of her and her parents when she was twelve. She opened her accounts and worked through the numbers. Her savings, plus accrued vacation time from Arizona Public Service, were enough to buy the necessary gear. Getting that gear into space was the real cost. Heavy lift from Texas was the cheapest way to get up, at twenty dollars per kilogram. There was a Russian ferry operator that could haul up to twenty-five tons from low Earth orbit to the Moon, but he was charging as much as heavy lift. The transit loan terms were steep; twenty percent interest, but they let her pay it back in ³He. With a steady hand, she hit the “APPLY” button.

She waited as they processed her application. Every day, more people were headed to the Moon. Big corporations were building advanced equipment and sending well-outfitted crews. Others were cobbling together used equipment and going on their own, all of them driven by the fever for ³He.

Li didn’t tell anyone at work. Every day when she got home, she checked the claim map, watching as clusters of claims grew. The ³He distribution was nearly uniform; she knew it didn’t matter. Still, she wondered what they knew that she didn’t. She hoped the good sites wouldn’t all be gone by the time she got the loan. If she got the loan.

She was in the office at her desk when she got the notification on her slate. Work suddenly meant nothing. She stopped by HR and quit, leaving immediately. On the way home, she began checking Craigslist, under Resumes/Moonwork for an equipment operator. She found Darrin Brooks maintaining offshore turbines in the North Sea. For years, he had logged every inspection and checked each seal twice. That was enough. She offered him a contract, passage included. Darrin looked up at the Moon and said yes within an hour.

Li went through a lawyer to get the claim. Each claim was limited to one square mile per person, which, on average, could yield just over one ton of ³He. By including Darrin Brooks on her paperwork, she was able to file a claim for two square miles in the southwest corner of Ball Crater. She got the paperwork back three days later. Her name was written as Tan, Li Yun. Fixing it would take a week; new claims were being filed every day. Her name was now Tan. She sold what remained of her assets and met Darrin in Tucson, at the market that had grown beside the Air Force Boneyard.

The vendors at the Lunar Supply Market traded in surplus and salvage, ex-military and scientific equipment, and a few new components from specialty manufacturers. They started at the aisle of helium extractors.

Li found large ovens with ten-ton tumblers and one-minute cycle times. The power rating was hundreds of megawatts.

Darrin called her across the aisle. “We can’t run those. That’s for industrial operations. RF extractors don’t heat the rock, just the gas.”

Li joined him. “How does that work?”

Darrin was reading a brochure. “It uses something called ‘Larmor resonance.’ The magnets adjust it, and the RF generator excites the ³He atoms more than it excites the other atoms.”

“So we get pure ³He?”

“We’ll be lucky to get five percent, but it is still a huge savings in bottling and shipping.”

Darrin was looking for trusted brands. Li kept her eye on the power requirements and the shipped weight.

They started looking at one extractor because it mounted on stilts to let them dump processed regolith easily, and had a large hopper so collection could be independent of operation.

The vendor talked them through the operation. “On the moon, regolith isn’t all the same. You’ve got ilmenite and other magnetic minerals mixed unevenly with anorthosite and other non-magnetic minerals. You can see sometimes it’s darker, sometimes lighter. When you run it, you adjust the magnetic field strength to compensate. That’s what the levers are for.”

That made no sense to Li. “How would I know where to set the levers? Test the load before it goes in?”

“If only it were that simple. You can’t see directly what the right field strength is, but you can see the effect it is having. You watch this screen. A mass spec is sampling just a few atoms at a time every few seconds. The screen will show peaks representing how much ³He, ⁴He, and hydrogen you are pulling.”

“So I keep the levers where I am getting more ³He than hydrogen.”

“Yes and no. ³He is rare. You will always get more hydrogen and ⁴He than you get ³He. You maximize the ³He ratio. Keep it cold, watch the hydrogen and ⁴He. If you let the field get too weak, hydrogen gets released faster. If it is too strong, more of the energy heats the rock, and your ⁴He extraction increases. You want to live in the sweet spot between them. Don’t just set the levers. You keep adjusting them the entire time. Generally, your purity will start to fall after you extract about half the ³He. That’s a good time to stop and process another batch.”

Li pulled out her slate and took a few notes. She and Darrin stepped a few stalls away. “Darrin, I know you like this one, but it weighs twelve hundred pounds more than some others, and it will cramp our budget.”

“It weighs more, mostly because of the regolith feeding equipment. That will make mining much more productive. The other ones that have hoppers and stilts weigh as much.”

“Lunar regolith eats everything regardless of reliability.”

“This one is built for easy repair.”

Li looked at a few more extractors, but Darrin was right. She returned and bought the one he recommended.

Darrin had started looking at living quarters while Li finished the extractor purchase. He found one he liked and called Li over. “Hey, what do you think of this? Dual Bosch air recyclers and a top-quality airlock.”

“Are you kidding? Other huts have much bigger airlocks.”

“I know, but this is from a top manufacturer, durable and solid. And small is better. Every time we cycle the airlock, we lose oxygen. The smaller the volume, the less we lose.”

Li studied the layout. It was a tiny Quonset hut, nine feet square, the size of a four-man tent. The bed was a three-quarter mattress, four feet across. The bathroom was just an alcove, without a door or even a curtain, and the shower had a hose with a low-volume mist emitter. The kitchen cabinet supported a small microwave oven with an electric heating coil on top.

Li pointed to the eighteen-inch window, which was packed separately. “What’s this for?”

The vendor looked at it, a hint of amusement in his eyes. “People on the Moon like to look at Earth. When you anchor the hut, you can cut a small hole in the roof, and this will fit in and seal up the hole. You align it to frame Earth. Because the Moon is tidally locked, Earth doesn’t move much. Pretty much every hut on the near side has an Earth window.”

She checked the price. It was higher than most huts of comparable size, but the compact dimensions and low mass made launch affordable.

It came with a heavy crate containing the life support core. The core was a column of cylindrically wound polypropylene tubing with embedded LEDs, configured as a closed algal loop. It took in wet and dry waste and stale air, returning oxygen, heat, light, and edible biomass.

“The hand grinder and impeller are more work, but it’s one less motor to maintain,” Li said, checking the heat welds. “This closes the nutrient loop.”

Darrin stared at the emerald-stained plastic, his face paling. “Nutrient loop? We have to eat that?”

Li wrinkled her nose. “Not appealing, but we are a long way from Earth. I’m also buying a seed package so we can grow microgreens and sprouts. We’ll manage.”

They looked at rovers and crawlers next. Li liked the faster, lighter rovers for covering ground quickly. But Darrin shook his head. “We need to haul twenty tons of gear to the site, then move hundreds of tons of regolith once we’re there. The crawlers are slower, but they can handle the load.”

Li bought the crawler, but wasn’t completely satisfied. “We won’t always have a full load.”

They got to the comms section. Li was looking at the radios. “Life up there will be strange, always saying ‘copy’ and ‘over’.”

A vendor stepped up. “People don’t do that much. They use Local Band. Here’s a headset, try it out. Don’t worry about etiquette, just talk naturally.”

Li and Darrin both put one on. Talking felt normal. They could both talk at once, hear other people clearly when close, while voices faded with distance.

She listened carefully, hearing two other conversations at the same time. “So natural.”

“It is an AM band. Multiple signals can add together, just like talking here on Earth. The one odd thing is that sometimes you get a heterodyne squeal when two systems get close for the first time. Just a short chirp.”

“So everyone can hear everything we say?”

“Most radio sets have a few encoded FM bands for restricted conversations. Then you have to use terms like ‘copy’.”

They collected all their gear and weighed it. Fifteen tons. Li bought ten tons of solar panels to reach the twenty-five-ton limit. They loaded everything into a semi-trailer and hauled it to Texas.

The launch team packed it alongside cargo from a few other crews. Li was perfectly calm until the elevator ride to the passenger compartment. The platform was higher than she expected. Below her, everything that had been important looked so small. She had been too busy to say goodbye. Again. As they strapped her into her seat, she closed her eyes.

The lift to orbit took twelve minutes at three G. Li’s vision narrowed, and her chest compressed. Every breath was a fight. Darrin’s knuckles were white against the armrest beside her. Then the pressure vanished. Weightlessness. They had made it.

In low Earth orbit, they transferred to a lunar ferry. It was a retrofitted module with Cyrillic lettering and a Russian pilot who did not waste words. The trip to the Moon took three days, cramped and vibrating the whole way.

A news alert flickered on her slate: three miners dead at Mare Serenitatis. Suit failures. The preliminary report cited “substandard seals and counterfeit pressure bladders.” The company issued a statement: “We are cooperating fully with the investigation.”

Li closed the feed and looked at the suits Darrin had bought. She’d found suits for a third of the price and thought they’d be adequate, but Darrin had insisted on these expensive suits. He bought decommissioned Indian Space Research Organization stock and insisted on inspecting every valve himself. At the time, she called it paranoia. Now she was grateful. She tightened her grip on the gear straps. Soon, they’d see how well every choice held up.

The ferry set them down three miles west of Ball Crater. Just regolith fines and glare, with the crater wall rising a thousand feet to the east. No cloud of dust on arrival. Nothing frightening or welcoming. The horizon was closer than on Earth, but it didn’t feel small; it felt empty.

Darrin and the pilot pulled the loaded crawler out of the cargo bay. Li noticed where the pilot’s gaze fell. It was always on people or equipment, never the Moon. They loaded their gear on the crawler and set off. Li was fine with the slow movement of the crawler. She was fine with absolutely everything.

She followed the lunar claim map on her slate. Darrin sat beside her, watching the terrain roll past. “How far?” he asked.

“Three miles to the rim. Our claim is just inside, on the southwestern floor.”

They’d spent the entire ferry ride planning where to position the solar panels for optimal sun exposure, how to route the power lines, and which section to start extraction from first. “First thing we do is anchor the hut,” Darrin said. “Get life support stable before we even think about extraction.”

Li looked up at the Earth, judging its phase. “We have about ten days left before lunar night. I’ll set up the panels, then I’ll run a site survey. Map the concentrations before we start sweeping.”

“You think we can pull an entire bottle before lunar night?”

“No, but even a half bottle is probably worth shipping back.”

Darrin laughed. “Then what?”

“Then we do it again. And again.” She glanced at him. “You ready for this?”

“I’ve been ready since you called me in the North Sea.” He looked up at the Earth. “Waxing gibbous. The best phase to start something new.”

Li looked at the claim. She considered where she would post it when they set up camp. The rim of Ball Crater rose ahead of them, a wall of ancient rock and debris about a kilometer high. The slope looked to be maybe twenty degrees at the approach point Li had mapped. “There’s our door,” she said, pointing to a pass where the rim had degraded into a rough ramp of rubble.

The crawler moved easily even as it began the climb. The treads bit into loose regolith, found purchase on harder substrate beneath. They climbed slowly, steadily, the wall rising around them. They reached the rim twenty minutes later. Before they could see into the crater, Local Band gave a loud heterodyne squeal. Less than a second, and it was gone.

Li looked at Darrin.

Darrin said nothing. The crawler pulled over the lip and onto the relatively flat ring of the crater’s edge.

“Li, something is wrong here. Verify our claim on the map.” Darrin leaned forward.

Li stopped the crawler. The coordinates matched. She checked twice. This was their claim, filed three months ago, survey boundaries confirmed. But someone was already there. The rig was no temporary setup. Solar mats stretched in neat rows, cables buried under regolith berms. An RF extractor hummed over processed material. Two huts sat leveled on foundation pads, their airlocks sealed, power lines running between them. This crew had been here for weeks. Maybe longer.

“That’s our claim,” Darrin said quietly.

“I know, Darrin.”

“So what do we do?”

She stared at the operation below them. She pulled up the claim on her slate. Morrison & Chen, certified attorneys. Tucson filing. All the seals and signatures. Everything proper. “We show them the paperwork,” she said.

Darrin looked at her, then back at the established rig below. “You think that’ll work?”

“It has to.” She started the crawler down the interior slope toward their claim.

When they arrived, Darrin climbed down, holding their paperwork. “This sector of Ball Crater is Li Yun Tan’s claim. You and your team need to pack up and move.”

The other miners had started gathering. From the side, one used his survey laser, cutting the paper in half while Darrin held it.

The leader looked at Darrin. “No.”

Li recoiled.

“We can’t just leave,” Darrin said. “We have no other claim. No way to get home.”

“We’ll get a surveyor in here to settle this,” Li said.

The leader turned his gaze to Li. “You need to leave now.”

One of the miners leveled a survey laser at Darrin’s chest. Just enough power to see the green dot. Another miner etched a warning line into the dust, a foot from Darrin’s boots.

Li’s throat went dry. The world collapsed into that green, speckled dot. A gentle squeeze and the laser would cut right through his pressure suit.

She understood these men would kill them. These men would keep the equipment she and Darrin had so carefully chosen, they would work the claim, and sleep fine. Darrin and Li would simply stop existing, and no one would ever know or care.

The paperwork meant nothing. The signatures, the seals, and the lawyer’s certification were all worthless. No one was coming. No one would ever come.

She looked at her claim, cut in half, on the crater floor. “Darrin, get back in the crawler.” Her voice sounded distant, like it belonged to someone else.

Darrin climbed back in. Li turned and moved the crawler as fast as it would go. She wished they had a rover. The claim-jumpers kept a green dot on Darrin until they made it over the pass.

Coming out of the pass, they continued straight southwest. Her hands were shaking. She’d believed the paperwork mattered. She’d paid lawyers, filed claims, done everything right. None of it meant anything.

They’d figure out the rest once they had distance. The Moon felt hostile, but there was nowhere to go but the Moon.

A few ridges later, they passed a wrecked crawler. Li slowed. No markings, no beacon. Solar panels shattered. The cabin hatch hung open, dark inside.

“Should we check it?” Darrin asked.

Li stopped, and they got out. “Salvagers already did. Look at the hatch. It was forced open from the outside.”

“What happened to the crew?”

“No obvious damage. Darkside, maybe. Ran too long on batteries, didn’t make it back before their battery died. Two weeks of night out here with no power…” She let the thought hang. “Their suits didn’t last.”

Darrin was quiet. The crawler was intact except where it had been pried open. Darrin found two oxygen bottles in the cargo space on the back. Empty. He tossed them over to their crawler. “Li, we don’t know when we may need another oxygen bottle. These folks don’t need theirs anymore. What do you say?”

“Sure. Let’s see if we can pull their battery pack, too. Our crawler can handle the load.”

Li peered inside the broken crawler, then recoiled. The driver lay in the back, where salvagers had tossed him after stripping his suit. Mummified. Face dark as jerky, eyes deflated. Li backed away.

“Darrin, forget about the battery.”

“I’ve almost got it, Li. Just a few more bolts.”

“Darrin. Let’s just go. Scavengers took their suits.”

They got back in, and Li drove without stopping. Someone else’s mistake. Someone else’s claim now. It wasn’t hostility. The Moon didn’t care.

They kept moving until there were no more tracks. No one else had gone this far. The ground got rough, with frequent small craters. No more claims showed on the map. The crawler moved slowly, its tracks whispering across the dust.

Then Li saw it.

A wall a mile high, maybe more. White in the sunlight, rising from the curve of the Moon like a broken edge of the world. She stopped the crawler.

“What is it?” Darrin asked.

She didn’t answer. She stared at the wall, at the way it climbed into the black sky, massive and silent and utterly indifferent. Navigation showed the crater ahead was Tycho. She’d heard of it. Everyone had. Ancient. Huge. Empty.

She started the crawler again, steering directly toward it.
