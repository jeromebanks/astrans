Chapter 34

Proof

1,962 words · about 8 minutes

JRI launched the prototype on a Wednesday without ceremony. The experiment itself had changed shape over the last six months. The dirigible had become a nuclear ramjet with a single centrifugal compressor and a palladium alloy membrane venting hydrogen. Isotopic separation was built into the first fifty miles of the straw. The Larmor resonance RF heater from a lunar helium extractor was used to keep the ³He warm while the ⁴He condensed on the sides of the straw. It would eventually plug up and need to be drained, but that was fine for a short term project.

Lunar Tug had their type I cargo ships ready by launch, and were happy to carry the gear to Jupiter. The new ships made the flight in six months.

Savic did not expect it to work. He had already explained that to his advisor, Dr. Miriam Vance. The atmosphere was hostile and the radiation could destroy it. The ramjet was operating too hot to survive. Dr. Vance scheduled his thesis defense, anyway.

It did work. The tether held. The helium climbed up the tube. It was slow, less than fifty grams per hour, but it was pure. By the fourth day, they had bottled five kilograms of ³He and the ramjet was still running.

A week later, the tip of the intake cone melted. The change in aerodynamics destroyed the ramjet within a minute. Without thrust, the ramjet tore free of the tether and descended into Jupiter’s depths. This was the expected end, the only possible end.

The helium in the tether continued up past synchronous, to the platform. There it was pumped into a simple K-bottle. When the tether was empty, the platform released it. Without the tether holding it back, the platform went into an elliptical orbit that brought it past Callisto’s orbit. Lunar Tug had been waiting for it there, and moved to intercept. As they approached, the platform shot the K-bottle towards the Lunar Tug ship. It was caught in a net and brought into the cargo bay. The platform continued on its elliptical orbit, going a little higher, then descending on an arc that intersected the atmosphere. It was no longer necessary.

Savic went over the process and showed slides of the catch at the end of his defense. Then his committee asked him to step out of the room. Behind closed doors, Dr. Vance asked a simple question. “Has any grad student at the JRI accomplished nearly as much?”

When Savic returned to the room, Dr. Vance popped the cork on a bottle of champagne.

Meanwhile, the Lunar Tug ship burned hard to get out of the Jovian gravity well and brought the bottle back to LEO. There it was transferred to a descender and dropped into Earth’s atmosphere. It was recovered at Punta de Maisi.

Twelve kilograms of high-purity ³He from Jupiter was flown to Lisbon. A tiltrotor brought it to the Ilha Fusão tokaplex. Cameras swarmed. The Prime Minister of Portugal gave a speech that managed to include both Columbus and climate change in the same breath. Then came the moment, the ceremonial coupling of the feed line. The valve was turned, taking ³He feed from Jovian helium.

Back in Jamal, JRI had a graduation ceremony. Nikola Savic, Rithica Patel, Marco Bianchi, and Jason Cole were all awarded their Ph.D.s. On a large screen behind the stage, the events at Ilha Fusão were playing.

Power output from Ilha Fusão remained stable. Sensors lit green across the board. For the first time, a fusion reactor ran on Jovian ³He. The press reported that it was stable, self-sustaining, quiet. Elin laughed to herself. Of course the reactor didn’t care where the ³He came from, but news had to make it sound dramatic.

It was exciting to her, too. This proved they could pull gas from Jupiter. No one had really believed that. Well, Raines had, but that was mostly because he needed it and didn’t know any better. He did not understand the gravity well problem, the radiation, the harsh atmosphere. Elin thought about the irony. The JRC, which seemed to Helion Dynamics fully intent on preventing mining on Jupiter, had actually found a solution that neither she nor anyone else at Helion Dynamics would ever have considered. It was because of JRC that this project would work.

Headlines followed in waves. “Jupiter’s Fire.” “A New Helium Age.” “Lisbon Lights the Future.” Financial analysts who had once called Jovian ³He a vanity project now rebranded it as inevitable. Everyone wanted to know when the next bottle would arrive.

The world, it turned out, had been waiting for an excuse to believe.

The success hit JRC like a concussion. They’d managed the Jovian mining program as a political asset, expensive, photogenic, and safely in the distant future. The kind of initiative you could gesture toward in budget hearings without the burden of actual delivery. Now there was helium in a tank, power on a grid, and headlines naming them the architects of a new energy age.

Suddenly, they had to act like they meant it.

Internal memos pivoted overnight from risk language to rollout frameworks. Leadership scrambled for talking points. A new office was carved out of the executive floor, glass-walled and overlit, and handed to Halden with a congratulatory nod. The gesture was meant to look like reward, but it was containment, an attempt to box the fire they’d accidentally lit.

Halden knew what this meant. He took the office. He made no speeches. But the whiteboards at JRI started filling faster.

Publicly, JRC embraced the future. They released videos touting their long-term vision. They featured archival footage of internal meetings that had never taken place. Executives who’d once argued ³He was impossible now recast themselves as visionaries. The whole thing had always been part of the plan, they insisted. Jovian ³He was well understood by JRC, and they were just waiting for the engineers at JRI to finally address the problem.

But the pressure was enormous. With the headlines came demand. Production targets. Timelines. Questions from sovereign energy agencies. JRC, once the architects of risk assessment, now found themselves required to deliver. And they had no idea how to scale what had been, just months ago, a fringe experiment.

So they leaned on JRI. Hard. The message was clear. Commercialize this right now. Turn it into something that ships quarterly numbers. Halden, in his new office, nodded politely, and said he understood.

Halden sat alone in the new office, surrounded by silence and expensive materials. The glass was low-reflection and ballistic-rated. The walls were layered in real hardwood. A print of a tokamak torus hung on the wall. It was glossy, oversized, and meaningless. The desk was deep and shiny, the kind reserved for signatures and staged photos.

The office was not about status. It was about visibility. The open walls, the proximity to the boardroom, the curious placement of the security sensors, he had stepped into a place where his privacy was no longer tolerated. They didn’t expect him to disappear again into the back rooms of JRI or meet Elin at the café where the rice and plantains were still served from tin pots. Those days were over.

He now had an assistant. Her name was Liora. Young, competent, unreadable. She had been hired directly by the board and reported to them in parallel. Her role was framed as support for scheduling, outreach, travel, whatever Halden needed to enable him to deliver. It was clear from her first day that she had another task, to ensure the board knew Halden’s decisions before Halden made them.

She asked questions lightly. She took careful notes. She had access to his calendar and kept it tightly managed. Her desk was directly outside his door. She offered coffee, then logged his answer.

Halden smiled through it all. He gave Liora every answer she asked for, then made sure to ask her questions back. Not to intimidate. He had no interest in a power game, and he knew he wouldn’t win that, anyway. He just wanted to see how closely she was watching, and how deeply she understood.

She was good. He appreciated that. It made things easier.

The board had reasserted control. They would not be left behind again. So they had elevated him, encased him in transparency, and attached a quiet observer.

Halden smiled again. Not performatively. Not defiantly. It was simply the smile of a man who recognized the shape of the world he now lived in.

And was already planning his next move.

Halden’s visit to JRI was announced a week in advance, scheduled down to the minute by Liora. She handled the logistics, vetted the agenda, and ensured every presentation aligned with the board’s expectations. She vetted every attendee. The mood at the institute was respectful, but clearly cautious. JRI had once existed outside the orbit of executive attention. That era was over.

The conference room was sunlit and scrubbed clean of clutter. A small cadre of engineers and researchers presented their ideas. They spoke in turns, showing modular designs, projected scaling curves, and flexible deployment models. The slides were crisp. The numbers were plausible. They spoke the language of commercialization now.

Halden nodded throughout. He asked a few pointed questions, mostly about throughput assumptions and power budgets. He thanked them when they finished, complimented their discipline. Then, turning to Liora, he said, “Let’s come again next week. Same team.”

She made a note.

As they left the room, Halden paused at the doorway, exchanged brief goodbyes, and stepped out into the corridor.

On the table behind him, unnoticed by his assistant, a single piece of paper remained.

It was not formatted. It bore no heading. Just a line of text in Halden’s careful handwriting:

Call Elin

Below that, a number.

Savic, still standing near the screen, picked it up and read it twice. No one else had noticed. The presentations had been pro forma, intended to look good to the board when they read Liora’s notes. But this note was for them.

By the time he looked toward the hallway, Halden was already gone.

Within a week, the vacations began.

Officially, the rest periods were both earned and necessary time off after the long push for the first tethered collector. Unofficially, no one asked questions. One engineer scheduled a trip to Madurai. Another took time in Biloxi. A materials specialist found herself rerouted through a research exchange program in Malta. One by one, they slipped out of the official workflow for a couple weeks. No one raised alarms. Liora logged the absences, but saw nothing particularly noteworthy. Their replacements were already trained, briefed, and eager.

No one of them went to the same place twice. But each met someone. Dr. Elin Sarai had a long memory and a short tolerance for bureaucracy. She knew who could build, and who was just waiting for permission. Her introductions were quiet and deliberate.

In São Paulo, a propulsion tech found himself seated next to Catherine Dahl at a private lunch. She listened, asked two questions, and pulled out a slate. Her sketches were brutal, elegant, and actionable.

From the base on Ceres, Dr. Knox briefed another JRI fellow on radiation shielding and passive cooling systems. The meeting was ten minutes long. The packet he handed over was three hundred pages.

Across these meetings, the design began to crystalize. Not by committee. Not through official channels. But through the steady cross-weaving of minds too tired to wait for permission.

By the end of the month, JRI was a different organization. Officially, they were still a white-paper institute. But beneath the paper trail, they were building something real.
