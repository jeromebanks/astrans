Chapter 21

Lunar Power Up

3,086 words · about 13 minutes

Darrin got off the call with Felipe. Planetary Metals had agreed to send him the tungsten pipe he needed, but they didn’t have the tin. Catherine Dahl was the only person who could help him out. He had never called her before, but Li had given him her number. He went over what he was going to say in his mind. Tin was of no value on Ceres. Raines sent him the reactor. If she has any tin that she could send to him, he would appreciate it. However many times he went over it, he felt like he was grovelling. All he wanted was to ask. He waited until noon Paris time, put in the number, and closed his eyes, finally pressing the dial button.

Catherine came on. “Tan, what a pleasant surprise. I haven’t heard from you in months.”

The time delay meant she would wait two and a half seconds of silence before she heard his response. Not long, but awkward, and every moment he didn’t speak only made it worse. “No, sorry, this is Darrin. She gave me your number. I am looking for Catherine Tin. Catherine Dahl. Tin. I need tin. Can you sell us tin from Ceres? Planetary Metals doesn’t have much stock, and it is already committed.”

The delay meant people spoke in longer segments. On Earth, the call would have gone back and forth several times with hellos and softer greetings. That didn’t work with the delay.

Catherine was laughing too hard to speak, so she waited until she was composed. “Darrin. I’ve heard so much about you, but I don’t think we’ve met before. Dr. Knox told me he often has leftover tin when he purifies nickel. He and Mateo have been talking about an iron-cased bowl, but so far, it has never been shipped. Do you want it processed on Earth or shipped directly to the Moon?”

“No, Ma’am. We don’t want that at all. I mean, we want it shipped directly to the Moon. We will melt it here, so we don’t need any fabrication. I can send Knox the location of the regolith-filled landing pit. He has sent us metal balls before. We need twelve thousand tons, total.”

“I am talking like an idiot,” he thought during the delay.

Catherine sounded calm as ever. “I am sure that there is no problem. We can swap straight across for titanium, if that works.”

“Thank you so much. And a pleasure to meet you.”

The call ended. “Did I really just end with a greeting? Either way, the tin is coming.”

Planetary Metals delivered the pipe, and Darrin, Luke, and Pavel assembled it one mile west of the OK-550. Four loops like a cloverleaf, each two miles long. There was a takeout every five hundred feet, and the lines were insulated with a foot of aluminum oxide aerogel, fragile but able to withstand the heat.

Darrin and Luke set up the expanded cold ammonia radiators on the inside face of the north wall. Pavel was afraid of heights, so he acted as ground crew, keeping them supplied with pipes and reflectors, and using his survey laser to show them where to set each bolt.

Six months later, the tin started falling, in thirty-ton balls inside copper spheres. They weren’t round after impact, but most of them stayed together. Darrin fished them out of the regolith landing pit and hooked them up to the cableway.

They pulled out the pure tin and ground it down to pebbles, which they poured into the pipes. There was a standpipe for adding more pebbles to keep it full when it all melted.

Finally, Lunar Tug brought the Kagutsuchi Vortexes.

The Kagutsuchi Vortex came in twelve crates, each containing an intricate structure of magnets and stainless steel. The enormous and complex magnetic fields made assembling the sections a difficult project.

It was lunar night, as it all came together. They were illuminated by Earthshine and worklamps.

“We are short one trim magnet, bottom-right quadrant,” Pavel Kerensky announced from the reactor floor. “And the manifest says ‘Molecular Turbine Vacuum Pump.’ There’s no pump. Just the large hole where it is supposed to be mounted.”

Darrin glanced at the hole, then at the gauge on his pressure suit showing the impossible vacuum of space. “We’ll be fine, Pavel. What pressure was it supposed to reach?”

“The manual says it needs to be pumped down to 10−10 torr, and I have no pump.”

“Check your gauge. My suit is showing 10−12 torr. The instruction manual is written for Earth engineers.”

“It is not about the vacuum,” Pavel muttered. “It is about the principle. The package is incomplete.”

Li Yun Tan descended from the mezzanine, a slate in her hand casting a pale glow on her face. “I’ll order another magnet, but that will take weeks. Let’s check again.”

The three of them spent the next two hours looking through every container and bit of packing material that had come with it.

Li found it. “The magnet was in the ‘Oven Mitts’ box. It’s huge. I thought a trim magnet would be a tiny thing. This is the size of a coffee mug.”

“It’s strong, too,” Pavel warned. “Keep it in the holder until we line it up.”

Pavel and Li worked together with the magnet, but the field strength made it impossible to line up well. Pavel set up the press, and Li began winding it slowly into place.

When the magnet was fully installed, he called out, “It’s ready. Let’s start melting tin.”

Darrin had been running a megawatt across the first loop for more than an hour already, but it wasn’t yet hot enough to melt the tin. He moved to the control panel on the OK-550 and increased the current until it was running two megawatts. “Pavel, you tell me when it reaches temperature.”

Pavel was measuring the temperature on takeout number eight. He called out the numbers for fifteen minutes, then, “Temperature’s not rising steadily. It must be melting.”

He moved around to the control panel and switched on the pump. It bogged, and he hit the off switch immediately. “Nyet. We wait thirty minutes.”

Luke got them to help install the new Ipsen tumbler ovens on takeouts three through nine. They went on easily enough, but it still took two hours. When they were ready, Pavel went back to the panel and tried again, and this time, a trickle of metal moved. With something moving over the walls, Pavel was ready to start.

“Say good-bye to this world,” he said as he started the Kagatsuchi Vortex. He kept the deuterium density low as he brought the temperature up. The walls were heating. Pavel increased the density of deuterium until he could see radiation from the D-D side reaction. Then he let in a puff of ³He. There was a brief thermal spike, as expected, then all the gauges held steady.

“It’s working. I am feeding it again,” he called out, and started a longer, slow stream of ³He. Gauges showed fusion increasing steadily. The tin hadn’t all melted yet, but the reactor wall was over five hundred degrees. As the hot tin mixed with the still solid beads of tin in the pipe, it melted grain by grain. Pavel increased the pump flow rate a little, but it quickly ran dry, and he pulled it back to the slow trickle.

While the electric current had been heating the entire loop evenly, the reactor was heating the beginning of the loop the most. That meant even though all the tin up to takeout three was liquid, there was only a little liquid on the bottom of the pipe by takeout twenty, where the generator was installed, and less by the pump inlet.

“No need to hurry it. The wall still needs to heat up.” Pavel increased the ³He stream, letting the wall rise to a thousand degrees.

Luke was measuring the temperature on takeout nineteen. “Gauge is moving. Try ramping the pump again.”

Pavel didn’t increase the flow rate much, but it held. The tin had melted. It was under 300°C, but the pipe was flowing. Pavel brought the flow rate all the way up before increasing the density in the stellarator.

Once the tin was flowing, he increased the ³He feed rate until it reached the Lawson criterion. All the electricity was still coming from the OK-550’s generator, but now that was just pumps and gauges. Pavel stayed calm. “We hit self-sufficiency. I am no longer heating the plasma.”

The temperature of the tin kept rising, and the pipe started glowing. Luke was watching the Pyrometer on the takeout when it glowed cherry red. He called out the temperature every hundred degrees until they hit white heat at 1300°C.

At that point, the takeouts were glowing too brightly, so Darrin draped them all with woven alumina blankets. “I didn’t know this came with free lighting.”

Pavel kept increasing the ³He flow, driving the temperature to the theoretical maximum. “Look at that loop glow. We are going to have to cover it with regolith to save our eyes.”

Darrin throttled open the sCO₂ turbine. They held their breath as they watched electric generation move up to 200 MW. The temperature on the radiators began rising. “Running at forty percent capacity, output is steady, and the radiator bank is stable at 38°C. Pavel, you can shut down the fission plant’s electric generator. This one has us more than covered.”

While Pavel went over to the OK-550 to start the shutdown sequence, Luke started a tumbler oven with a hundred tons of regolith.

“Oh, this is smooth. With the bags automatically dumping on the conveyor, and a cableway to carry away the tailings, these will go a long way to helping me catch up with the increased demand,” Luke said.

“While I am shutting down the generator, can I shut down the entire OK-550 reactor?” Pavel asked.

“Not yet. I still need the old ovens running to help with the backlog. I’m going to be running both systems for a while. Darrin, I am going to need another twenty Ipsens, especially if operations continue to expand.”

When they were all satisfied, everyone but Luke headed back to the cantina. The blankets were glowing bright cherry red.

Pavel went to the bar and lined up four chichas.

Li and Darrin took Li’s favorite table, next to the claim map. “Darrin, we have the power, now. Can we distribute that power by the cableway? There was another darkside death last cycle. A solo miner. He didn’t push things, but his battery had been slowly degrading, and he didn’t notice. We can do something about this.”

“Tseng is making aluminum, and I use that for conductors. I can put HVDC cables on the cableway pylons. We can provide emergency power. What we can’t do is power everyone’s camps.”

“If we could power sweeping during the night, we would double our production.”

“The sweepers take too much energy. We can power lights and huts. We can provide emergency power. But we can’t power thousands of miners running heavy equipment. We can do is provide oxygen at the towers, though. Tseng is producing more than Lunar Tug needs. If anyone is in danger, they get in a crawler and drive to a tower, hook up, breathe easy and stay warm.”

“That’s what I want. If chechakos know this area is safer, then all the sites under the cableway will be filled fast,” Li said.

“I’ve been thinking about Tan’s Depot. Right now, we can refuel Lunar Tug ships, they have a pressurised warehouse for shirtsleeve loading and unloading, and the soft massdriver saves them fuel both ways. But what would you think if we were to install a new tower, coming over the rim to the northeast. It could be five miles long, and really cut Lunar Tugs expenses.”

“What would it do for us?”

“Tramp ferries will start charging less, and I am sure Lunar Tug would follow suit. And they could move more cargo. We have the power, now. I’ll get the materials from Tseng.”

“The depot has been very helpful. If you think it will be good for business, go ahead. I don’t mind.”

An hour later, Luke came in to the cantina, and Li and Darrin joined him and Pavel at the bar.

One of the miners, Rook, had sent a notification that he was running low on bags. Easy enough to send him more bags, but Darrin liked visiting the miners. He liked to see their camps and operations. The next morning, Darrin set up his pressure suit with extra batteries and set out.

The new cableway ran a circle inside the crater with a single north and south line connecting that ring to another ring outside the crater. The steel towers were fifty meters high, five times taller than the old system, fabricated from Tseng’s steel made from lunar iron and Earth carbon. In lunar gravity, their height allowed two-mile spans.

Six primary cable lines radiated outward from the external ring. The titanium 5 cables had a very low thermal expansion coefficient and were completely impervious to UV which had been a problem for the old dyneema lines.

Darrin watched the loaded bags coming in on the line across from him. The large bags held thirty tons of regolith each. Some miners were still using the old ten ton bags, but the thirty ton bags meant less handling and simpler operations. That drove most larger operations to thirty ton bags, and there were requests for larger sizes.

As he rode along, he watched a miner drop ten bags into the transfer station. The station would automatically load them on the line to Tycho Crater. The miner was still using the ten ton bags.

The visits helped Darrin understand the miner’s needs and it let him connect to them.

Rook was the first miner Darrin met. He wasn’t on the new line, a little south of the northeast line. Darrin dropped off and followed hauler tracks to Rook’s camp. Just a squat hut burried in a pile of regolith and the long, smooth trails of his sweepers combing dust into low mounds. Darrin waited for Rook to finish what he was doing.

“You’re early,” Rook said. He wore an old pressure suit, logo faded, patched with silver foil. His voice was rough, like a man out of practice with conversation.

“You’re on the schedule,” Darrin replied. “You flagged the system. Said you were exceeding bag capacity.”

Rook nodded and walked toward the docking rig where four bags hung from a low truss, each already sealed and tagged.

“New sweepers,” he said. “Bought ‘em from vendors at Tycho Crater. Tripled my coverage. Got more dust than bags now.”

“You know we have new bags now, thirty tons each.”

“My hopper is set to fill ten-ton bags. I just need more of them. I would like a cableway out here, though.”

“If you can do the work, I can bring you some old towers and Dyneema cable. It only works for ten-ton bags, but that will be enough for you,” Darrin said.

Rook thought about it. “That would be fantastic. I could stop wasting my time driving and focus on mining. Are they complicated to set up?”

“Mostly easy. Let us know when you are ready to go operational, and a technician will come make any needed adjustments and tension the lines for you.”

Rook looked Darrin up and down. “Tan’s already pulling twenty percent of my income. How much will that cost?”

“Right now, she would just be happy if you could send more regolith. No charge,” Darrin explained. “The one thing is we expect that if you add a spur, you will extend it out to other miners beyond you. Does that work for you?”

Rook thought about it for a long moment, tapping his gloved fingers on the docking truss. When he spoke, the rough buzz of his voice was calculating.

“You’re offering me a faster turnaround on my dust, and all I have to do is run the line past my hut,” Rook summarized. He nodded slowly. “I’m good with that. But if I’m going to be the gateway for the whole sector out here, that’s real estate that costs me something, even if it’s just the hassle of dealing with technicians.”

Rook walked closer, his shadowed visor reflecting Darrin’s suit. “Here’s my offer. I allow the expansion, I place the towers where they don’t foul my sweepers. And I get a cut, maybe 10%, of the haul from any miner who hooks into the grid past my claim. That’s my premium for being the local node.”

Darrin shook his head before Rook had finished. His voice, clear and steady, cut through the silence.

“I appreciate the ambition, Rook, but that’s not how Tycho’s infrastructure works,” Darrin said. “The cableway is free to everyone. No fees, no cuts, no exclusivity. You profit from it. We pull out more helium and pay you faster. But you don’t get to charge the next guy your own cut. Darrin paused, letting Rook think about it.

“What I can offer you is a guarantee that we’ll maintain everything we set up here. We give you the tools for free, Rook, because we want you to succeed, but we can’t let you charge a tax on the rest of the network.”

Rook hesitated. “Appreciate that. With the system you built, I don’t need a crew. I drop the bag, and you guys do all the rest. I really like that. It lets me focus. OK, we have a deal.”

“Perfect,” Darrin said, retrieving his hook from the truss. “I’ll have the tower team prep the ground posts tomorrow. Expect a tech out here to set the anchors in two days. You just focus on filling more bags.”

Darrin and Rook spent a while walking the perimeter of Rook’s claim. Darrin loved seeing how each miner ran their site a little differently.

Finished, Darrin started his long walk back to the cableway. He gave Rook a final wave. He was pleased with the infill strategy, repurposing old equipment and extending the reach of the cableway. Darrin returned to the line and clipped onto the inbound cable, headed back home. He remembered when Li had flashed her laser across the crater.
