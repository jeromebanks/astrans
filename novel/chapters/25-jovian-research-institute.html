Part II · Jovian Resource Corporation

Chapter 25

Jovian Research Institute

1,625 words · about 7 minutes

The shuttle climbed inland from Baracoa, following the winding road past forest and coffee plantations until it reached the settlement of Jamal. Here, where the foothills of the Sierra Maestra began, the Jovian Resource Corporation had carved out something new from the Cuban countryside.

Amira Bastos stepped out into the humid morning air, squinting at the campus spread across the hillside ahead. It had been four months since JRC had established the Jovian Research Institute, the JRI, and this was her first visit to see what their money had bought.

The campus spread across a natural amphitheater in the foothills, its buildings arranged in terraced rows. Low-slung concrete structures that opened onto sunlit courtyards were connected by terra cotta walkways. Abstract murals of planetary forms caught the morning light, and the pale plaster buildings glimmered against the green hillside. Small clusters of students and postdocs moved between laboratories, carrying slates, tools, and cups of coffee.

Looking northeast, Bastos could glimpse the curve of the beach half-hidden, glistening under the sun, only a couple miles away.

Dr. Ji-Wu Kim, the Jovian Research Institute’s director, appeared at her elbow with the practiced smile of someone accustomed to managing up. “Ms. Bastos, welcome to JRI. We’re honored to have you here for our first major symposium.”

“Beautiful layout,” Bastos said, noting how the buildings seemed to flow naturally into the landscape. “Good turnout?”

“Excellent. Sixty-two researchers from fifteen institutions. Graduate students, postdocs, senior faculty are all here to tackle the Jupiter problem.”

The Jupiter problem. Bastos liked how that sounded. Simple, focused, the kind of thing that looked good in board presentations. “And they’re making progress?”

“Significant progress. Why don’t I show you?”

Kim led her along a shaded walkway toward the main complex. The central building was larger than the others, its conference rooms opening onto a courtyard where a fountain trickled over carved stone. Through the glass doors, Bastos could see the main hall was already packed.

“That’s Rithika Patel,” Kim whispered as they took seats in the back. “One of our senior systems engineers. She’s presenting failure mode analysis for atmospheric collection platforms.”

At the front, a young woman was explaining something involving a whiteboard covered in equations that might as well have been hieroglyphics to Bastos. The presentation involved charts and technical jargon, but she caught the key phrases: “yield optimization,” “operational parameters,” “viable collection rates.”

She turned to Kim. “Kim, I am going to need Rithika to write an abstract for me. I want the board to hear about the kind of work we are doing here.”

When questions began, an older man with precise gestures raised his hand. “What about material degradation in the radiation environment?”

“That’s Dr. Leland,” Kim murmured. “Our materials specialist.”

“Excellent question,” Rithika replied. “We’re modeling accelerated wear patterns, particularly for membrane systems and structural components. Current projections suggest…”

Bastos tuned out the technical details and focused on the energy in the room. These people believed in what they were doing. The details mattered to them.

After the presentation, Kim guided her through the hallways of the main building. Along the way, there were signs pointing to various interest group meeting areas: Heavy Lift - South Wing, Harvesting - Central Hall, Jovian Ejection - North Wing.

“The institute is broadly chartered with the impossible task of solving the ‘Jupiter problem.’ We have broken this problem into four subproblems.”

She ticked them off on her fingers. “First: heavy lift. Getting mass from Earth to orbit cheaply enough to build the enormous infrastructure needed for mining Jupiter. Second: interplanetary transit. That one’s handled. A private company, Lunar Tug, has announced plans to provide regular freight and passenger service throughout the mid solar system. Third: harvesting the ³He from Jupiter’s atmosphere. That is where most of our people focus. Fourth: ejection, getting that helium back out of Jupiter’s gravity well economically.”

Bastos copied the list of talks. This was the kind of clear problem statement she could work with and share with the board.

“The harvesting problem has the best characterized solutions,” Kim continued, leading her to a different conference room in the central hall. “We’ve organized our harvesting research around three main paradigms, each with multiple variants.”

Bastos pulled out her phone and discretely started taking notes.

Kim introduced Bastos to a graying woman. “Amira, this is Jackie Morrison, formerly with NASA orbital mechanics. She is our department chairman for Helium extraction.”

Bastos smiled and shook her hand. “Pleased to meet you. What are you researching?”

Jackie launched into a catalog. “First approach: orbital intercept missions. The two main variants are elliptical orbits that dive into the atmosphere periodically, and low circular orbits that skim the exo atmosphere continuously. Both require significant propulsion to maintain their trajectories.”

“Second, atmospheric aircraft. Fixed-wing designs optimized for the density and pressure environment. Some maintain level flight while others dive repeatedly. These craft experience much higher pressures, so collection is faster and easier than with the exoatmospheric collectors, but powering them is a huge problem.”

“And third, buoyant platforms,. Various dirigible and balloon concepts for sustained atmospheric presence. The problem there is keeping them from being ripped apart.”

Bastos looked at her perplexed. “Which one’s most promising?”

“That’s… complicated. Each approach solves different parts of the problem. The dirigibles are probably most feasible for sustained collection, but they are the worst when it comes to ejecting that payload from the gravity well.”

By midday, the Caribbean sun had turned the courtyard into a furnace, and Kim suggested they take lunch in the cafeteria. The dining hall occupied the ground floor of the western building, with large windows overlooking a valley that stretched toward the distant sea.

Kim guided Bastos to a table with several of the researchers, picking at a plate of rice and beans while listening to animated discussions about membrane chemistry and orbital mechanics. At the next table, a group of graduate students were sketching diagrams on napkins, their conversation a mix of English, Spanish, and technical jargon.

“You know what strikes me?” Bastos said to Kim. “The energy here. These people aren’t just doing research; they’re on a mission.”

Kim took a sip of carbonated water. “That’s what happens when you give smart people an impossible problem and tell them to solve it.”

“Is it impossible?”

“Six months ago, I would have said yes. Now…” Kim gestured toward the windows, where they could see other buildings scattered across the hillside. “Maybe it’s not impossible. When you break the problem down, as they do, it is just many nearly impossible problems that need to be solved all at once.”

Through the windows, Bastos could see the landscape that had drawn JRC to this location. Jamal sat in a natural bowl surrounded by green hills, isolated enough for security but connected enough for logistics. To the north, the mountains rose toward the Sierra Maestra. To the south, the land dropped away toward the coast where Baracoa waited with its harbors and launch facilities.

After lunch, Kim walked her through the rest of the campus. The afternoon light slanted across the courtyards as students and researchers moved between buildings, animated conversations spilling out of doorways.

As they walked through the main hall, filled with people working on their slates, Bastos asked Kim, “The talks were fascinating, but surely they exchange papers, so they already knew all that. Why do you have the symposium?”

Kim gestured to a young man sitting in a chair staring at the ceiling. “Several reasons. I may poach someone we need on our team. I get people talking to each other, to see if they can engineer something. But, mostly, I do it to feed thoughts to the creative thinkers. Like Nikola Savic over there. He’s exploring alternative approaches. He’s not ready to present them yet, and maybe he never will be, but independent thinkers like him are our real hope.”

This was something the board would not appreciate, but Kim’s plan made sense.

“How long?” Bastos asked as they began walking back toward the shuttle pickup point.

“For what?”

“For answers. Real ones. Not just more questions.”

Kim considered this as they descended the terraced walkways. “If we’re talking about proof of concept? Demonstrable technology? Maybe ten years for collection systems. Twenty for integrated operations. I don’t know.”

Bastos nodded. She didn’t have a deadline, she simply needed a number. Twenty years was long enough to sound realistic, short enough to maintain political support.

As the shuttle pulled away from the JRI campus, Bastos settled into her seat and watched the buildings recede into the afternoon haze. The vehicle wound back down through the hills, past small farms where cattle grazed in fields bordered by royal palms. The landscape here felt ancient and settled, a stark contrast to the cutting-edge research happening in the labs they’d just left.

The road curved through a valley where a river ran clear over limestone rocks. On the far bank, she could see the remains of an old sugar mill, its chimney rising like a monument to an earlier age of Cuban industry far removed from space travel and helium.

As they descended toward the coast, the view opened up. First the red tile roofs of Baracoa appeared through the trees, then the blue expanse of the Caribbean beyond. The JRC headquarters building sat prominently on its hillside overlooking the harbor, where even now she could see ships moving between the piers and work lights beginning to flicker on as daylight faded.

The shuttle approached the JRC complex, with its security gates and administrative buildings and the gleaming headquarters. Bastos didn’t understand it all, but the people working on it seemed to.

And that was enough.
