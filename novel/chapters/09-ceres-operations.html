Chapter 9

Ceres Operations

2,920 words · about 12 minutes

The flight had been long, crowded, and without reprieve. No one slept well. The air thinned over time in moisture, warmth, and patience. They expected space to be an empty void, but the cramped ship, poor food, and lack of diversion became its own form of pressure. For nearly a year, they floated in the slow, shared erosion of appetite and privacy, caught in a loop of chemical meals and scrubbed breath.

When Ceres finally came into the viewport, no one spoke.

It expanded from a dot to a looming face of dust and fracture. Gray ridges and black cavities etched a surface older than Earth’s oceans. There were no soft curves, just a slow-turning mass, pocked and grooved, where sunlight caught on crater ridges.

The ship descended into low orbit. The station appeared on schedule, a square kilometer of hard foam, angles, and scaffolds. It looked temporary and skeletal, a foothold blown into place by machines.

Most eyes went to the barracks, a dull rectangle lit in amber.

Knox stood at the forward viewport, arms crossed, gaze fixed. The station looked like a thin exoskeleton clinging to rock. But he could already see its future; the rhythms were already in his head: power draw, tailings piles, feedstock flow. He had revised the protocols, rewritten the drills, and practiced until decision became reflex.

“We go in thirty,” he said.

They suited up. Pressure gear sealed. Packs checked. When the hatch opened, ten of them spilled out, crowding onto a rover built for two. They jostled for footing, half-laughing, half-shouting in Local Band. The wheels groaned under the load as they lurched toward the barracks.

From the outside, the module looked intact. The lights were on. The air lock didn’t pressurize and open, though. They cycled through by hand and stepped into cold metal. Emergency lights pulsed, casting the room in a slow, amber rhythm. There was no air. No warmth. No weight.

For a few seconds, no one moved. The silence wasn’t just in the room. It was on the channel too.

Knox scanned the room, jaw tight. He had run this scenario in his head a dozen times. And here it was. Real.

“Start tracing,” he said. The voice was flat, and his order steadied them.

They moved fast. The leak was easy to find. Frost marked a seam on the outer paneling where a misaligned duct junction was improperly fused. They clamped it, sealed the breach with epoxy, and reinforced the joint with a crossbar from the maintenance rack.

Once the repair cured, they restarted the air feed, slowly. Full pressure would take hours. They watched the numbers rise as the module stabilized.

The gauge inched past the safe threshold. The station was breathing again. The crew cheered and started moving into their rooms.

Knox made his rounds through the other modules. Life support: green. Power: steady. Food systems: operational. Water reserves: full.

He checked the water processor status panels.

Three showed green. The fourth had a warning indicator.

He pulled up the diagnostic log. The warning had been active for five months. Processor four: catastrophic failure.

He suited back up and went to inspect it physically.

The housing was intact from the outside, but when he opened the maintenance panel, he saw it immediately. A puncture the width of a pencil was punched clean through the primary heat exchanger. The metal around it was deformed, petaled outward. Micrometeoroid strike. The damage to the tubing and electronics was beyond their ability to fix. The processor was destroyed. Completely unsalvageable on Ceres.

He stood there for a moment, staring at the hole. One piece of rock, smaller than his fingertip, traveling faster than a bullet. It had hit during transit or early setup, and they’d been running on three processors ever since.

He checked the production logs. Three processors had kept the reserves full during automated operations. Three was enough.

He made his way to his office and stopped in the doorway. The L desk. The storage locker. The soft chair. He’d walked this room a thousand times in his head, checking sight lines, testing layouts, and memorizing every detail. Now it was real. The door had his name stenciled on it. Dr. Silas Knox, Captain.

He sat down, pulled off his helmet, and queued a voice packet to Earth. “Hi, Catherine. This is Silas. We arrived. Air leak in habitat unit fixed, habitat pressurized. One water processor is shot. Water reserves are full.”

She wouldn’t receive it for half an hour. He sat for a few moments before going to dinner. He could smell lithium grease in the air.

The people pulling kitchen duty had found unexpected items in the freezer: cake and ice cream. The cake said, “Welcome to Ceres.” Knox had sent that with the cargo ship years ago. It had come with the supplies and sat frozen for six months in the mess hall freezer. The men cheered when they brought it out. There was plenty for seconds.

After dinner, Knox stood on a table. “We are here to get metal. The first people to set foot on an asteroid.” He pumped his fist in the air.

His voice shifted register. “Don’t get carried away with the excitement of it all. Asteroids are more unpredictable than any place you have mined before. Keep your heart rate low.”

Knox paused in silence. “I designed our system. I will lead the first crew. I need three volunteers.”

Three others slowly stepped forward.

Knox logged the names and sent the list to Dahl, his own name at the top.

They prepped in silence. Gear was checked, packs filled, timelines drawn. As the crew suited up, his terminal blinked. A private message had come in from Paris.

Knox read it alone.

Silas, you’re going to have to be the adult now. That whole first crew may be killed. If they die, your job is to make sure a second crew goes out and does it better. If you go and you’re lost, then everyone on Ceres dies. That’s the math. Stay put.

He stared at the screen, jaw locked. Verified the timestamp.

Radio delay meant he didn’t need to respond right away. He could think first, or pretend he didn’t get the message until after he left.

Knox put on his suit and the leader armband. He read the nametag. Dr. Silas Knox, Captain. He strode into the mess hall and watched the three volunteers. They were calmly waiting for his direction.

Then he looked at the rest of the crew. Forty-six other men. The cleanup crew was hauling the plates back after dinner. Their faces looked the same.

If he died, what would happen to them? He looked around the room.

He removed the leader armband and returned his attention to the crew. “I need a fourth,” he said.

Lennox stepped forward. Knox gave him the armband.

Knox pulled up the regional map. A target was marked in red on the display.

Knox explained the plan. “Feld believes this rock has tungsten ore. We are going to find out. You will be on that rock for one month. You will be bringing a thousand bags and a hundred cups, stacked just like a pack of Dixie cups on top of the extractor. Fill the bags, tie them off, connect them to the tether, and let the extractor pull them back to itself. The bags hold ten tons. Gravity is weak, so they feel light. You can lift them. Don’t be fooled. They retain their momentum, so the key is to let it move slowly. The cups hold ten bags each, twenty when you screw them together. You load that tumbler pack onto the extractor’s mass driver.”

“How are we going to lift two hundred tons onto the top of the extractor?”

“Gravity is weak. The tumbler will weigh about fifteen pounds. Get Jameson to help if it is too much.” Joe looked at Jameson.

A soft chuckle went through the room.

Knox continued. “Right now, our target is approaching. It will reach periapsis in a month. At that time, you come home. Get the job done. That asteroid will drift farther and farther away, so we won’t be able to access it again for decades.’

The first crew boarded. The target was loaded into the extractor’s autopilot, and the ship calculated its own approach. They came in slowly and smoothly. No burns inside two kilometers. Hall effect thrusters hissed in short bursts, guiding them toward the sunlit ridge. The surface emerged slowly, revealing a rugged texture of silicate rock interspersed with metallic veins. The asteroid bore the scars of countless impacts. Dusty hues of gray and brown mingled, highlighting the mineral richness in its ancient crust.

They coasted forward until a flat segment emerged, broad enough for gear deployment. Landing gear touched with a whisper. They were on the asteroid.

The crew stayed strapped in, eyes on their readouts. With sub-milligee gravity, even a shift in posture could break contact. The extractor balanced on three legs, each tipped with a fast-spinning, low-bite drill. The bits skated, then caught. The frame hummed as the drills bored downward, slow and steady. No weight to help them, only friction and time. Once the holes were cut, the anchors extended and began threading into place. One turn at a time, the ship moored itself. When the procedure was complete, a green light came on. They were fixed to the rock.

The sigh of relief was audible as they unstrapped. They checked their maps again, running overlays, marking terrain features, and blind zones. Then they clipped in, one by one, and, hearts racing, fanned out over the surface.

Moving slowly enough was difficult. A few bounced off the surface, rising into long, slow arcs. Tethers snapped taut. They reeled themselves back slowly and tried again.

The footing was unreliable, with loose regolith in some places, hard plate in others. They moved carefully, staying low, using gloved hands for balance and traction.

At a ridge near his waypoint, Lennox steadied himself and unholstered his survey laser. A short pulse traced the edge of a visible seam, heating the surface until a fracture opened along an old fault. He waited, tapped it with a glove. The split held.

He reached back, retrieved a cam, and worked it into place. Locked. He tested it, then moved on, slow and low, anchored with each step.

At the next site, he used the laser again to chip a sample, dropping it into the mass spectrometer. Seconds later, data reached the extractor and was relayed to Ceres.

Knox read it: scheelite.

He forwarded it to Earth but didn’t wait for confirmation. He keyed back: “Tungsten ore. Begin harvest.”

Lennox returned to the extractor and retrieved a ten-ton ore bag, drill, expanders, and saw. Moving one handhold at a time, he made his way back, anchored the saw, verified the clamps, and powered it up.

The blade bit deep. He followed, inserting hydraulic expanders, breaking rock, and packing it into the bag. At the end of the first cut, he tied the first bag shut and sent a signal to the extractor to pull it in. Slowly, the bag inched away from him.

He shifted the saw a meter, and started it again. He went back to cutting and inserting expanders. From the corner of his eye, he noticed something drifting. The saw had cut through his tether smoothly. He stood reflexively to grab it, but he stood too fast. He continued up, his boots losing contact with the asteroid.

“I’m drifting,” he said over the radio.

That was when the others looked up.

One miner froze. Another dropped to all fours and began crawling toward the ridge, pulling hand over hand. A third unhooked a tether line and threw it. The shot was wide, but, under tension, the working end moved towards Lennox.

“I’m close,” Lennox said, reaching for the line. It spun inches out of reach.

A second tether fell short.

“Still got eight hours of air,” he said. “Plenty of time to think.”

Silence followed.

The miner who’d been crawling reached Lennox’s cut site. The saw was set firmly, as solid as the extractor. He clipped himself to the saw housing and pulled a tether from his pack. He jumped toward Lennox, paying out line as he went.

As he got close to the end of his own tether, he threw another line to Lennox. The throw was accurate. The working end was going straight towards Lennox. The reaction from the throw sent the rescuer tumbling backward. Suddenly, the rescue line snapped taut. The working end of the tether pulled back when it was about ten feet from Lennox. Close enough to see the clip spinning.

The rescuer reeled himself back in, repositioned, and tried again. This time, the line was thirty feet short. Lennox continued his slow drift, now over a hundred meters out.

Eventually, the crew turned back.

Bags were filled, sealed, and labeled. One by one, they were brought to the extractor’s rail. The extractor calculated trajectories and launched each in a low delta-v path toward Ceres.

Four hours later, drifting cold and clear, Lennox understood the arc wouldn’t return him.

He unlatched the survey laser from his belt.

“The site’s solid,” he said. “Keep harvesting.”

Then he angled the beam and fired once through his own faceplate.

The transmission stopped.

The miner anchored to Lennox’s saw watched the tether drift. “Alright. Back to work.”

Back on Ceres, the crew had begun preheating one of the smelters. They were expecting the first tumbler to arrive the next day. The crew moved slowly in thermal suits, checking and rechecking seals and insulation. Every motion was deliberate. Hot mirrors redirected much of the radiated energy back into the furnace, keeping the crew just outside the danger line.

Water use was constant. Cooling circuits, steam traps, and intake purges. Knox tracked it all by hand, logging fluctuations. The system was closed-loop, but not perfect. Recovery was never one hundred percent.

By mid-shift, the reserves weren’t rebounding, despite continuous cycling.

He ran a full diagnostic. Another water processor was nonfunctional. Corrosion had eaten through a weld where the titanium plating had flaked away. The remaining processors would also have a limited lifetime before the corrosive water ate through their welds, too.

He stared at the readouts, checked them again. The station could function with three processors. Two would force rationing and a slower mining cadence. Hydrolox production, drinking water, food systems, and, as they saw now, thermal regulation for smelter operations were all affected.

He drafted a notice to Dahl.

Second water processor dead. Two functional but strained. Corrosion failure - titanium plating compromised. Remaining units likely on same timeline. Water more corrosive than anticipated. We’re in trouble. Have not shared this with the team.

The response came three hours later.

I saw the telemetry. Keep mining.

Dahl’s message confirmed Knox’s decision. The station existed to extract metal; nothing else was guaranteed.

A second crew was sent while the first crew was still working. Another target, another haul. Tantalum this time, mixed with niobium on a stony asteroid.

In Paris, Dahl stared at the water system logs for hours. The text blurred. Her window overlooked the Seine, but sound, color, and distance all felt muted. She thought about Lennox. She put away her slate and left her apartment.

She walked down the street, turning where it was unfamiliar. A few blocks later, after a couple more turns, she found a bar she didn’t recognize. She walked in, looking to see if she recognized anyone there.

She ordered a carafe of the house red, downing her first glass in a single gulp. The second went down more slowly. Her shoulders loosened. She drank again.

Her eyes fixed on an old poster on the wall, with a redheaded woman wearing a white hat, then returned to her wine glass.

“Of course, Ceres is dangerous. They knew. It’s not my fault.” She sipped her fourth.

“Why Lennox?”

The couple next to her moved to a farther table.

“The reserve tank is full. I hate corrosion. The manufacturer promised me it wouldn’t corrode.”

“I can’t send anything. I don’t have a rocket, and the transfer window won’t open for seventeen months. You think I wouldn’t? Why is it corroding?”

The bartender asked her to keep it down. She ordered another carafe.

“They can’t just stop. Do nothing, wait to die? They can keep pulling metal. I’m not a monster. I am giving them purpose. I’d send them the Seine.”

“They’re all going to die up there. I just don’t know how fast.”

Another patron moved closer to her. “Is it water?” he asked.

She turned. He was sitting at the bar, finishing a beer. She hadn’t noticed him before.

“That’s part of it,” she said. “It’s all of it.”

“What would it be worth, if I could get you water?”

She blinked. Laughed, briefly. “What, like magic?”

“Let’s just imagine it’s possible,” he said. “What would the number be?”

She studied his face. Calm, curious. She pulled a napkin from the bar and wrote on it.

One hundred tons. Pure. FOB destination: Ceres. Weekly.

She paused, then added the numbers and signed it.

Two hundred dollars per kilogram. Paid on arrival.
Catherine Dahl, Helion Dynamics.

He put the napkin in his pocket, and the two of them drank and talked through the night.
