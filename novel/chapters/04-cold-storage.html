Chapter 4

Cold Storage

2,094 words · about 9 minutes

Price of ³He$8,000,000/kg
Current Production18 tons/year
Current Consumption12 kg/year
Time Since Start18 months

Tobias Raines sat in his office at Helion Dynamics headquarters in Geneva, looking out over Jet d’Eau in Lac Léman. The bells at Saint-Pierre marked the hour. His policy was no meetings before nine, but today he had a visitor from China, where it was already noon.

Energy Minister Chen Zhengrong walked in with three assistants just as the sixth bell pealed.

“Good morning, Mr. Raines,” Chen said. “Our premier sends his greetings. He wished to attend personally, but was caught up in internal affairs.”

“Good morning, Mr. Chen. What can I do for you?” Raines asked.

“Fusion power is an enticing concept, but our concern is stability. We have been watching the Lisbon performance data.”

“We publish the Ilha Fusão logs specifically to allow verification.”

“Yes, of course, Mr. Raines. We have reviewed your extraordinary logs. We can see routine maintenance ongoing, without a flicker in voltage or power output since operations began eighteen months ago. This stability is what has piqued our interest.”

“Fantastic. You’ve done your homework. Several countries have purchased our ten-gigawatt tokaplex. The first one, in Argentina, will go live soon. Would you like to purchase one of those? We have strict requirements on infrastructure, which we can go over…”

“No, Mr. Raines. We do not wish to purchase a ten-gigawatt tokaplex.” Chen folded his hands. “We have read your documentation on required infrastructure, and for China, we find that to be inadequate. Instead, we would like to purchase a one-terawatt tokaplex.”

“Mr. Chen, our production of the ten-gigawatt tokaplexes is deeply streamlined. All major components are produced on an assembly line in Biloxi. We can get one set up for you quickly.”

“No, Mr. Raines. You do not understand our needs. We wish to centralize production, freeing our country to use electricity in new and better ways. We call it hyperelectrification. With your terawatt tokaplex, we will be able to provide the power needed to enable that step forward.”

“That’s… forward-thinking.”

“It is necessary. Ten-gigawatt facilities are insufficient for our scale. We would begin with one terawatt installation. Operational validation. Then expand. Eventually, we would expect several such plants.”

“Mr. Chen, our terawatt facilities have not proven as popular yet with other nations. I’m not certain the infrastructure specifications have yet been made public.”

“We would appreciate your alacrity in this matter,” Chen said. “China will do what we can to help. Our engineers can draft infrastructure proposals to move the power out across the country. And we can assure you that you will not face any bureaucratic difficulties.”

“Mr. Chen, as I’m sure you’re aware, we have not yet made a final determination on the price of such facilities. I’ll get back to you with that…”

“Yes, of course, Mr. Raines. However, our premier wishes to assure you of our commitment to the future. He has sent a note with me.”

Chen handed Raines a red envelope. Inside was a card with an account number with a Hong Kong bank, a PIN, and a balance.

$500,000,000,000.

He kept his hand steady as he folded it and slipped it into his jacket pocket.

“Please tell the premier that we appreciate his commitment and will put this together now. I’m sure you realize we have a significant backlog…”

“The premier wishes to tell you this is not a down payment, but a gift for your consideration in this matter.”

Chen rose, they shook hands, and his assistants filed out behind him.

Raines stood at the window, looking out over the lake. His slate was already buzzing. Meetings, messages, a dozen fires needing attention.

He called Elin.

Elin’s answer cut right through all the pleasantries. “Raines, why are you calling so late? It’s almost midnight. I am at my wits’ end! We’re out of molybdenum. Production stops in two days without a resupply.”

“Elin, I am so glad you are still up. I will be arriving in Biloxi tomorrow at noon. I would love to see you while I am there. Molybdenum? Let me check into that.”

He pulled up his calendar. A meeting with India in three hours. Egypt that afternoon. Poland and South Africa tomorrow.

He canceled them all.

A half hour later, his Gulfstream was still climbing when he reached Miguel Flores, a metals broker in Lima.

“Miguel, I need molybdenum.”

“Tobias, it’s so nice to hear from you. Unfortunately, you have caught us at a bad moment. We have eighteen tons here, but it’s already been sold to a buyer in Shanghai.”

Raines said nothing.

“Let me make a call.”

Raines touched down at the Gulfport airport. He waited for the molybdenum to arrive, got it loaded in a semi, and went to find Elin in Back Bay. She was giving a talk to newly hired welders when he arrived.

“Elin, wonderful to see you. I found some moly to tide you over. Eighteen tons,” he said, gesturing to the truck.

Elin got a crew to unload the truck.

“I appreciate this, Tobias. We have five hundred tons coming from South Africa next week, but we have improved our production cadence. We’re building faster than the supply contracts anticipated.”

“That’s the third time this month.”

“I know. Every time we improve the process, every time we get faster, we burn through materials faster. The contracts can’t keep up. Production here has been a tornado.”

“That tornado has been a miracle, Elin. I can’t believe how much you have done. Eighteen months ago, this was bare ground.”

Tobias looked around at her manufacturing facility. It looked nothing like a factory. The grounds stretched for miles in every direction. Six cooling towers stood in various stages of completion. The cranes moved overhead, welders beneath sending up showers of sparks, heat rising through the salt air. In the distance, they could see the metal storage yards, large Quonset huts filled with refractory and base metals.

“Argentina’s core is being loaded onto a barge now,” she said. “Japan’s will leave in a month. We’re moving, Tobias.”

They stepped inside one of the buildings. Raines watched as electricians wired up control panels.

“We can build every major component in about a year. With pipelining, our goal was to complete one per month. I am trying to catch up with orders, but the faster we move, the further we are behind. Each step forward brings its own headaches.”

Raines responded, “From your side.”

She stopped. “What does that mean?”

“You’re improving the process continually. Each improvement reveals new constraints, new risks, and new obstacles. From my side, it’s a cascade. Every plant we sell triggers demand for ten more. Interest becomes orders before we can prepare. I couldn’t slow this if I tried.”

“Maybe you should try,” she said quietly. “Let’s get some lunch.”

They took a car to the Beau Rivage.

The doorman greeted Elin by name when they arrived. Elin brought Raines to her favorite restaurant. “Coralline’s. They have great seafood and a private dining room.”

Raines sat down. “How do you like staying here?”

“It is close enough to the manufacturing site.”

The waiter left with their orders. Raines waited for the door to close. “I spoke with China yesterday. They want a terawatt tokaplex.”

Elin put her fork down. “Seriously?”

“Minister Chen was serious. They call it hyperelectrification. Centralized power production for the entire country.”

“That’s…” She pulled out her slate, already running calculations. “Tobias, that’s exactly what this technology is meant for. Economies of scale kick in at the terawatt level. The current design is a compromise. I favored simplicity and speed in construction to meet the volume. Moving up, though, I will be able to use direct energy capture. Efficiency improves dramatically, and non-fuel expenses will be similar to the current design. You could power…” She stopped, staring at the numbers on her slate. “Oh.”

“What?”

“Tungsten requirements.” She turned the slate toward him. “Eighty thousand tons per plant. Global annual production is under one hundred thousand.”

Raines nodded slowly. “I see.”

“And that’s just one material. Molybdenum, tantalum, and beryllium all scale the same way. Square-cube problem: a hundred times the power needs a thousand times the materials.”

“The manufacturing facility is designed for ten-gigawatt tokaplexes. We’re good at it. But terawatt? That’s not just bigger. It requires materials that don’t exist.”

“I understand.”

“I want to build it, Tobias. This is what fusion should be. But I can’t.”

“What if you didn’t have to worry about materials?”

She looked at him. “What does that mean?”

“China is working on infrastructure design. When they’re done, I want you to review them. Can you do that much? Not the real plant, but how they plan to distribute electricity.”

“Of course. But it won’t matter if there is no tokaplex to power it all.”

“In the meantime, I would like you to consider what a terawatt structure would look like. Not detailed, but more than an overview.”

“Design for when the materials magically appear?”

“When I figure out how to get them.”

She took a slow drink of carbonated water. “Okay,” she said finally. “I’ll indulge your fantasy. I will check China’s specs, and I will daydream about a terawatt facility. Is that enough?”

“That’s all I am asking.”

“Until then, I have a backlog of twenty tokaplexes. Before we are done with those, I expect you will drop another thirty on us. I have real work to do.”

After lunch, Raines returned to the airport. He had one more stop.

Dutch Harbor was gray and cold when his jet touched down. The working port stretched along the waterfront where fishing boats were unloading king crab, processing plants were running overtime, and warehouses were stacked with pallets of frozen seafood.

His facility looked like all the others. A low warehouse, maybe eight hundred square feet, with weathered siding, no signage. Cryogenic containers came off the boats, got wheeled inside, and others came back out empty and were reloaded on boats. Routine.

Today, Raines needed solitude. He walked in. The floor was ordinary. A few rows of 150 liter ³He cryobottles using ⁴He as a shield to prevent loss. Two workers in coveralls were moving helium tanks.

He went to the back office and closed the door.

This was a cold storage facility for bottles of ³He. Behind a partition, there was a compressor takeoff feeding an armored pipe. That pipe ran through bedrock into the North Pacific to the edge of the continental shelf. From there, it descended eight thousand meters into the Aleutian Trench, where the pressure reached twelve thousand psi. Three spheres, each the size of a city block, were filling with ³He, their flexible bladders displacing seawater as they filled.

Current level: 12.3 tons. Capacity: 600 tons.

Enough buffer capacity to run multiple terawatt plants for a year while lunar production scaled.

He opened a secure line to his purchasing team in Geneva.

“I need contracts,” he said. “Tungsten, tantalum, beryllium, titanium. Long-term agreements. I’m authorizing up to double the current spot prices. Our manufacturing facility can’t stop.”

“Understood. I’ll have proposals by end of week.”

He ended the call and pulled up Elin’s materials list.

For the ten-gigawatt plants, the contracts would work. Premium prices, aggressive terms, but the metals existed. He could buy them.

For terawatt-scale… he scrolled through the numbers. Tungsten requirements: 80,000 tons per plant. Global annual production: 95,000 tons. China wanted one now, but would probably want three more as soon as they got that one. Then all the major economies. These were no longer replacing old generators or expanding generation. Hyperelectrification would change everything.

But the math didn’t work.

He could stockpile helium. He could lock in metal contracts for current production. He could keep Elin supplied, keep the ten-gigawatt plants rolling out, keep the orders coming.

But terawatt? He pulled Chen’s hongbao from his jacket.

He had no way to deliver what they wanted.

He sat there for a long time, staring at the numbers.

Then he walked outside. The wind off the Bering Sea was sharp and cold. The dock was empty. Just the ocean, the cold, and the problem he couldn’t fix.

He stood there, hands in his pockets, until the light began to fade. There was no moon tonight.

He walked back to the airport. There was no way on Earth, and the moon didn’t have the refractory metals he needed. That would not stop him.
