Part II · Jovian Resource Corporation

Chapter 24

The Seine

2,291 words · about 10 minutes

Max Juno stepped into Catherine Dahl’s apartment and immediately moved to the tall windows overlooking the Seine. “God, Catherine, this view. How do you get any work done?”

“You get used to it,” Catherine said, as she set down two cups of coffee on the small kitchen table. “Though I admit, some mornings I just stand there watching the boats.”

The apartment had the comfortable, lived-in feel of someone who’d actually made Paris home rather than just renting space. Books in French and English lined the shelves. Her dishes appeared to be handcrafted. Max wondered where in her travels she had found them. The pictures on her wall were mostly of friends in amazing places. Max thought about all really incredible projects she had been a part of. One photo was of Dr. Knox on Ceres with a smelter in the background.

Max settled into the chair across from her, cradling his coffee. “So how are things on Ceres? Your last reports sounded optimistic.”

Catherine’s expression grew animated as she pulled her slate toward them. “More than optimistic. I want to show you what we’ve accomplished.” She turned the screen so Max could see an aerial view of the ice mining operation. “Four massive steel piers screwed deep into the asteroid’s surface, forming a square a mile across.”

Max leaned forward, studying the image. He could see tiny figures of workers near the base of one pier for reference.

“We’ve got cables running between the piers,” Catherine continued, swiping to show the system in operation. “We use them to pull scrapers across the area. Regular scrapers wouldn’t have enough traction in the low gravity, but this cable system lets us load up enormous amounts of ice with each pass.”

“That’s ingenious. Whose idea was that?”

“Knox’s team worked it out after we lost too many conventional vehicles to poor traction. Sometimes the worst constraints force the best solutions.”

Catherine swiped again, showing processing facilities that looked like a cross between a chemical plant and a foundry. “Here’s where it gets interesting. We take that ice and separate everything. carbonates, phosphates, ammonia, other salts and minerals. But Max, the volume of pure water we’re extracting is enormous. Forty tons per day of double distilled water.”

Max felt something cold settle in his stomach. “Forty tons a day?”

“Plus about six tons of organic compounds as byproducts. The process is so efficient we’re running out of storage space.” Catherine’s enthusiasm was evident, but Max was struggling to focus on her technical explanations. Forty tons of water per day meant…

“I was thinking,” Catherine continued, “we could send the phosphates and ammonia to Jakob. Those could boost the agricultural yields.”

Max managed a nod, though his throat felt tight. “I imagine so. Jakob’s team makes ammonia now, but that might let them expand.” But she was talking oddly. If their water processor was working so well, what was Mars doing for Ceres?

“That’s great. I’ll let Dr. Knox know!” she enthused. “Since we started this water mining operation, Ceres has become much more viable. I am planning to expand operations. We can support two hundred more crew.”

Catherine set down the slate and her expression grew more serious. “Max, I need to be direct with you about something. We’re going to end the water contracts. Also, the block carbon contracts.”

The words hit him like a physical blow. Max stared at her, his mind reeling. Those contracts were the foundation of everything they’d built on Mars. Without them…

“Catherine, those contracts are our entire business.”

“I know.” Catherine’s voice was gentle but firm. “And I can’t tell you how grateful we are for everything Jakob accomplished. If it weren’t for his team, the Ceres project would have failed. My crew would have died on Ceres and never delivered metal.”

Max felt like the room was tilting. He could already picture Jakob’s face when he heard. This would cut him deep, yet Catherine was leaving it to Max to deliver the news.

“We’ll work out fair compensation for the contract cancellations. We will pay for everything in transit and the value of one additional year to sever the contract,” Catherine continued. “We simply don’t need any more water or carbon shipments. Stop today. Ceres is completely self-sufficient now, as we had always planned to be.”

Max stared down at his coffee, watching the steam rise. His mind was racing through implications. Martian operations had been built around those enormous, steady, growing orders from Ceres.

“I’ll let Jakob know,” he said quietly.

Catherine nodded. “I know this is difficult. I can’t thank you enough for what you have done. We will continue to support you. Ceres can help Mars. We make plenty of steel now, but it is not worth shipping to Earth. And the ammonia and phosphates I mentioned. We can ship those to you free of charge, for old times sake.”

Max didn’t have anything to say. He was still thinking about how this would affect Jakob and his team.

Catherine paused, then slid a different document across the table. “There is something else I need to discuss.”

Max looked down at the contract. Food procurement. The numbers made him blink and look again.

“You want food for two hundred people? Three meals a day?”

“Two hundred and growing. Max, you can’t imagine what your food shipments have meant to Dr. Knox’s team. The bacteria and yeast slurry is horrible. I tried drinking that stuff once and gagged. That’s what they are eating three meals a day except when Jakob’s meals arrive. What Jakob’s been sending from Mars, real vegetables, actual meat, and that wonderful amaranth bread is not just nutrition. It’s sanity. It’s hope. It’s the difference between existing and living. That one meal per week is the world to them.”

Max studied the payment terms, his shock slowly giving way to amazement. The figures were extraordinary, more than he’d ever imagined food could command.

“The crew fights over portions,” Catherine continued. “They plan their days around meals. What you’ve built on Mars isn’t just farming, you’ve created something that makes life in deep space bearable.”

Max looked up from the contract, his mind spinning. “This is… Catherine, this is worth more than the water and carbon contracts combined.”

She smiled. “Good food is priceless when you’re stuck in the asteroid belt.”

Max sat at the table a moment, studying the numbers. He stood and he and Catherine shook hands. “Catherine, we are all excited by what you have been able to accomplish in the Asteroid Belt. And we appreciate your generous terms for closing the contract. I will discuss the food contracts with Jakob. I expect he will be as excited as I am.”

They stepped together for a brief bise. “Good-bye, Catherine. And thank you.” He then turned to walk down the stairs.

He crossed the street and stepped down onto the quai where he turned west. He replayed the conversation in his head as he walked. The original Mars business was dead but other businesses were growing. Pacha and his demands for greenhouses and livestock had always been a distraction. On the Moon and Ceres, people ate algae. That Martians had complex agriculture had always seemed like a distraction, a small tax he had to pay to keep them happy. That distraction was now becoming the main business. Mars was becoming the mid solar system’s grocery store, selling to the Moon and Ceres.

Callum’s mine had played out. He made billions from it, but it was done. Instead of coming home, Callum found a second beryllium vein. And copper. Copper was not rare enough to justify shipping to Earth, but, as CuBe, it worked. And Callum also found minerals that Pacha wanted to add to the soil in the greenhouses, improving the yield.

As Max walked along the Seine, he watched the water flow beside him. Upwellings would spread out and move downstream. He couldn’t find a pattern to it, but could see the dynamic.

Max was still mulling the implications when he stepped up off the quai and crossed the street to the Le Puits Perdu. He noticed they now featured “Astran Cuisine,” made with Martian ingredients. That food came from his team. How did they get it?

Max had never tried to be controlling, but he didn’t realize this was going on. Jakob never said anything. Jakob wasn’t there to ship ice. Jakob was a person.

Jakob had listened to Catherine in a bar. He would listen to others. They aren’t pieces in his puzzle. They are actors.

Emmanuel greeted Max when he stepped inside and showed him to the back where Tobias Raines was waiting.

Max took him aside for a moment before meeting Raines. “Emmanuel, I notice the Astran cuisine. Has Raines ordered yet?”

“No, he has not.”

“Then I want two Astran plates. What would you pair that with?”

“With amaranth chicha brewed on the Moon.”

“Perfect. Let me just pay now.”

“Of course, sir. That will be forty two thousand Euros.”

Max drew in his breath.

Then paid the bill.

As he made his way to the back room, Raines stood to greet him. “Max, good to see you. How was your meeting with Catherine?” Raines asked, sitting back into his seat. “How’s the Mars business?”

“A disaster,” Max explained. “The Cerean water business is the entire reason we went to Mars. That business is completely dead. So is the Cerean carbon business. But other businesses are growing, especially food. Financially, it’s the best day we have had so far.”

Raines studied the numbers. “The only reason your team ever grew food on Mars was for survival rations. When you came to me, I was impressed with how thoughtful your team was about food and shelter. You have fantastic housing compared to anyone else in space.”

“Yeah, I am constantly impressed by this team. They are making improvements in everything they do. Their new housing is much safer, agriculture is more automated, water collection is easier.”

“Why are you having them spend so much time on that instead of their business.”

“I’ve been asking myself that same thing. But I was looking at it all wrong.”

“You’ve been pretty successful for someone looking at it all wrong.”

“I thought Jakob had found a good opportunity. That’s not what he did. Jakob put a farmer next to a builder next to a mechanic next to an electrician, and set them loose on civilization. When we added a miner, it didn’t add a revenue stream. The entire team became more powerful.” Max leaned in. “Mars can do more. I am going to add two new groups.”

“What are you thinking of?”

“I’ve been talking to a chemical engineer, Dr. Marc Lafevre, an assistant professor at the Sorbonne. I met with him last time I was in Paris. He wants to set up polyethylene production on Mars. Ellie is making some, as she needs it, but he wants to have actual chemical factories. We’ve got abundant carbon and oxygen from the atmospheric processors, some nitrogen in the air, and we are careful to recycle everything. With Callum’s mining operation pulling rare earths as byproducts, we could build a real chemical industry.”

Raines was quiet for a moment, processing. “That takes serious equipment.”

Emmanuel brought their meals.

Raines looked up.

“This is the Astran plate, sir. Roast cavy, baked oca, amaranth salad with lunar microgreens, amaranth bread rolls, and chicha made from amaranth grown on Mars, fermented on the Moon,” Emmanuel said. “Enjoy.”

“I saw that on the menu, food raised by my team, and I had to have some. We should know what they are eating,” Max said, picking up his cup. “Cheers.”

They both took a drink. “Creamy,” Raines said.

Max continued talking. “Yes, the equipment for the chemical industry will be exorbitant, and shipping will triple that, at least. I am planning on using Andrei’s new ships.”

Raines paused his fork with cavy meat. “Are the ships ready yet?”

“The first standard cargo ship will be rolling out in six months. I plan to hire it.”

Raines nodded and chewed.

Max continued. “When you are in Paris, where do you stay?”

“Le Maurice.”

“That is a very nice hotel. I was staying there recently, and met the director of operations, Bastien Laurent. He is fascinated by Mars, and wants to build a hotel there. Not rough crew barracks. He wanted to make a destination.“

Raines finished a bite of salad. “A resort on Mars?”

“The first interplanetary destination. Hotel, casino, spa, maybe some kind of theme park. Think about it. Where do crews from Ceres go for leave? Where do their families live while they’re working the asteroids? And when JRC finally tackles Jupiter…”

Raines was nodding slowly. “Mars becomes the staging ground.”

Max continued. “And the leisure hub. Mining Ceres is brutal. Working Jupiter will be worse. But if crews know they can take leave on Mars, hit a casino, catch a show, see their families…” Max spread his hands. “Mars becomes indispensable.”

“The JRC is moving slowly,” Raines said, half to himself. “But if they ever are ready for serious Jovian operations, they’ll need infrastructure. Mars can provide that.”

Raines smiled. “You’ve been thinking about this for a while.”

“It’s been on my mind since I signed Callum,” Max said leaning forward. “But I didn’t understand it until after I talked to Catherine.”

“This is going to stretch you, Max. Can you cover it?”

“It will be tight. Andrei is stretched too. But we’re both excited.”

Raines called out. “Emmanuel, another round of chichas!”

After Emmanuel poured, Raines lifted his glass. “Vive Mars!”
