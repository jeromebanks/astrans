Chapter 11

Martian Ice

4,128 words · about 17 minutes

Five weeks after his meeting with Max Juno, Jakob stood alone in the cargo bay as the last of his team trickled in.

The ship was built for function, not comfort. The five of them were packed in with their equipment and a few cavies in cages that reeked of hay and ammonia. The seed stock and tubers were sealed in insulated crates. Three air recyclers stood lined up against the wall. One was all they needed, but if anything went wrong, help was years away.

They gathered in the main bay, where Jakob had been staging equipment for a week. The warehouse still felt temporary. Crates lined the walls. Cables coiled like snakes. The air smelled of steel, plastic wrap, and lubricant.

Jakob let them settle. No introductions. No speeches. Just the team and the tools.

“We have one shot at this. Don’t screw it up.”

His voice was neutral, tired, stripped of anything unnecessary.

“Max has paid for this cargo hull and all our equipment. He bought a rocket from Lunar Tug that they were not using. Helion Dynamics graciously fronted us the money to get this all into LEO, where we will connect to that rocket and head to Mars.”

“This isn’t a dry run. There’s no backup. Mars doesn’t care if we make it. If something breaks, Max might be able to send a replacement, but launch windows only open every twenty-six months.”

He gave them time, then continued. “If you don’t think we can do this, you shouldn’t be here. If you do, then you already know what’s required: working without a net. Each of us is doing more than we were trained for. Living with whatever comes. But if we pull it off, if we send clean blocks of ice, hit delivery windows, and hold the contract, then we become the first Martian exporters. More than that, we have a chance to build something real up there. And maybe, down the line, find a way back. On our terms.”

He looked at each of them in turn. “Got it?”

They nodded, one by one.

Mira cracked a grin. “You won’t have to worry about me.”

It broke the tension just enough. Jakob didn’t return the smile, but he moved forward.

There was no time to waste. Each person checked their own manifest, then verified someone else’s. No second chances.

They rode a heavy-lift to low Earth orbit in their cargo hull. Mira loved the heavy acceleration, then weightlessness. Tom worked through the transfer sequence, moving them in their cargo hull from the heavy lift rocket to a rocket Lunar Tug no longer wanted. This one would take them to Mars.

Mira was excited. “I love the feeling of acceleration. I can’t wait for our burn to Mars. It just totally crushed me down into the gravity couch last time!”

Jakob humored her, counting down to launch. “Five, four, three, two, one, go.”

Tom threw the switch, and the hydrolox rockets lit with a roar. The gentle push barely registered. The look of disappointment on Mira’s face brought laughter from the rest.

“Not what you expected, Mira?” Jakob asked. “This is a shuttle rocket, not heavy lift. It will burn for an hour, but at 1/10th G, enough to insert us into a Hohmann transfer orbit to Mars. After this, it will be six months of true weightlessness.”

Mira pouted.

The time was spent in tight, unchanging air. The ship was the size of a bus, but none of the space belonged to them. Every surface was spoken for: cable runs, storage racks, sealed containers, and equipment. Every tool, part, and system they would need was packed inside with them. There was no separate cargo, no second flight, no margin for error. The entire mission was compressed into this one hull.

They slept belted into stacked bunks and moved through narrow corridors. It was warm and humid, so they dressed in the bare minimum. Most days, just underwear.

There were no showers. Just sealed hand wipes.

The first time Ellie had to clean herself, she wedged into the corner by the water recycler, back to everyone, and worked fast. Her shoulders were tight. No one said anything, but everyone looked away. Studied their screens. Checked equipment they’d already checked.

She pulled her shirt back on immediately after, still damp.

“It’s fine,” she muttered to no one. “It’s fine.”

By week three, she stopped retreating to the corner. Just wiped down at her bunk, quick and mechanical. Still wore the underwear. Still kept her back turned.

By month two, the underwear started to smell. Everyone’s did. The fabric held moisture and body oil with nowhere for it to go. The water purifier was not built for laundry. Pacha stopped wearing his first. He seemed completely natural that way. Mira followed a week later.

Ellie held out longest.

Month three, Jakob woke to find her sitting on the edge of her bunk, underwear balled in her fist, staring at nothing.

“There’s no point,” she said quietly.

She tossed it into the waste bag and didn’t put another pair on.

After that, no one wore anything unless they were cold. Hair matted. Skin dried and cracked. The air smelled like polymer, machine grease, and breath. No one mentioned it, but everyone noticed.

Ellie stopped turning away when she cleaned herself. Just did it, same as eating, same as checking the boards.

One morning, Jakob caught her looking out the tiny porthole at Mars, growing slowly brighter in the distance.

“Can’t wait to have a door again,” she said.

Jakob nodded. “Yeah.”

“Dinner time!” Pacha called out cheerfully.

No one was happy. Food came in pouches, nutrient-balanced and soft. No flavor, no texture. No one wanted to eat. You ate because the others did. They were all losing weight.

Tom didn’t like that. He and Mira set up a stand to hold a cargo bicycle in place and connected it to some paddles, like an assault bike. Everyone got thirty minutes a day. Jakob hated it, but he knew Tom was right. As their leader, he showed enthusiasm he didn’t feel.

They also used resistance bands. Not the same as real weights, but it slowed how fast they were wasting. Ellie kept their schedules for resistance bands. Push, pull, legs, rest.

Time passed in repetition with seemingly no progress. Same lights. Same air. Same bodies. Nothing changed. No distance closed. They slept and woke in the same space. Thought the same thoughts. Waited for the gravity to shift.

The only way to be alone was to close your eyes and stop reacting. Then Jakob made an announcement. “We are about to do our slowdown. Watch out the window.”

Mars was big. No longer a red dot, the landscape was clearly visible. And, as they watched, the craft tumbled. A controlled and slow reversal, the craft now pointed away from Mars, and the engines came on. They ramped up slowly, giving the crew the feeling of gravity. Softly, for about ten minutes at a tenth of a G as they performed a Mars orbital insertion. Tom put them in a highly eccentric orbit. They stayed in that orbit, heating at periapsis as they entered the atmosphere, cooling during apoapsis. The heating cycles grew longer each time, and the cooling cycles shorter.

Then the real entry. The heat shield was ablating as they came in. The whole frame began rumbling. Jakob kept his hand on the parachute deployment lever, while Tom watched the gauges. At Mach 6.0, Tom called out, and Jakob pulled the lever to deploy the high-speed parachute. When it unfurled, it felt like an impact as the craft was suddenly decelerating at two G. Mira didn’t like it this time. The G force decreased as the square of the speed. Tom kept a steady eye on the gauges. At Mach 2.0, the force had decreased to about Martian gravity. Tom called out, and Jakob pulled the second lever. The high-speed chute detached and drifted away, and the much larger medium chute deployed, putting them back to two G. Pacha threw up, but no one could clean anything. When they got to 1G, Tom called out for the final chute. It was enormous, but they had already slowed significantly. They only reached 3G. As they neared the landing site, Tom started the engines. This burn was harder than they had used for the Hohmann transfer orbit burns. The soft jolt of touchdown came like an afterthought, as if the ship had changed its mind at the last second and landed anyway. They sat at an angle, but the legs slowly adjusted until the deck was level. Everyone cheered when it stopped. Through the portholes, they saw a new world. Red, with a rosy sunrise. 75°S, 30°W. Smooth, near the edge of the ice cap.

Pacha began cleaning himself up. Mira came over and lent a hand.

Jakob felt the impact in his ribs before he felt it in his boots. The noise was low and wide as the ship settled. He stood too fast, while the legs were still leveling the ship. He cut his head on the cabin bulkhead and swore.

“First blood on Mars,” Ellie muttered as she cleaned him up and added a bandage. “Good omen.”

Jakob didn’t smile. He wasn’t here for moments. He was here to keep the mission from falling apart one piece at a time. He called them together. “OK, we have eight months of summer remaining. The ice cap is retreating, so each day we will go a little farther to collect. Be greedy. Pull as much as you can as fast as you can, because it is all going away anyway. We have thirteen days before we launch on the shortest transfer orbit to Ceres, and we have a lot to get done. The clock has already started.”

He posted the basic tasks, taping the list above the airlock.

Solar Panel Deployment
Dig foundation for Agriculture dome
Collect Ice in ten-ton blocks.
Deploy mass driver
Start growing crops
Build ag dome
Make fiberglass ablative boxes to carry the ice to Ceres

Sol 13: launch package 1, one hundred tons of pure ice, shortest transfer time to Ceres.

The solar arrays went up first. Nothing moved without power.

Tom and Pacha were out of the lock within minutes, boots crunching through red dust. The panels were flat and wide, packed awkwardly for transit. One caught halfway through its extension. Tom swore, dropped to one knee, and cleared the rail with a multitool.

“Dude. Dust already?” Pacha laughed as he drove a stake into the regolith a few meters away.

“Fine as talc,” Tom muttered. “Static pulls it into everything.”

“I bet the first storm is going to knock this all down,” Pacha smirked, watching a distant dust devil.

“Low atmospheric pressure means low force from wind,” Tom replied. “But when the first storm hits, I’ll be watching.”

He finished the bolts and moved to the field generator: carbon frame, three-phase, clipped into the central rack. When he flipped the switch, the panels hummed faintly, and a shimmer passed across the glass.

“The alternating electric field pushes dust off,” Tom said. “Indium tin oxide electrodes on the surface. A slow three-phase charge walks the grit downhill.”

Pacha watched the effect, the dust slipping downward in slow motion, as if peeled by invisible hands.

“Reduces the buildup,” Tom said. “We will still need to rinse the panels weekly to remove what this doesn’t.”

Pacha nodded. Then turned back to his line of panels and kept working.

Mira passed them without slowing. She’d pulled out the saw sled and had assembled the disk. Six pie-shaped wedges were screwed together to create an enormous wheel fifteen feet in diameter. She got it all assembled and went straight to the edge of the shelf.

“This ice better be worth it,” she said over the radio.

Jakob followed. The regolith cracked faintly under his boots. It didn’t feel like dirt. More like baked ash. He stopped before joining Mira. He found a shovel and went about a hundred yards off to dig. After ten minutes, he stepped back and took a picture for Max. The words “Magazine Beach” were dug into the regolith.

The shelf wasn’t flat. That was the first problem. The models had shown clean, horizontal strata. What they found was a tilted slab with wind-carved seams and dust-packed hollows.

Mira stood at the edge, eyeing the slope. “The grade is too steep,” she said. I can’t cut straight on that.”

Jakob reached for one of the adzes. “I’ll fix that.”

The adzes were carbon steel heads on collapsible shafts. Mira joined Jakob, knocking down the surface in ragged swings. Tom joined in. The crust came off unevenly in slabs, shards, and soft sections that crumbled like stale bread. Red dust had seeped into everything. Some layers were dark, almost black from meteorite soot. It wasn’t easy work or fast, but the surface had to be flat before the saw could track true.

Inside, Ellie was running diagnostics. The water recycler was green. Air mix: oxygen levels were good. CO₂ was low enough, hovering near 1600 ppm. The organics were too high, though. She tapped at the console like it had insulted her, lips tight, not speaking. Numbers came in slowly, sometimes delayed by dust interference. She looked at the catalytic cleaner, a U-shaped pipe packed with nickel dust. Countercurrent flow minimized heat loss. Normally run at 300°C, she set it at 500°C and turned up the fan. “This whole vessel needs to be scrubbed with bleach,” she thought.

Meanwhile, Pacha moved gear off the ship. Cargo that could be outside was stacked and covered with tarps staked against the wind. With space cleared in the aft bay, he began assembling the grow tent. Ellie watched as he snapped the framework together, section by section. She said nothing, but her expression softened.

Jakob keyed the team channel. “Tom, Pacha, thank you for getting the arrays live. This is summer here, so the sun won’t set for long, and twilight will persist much longer than we are used to. Too easy to work past the edge of exhaustion. From here on, we follow a schedule. Dinner, chat, then sleep. Your body won’t tell you when it’s tired, so we’ll use the clock instead. He let that settle, then continued. “Two weeks. We cut, refreeze, package, and launch ice for Ceres. If we miss it, we don’t get paid. And Ceres runs dry. This isn’t theoretical, it’s existential.”

The team line stayed quiet. They already knew the plan, but now in the light, they understood it.

Pacha called them to dinner. “Hey, guys, are you getting hungry? We are working now, so we need more calories. Eat up!”

There was some grumbling about the continued lack of meat. After a few minutes, Pacha spoke up, “We have limited feed pellets, so we won’t breed the cavies until their feed is growing. And not just algae.” He gestured toward the tent in the bay. “It is germinating now. Once we have an agriculture dome, I will plant. Give me twenty-five sols, and I’ll get us roots and leaves. Then we breed one sow. No more. Crops come in, we expand. Not before.”

Tom grumbled, but everyone understood. Rations would hold.

The sled creaked its way toward base, trailing a fine mist of powdered frost in its wake. Mira stood behind it, boots planted wide for stability, pushing hard. The traction assist wheels pulled, sometimes slipping. On top, the sled held a triangular prism of ice, seven feet high, twelve feet long, a mass of ten tons, faceted and glinting faintly in the sunlight. It looked like an enormous gemstone from a fairy tale.

She parked the sled with practiced slowness, locking the wheels into place beside the melt chamber, which Pacha had already hauled out. It was a pressure-sealed tank with water filters. Jakob stepped out to meet her, brushing ice dust off his gloves.

“Not bad for a first haul,” he said, voice crackling over the suit comms. “Smooth cuts.”

Mira smiled. “There was a seam full of rust-dust. I had to trim it on the fly with the adze.”

They both stared at the block for a moment. Jakob keyed the panel, and the chamber hissed open. Using the sled’s crane, they lifted the block up and into the chamber.

Inside, air pumps brought the pressure up to 1 bar, coils of heating wire glowed faintly red. The block started warming up from about a hundred degrees below zero up to where it could start melting. That operation was slow. As the water came off, it was filtered and poured into the massive freeze chamber, making an enormous lemon shape that would weigh a hundred tons when complete.

At night, their beds were uncomfortable. Gravity felt weird now. The ship creaked in ways no one liked, and the silence outside pressed in like a weight. With the equipment unloaded, they had a lot more space, though, which helped.

Jakob stayed up late. Max had pinged him from Lagos just before comms cutoff:

“Nice picture. I put it on my wall. Ice ships in twelve sols or you lose the money.”

No pressure. Outside, the ice shelf waited. Tomorrow, they would cut nine more blocks.

The second sol on Mars started with shovels. First, the foundation needed to be dug. They moved the top meter of regolith without encountering any large rocks. Next, they sorted out the finest dust. They filled the hopper of the 3D printer with that powder and set it to make foam. They laid out six inches of foam, about half fine powder and half polymer. The reddish-orange color looked beautiful to Ellie.

They let it cool for about an hour, then changed the settings. Now they were making the solid walls. Ellie jammed her shovel blade into the regolith, scooping up load after load, and dumped it into the intake hopper of their 3D printer. The dust didn’t puff like it would on Earth. It fell sluggishly, clinging to itself in dry clumps, static-charged and oddly heavy for something so fine. The printer’s auger rumbled beneath the housing, pulling the dirt toward the heating chamber.

Pacha stood at the controls, eyes on the temperature readouts. The polymer reservoir stayed just above the melt point, viscous and glassy, ready to bond with the crushed stone and dust. It made the regolith printable. Stronger than concrete once it cured, or so the specs claimed.

No dramatic hum of motors. No hiss of steam or whine of servos. Just the low mechanical growl of the extruder and the broad scrape of oversized shovels against broken stone.

The print pattern was simple: a low hemisphere, six meters wide, cut cleanly on one side for an airlock. Shelter from radiation and micrometeoroids. No windows. A transfer box with a pipe for air, water, and sewage. This was the agriculture dome, but they would be living in it for a while. After this came the equipment storage barn. Eventually, the habitat.

Ellie paused, leaning on her shovel with its wide blade waist-high, more like a snow scoop than a spade. In this gravity, it worked. The regolith was loose, fractured, and dry. She watched the curve rise slowly with each pass of the nozzle. It looked layered, almost clumsy, but she could already picture it finished. Not a home, exactly. But the first place on this planet built by hand and meant for life.

“Load’s holding,” Pacha said in his headset, voice clipped and dry. “Poly’s stable. We keep pace, we finish the shell by bedtime.”

Ellie nodded, though Pacha couldn’t see it. She just kept shoveling.

It was hard work. No gravity to help the shovel bite. Just pressure, leverage, and grit. But neither of them stopped. There was nowhere else to be, and nothing more important to do.

The sun hung high. No shadows moved. No breeze stirred the dust. Just the dome, growing layer by layer, like a promise they meant to keep.

By midmorning, they’d settled into a rhythm, trading roles when the one on the shovel was too tired to continue.

Pacha took the shovel, breathing slow and steady inside his suit as he fed the hopper with one scoop after another. Ellie stood at the printer’s interface, fingers flicking through the displays, keeping the temperature in the right band, watching the extrusion for skips or warps. The dome was waist-high now, a rough curve taking shape against the orange dirt.

From the east, a figure approached, slow and broad-shouldered in his suit. Tom. He dragged a sled behind him with a rectangular shape strapped tight beneath a dust blanket.

“Hey,” his voice came through their headsets, distorted a little by the local mesh relay. “Mass driver’s online. She hums like a dream. Got time for a door?”

Ellie glanced over and nodded. “We’re at the cutout. You’re just in time.”

Pacha stabbed his shovel into the ground and leaned on it, panting. “How many tons to Ceres?”

“None yet. But she’s aligned, powered, and ready. I’m gonna do a test launch tomorrow. Could hit a boulder on Deimos from here.”

Ellie raised an eyebrow. “Please don’t.”

Tom just chuckled and stepped around the printer. He pulled back the blanket and revealed the airlock, composite, pre-fabbed, pressure-ready. Not big. Just wide enough to crawl through in a suit. Airtight seals, no windows. Practical. Ugly but adequate.

He propped it next to the rising wall. “You want me to anchor it now, or wait?”

Ellie waved him over. “Do it before we print too high. We’ll match the layers around it.”

Tom got to work without another word, pulling out anchor rods and the little bolt gun they’d brought for this kind of thing. Ellie watched for a minute, then turned back to the readouts.

Polymer holding at 145°C. Flow rate steady. No signs of clog. She gave Pacha a nod. “Back at it.”

He groaned, picked up the shovel again, and dug.

“Your hole,” Tom said to Ellie, with a flourish after he finished bolting it in place. “I’m going to grab lunch and yell at Jakob about calibrating the mass driver scope. You two shovel heroes can finish the shell.”

Ellie checked the interface again. Still green. “We’ve got three more layers to seal the door. Then we start capping.”

Pacha kept shoveling. “Better get moving, then. Before Tom eats all the rations.”

“I didn’t think about that,” Ellie exclaimed and turned the machine to standby. They finished filling the hopper together, then started walking back to the ship.

Five more blocks had been cut by lunch. They were lined up by the processor, going in one at a time. Everyone was in high spirits as they ate. Mira talked about the ice. “That saw is a monster. Huge, and when it cuts, it is like a blizzard, but it cuts smoothly and fast. When we get the hang of it, I bet we can cut two hundred tons a day. I cut fifty so far.”

Tom jumped in. “The mass driver is ready to go. I tossed a rock with it slowly. It flew about ten miles. I will calibrate it tomorrow with a bigger push, but it is at least functional.”

After lunch, Pacha and Ellie returned to the printer, and Tom joined Jakob and Mira on the ice field. Jakob was starting on block seven. The sled saw ran smoothly. His movements were measured, calm, and smooth. Eyes on the cut line. Depth consistent. No rush. The shelf was cooperating.

Then the whine started. Jakob hit the kill switch immediately. Too late. The saw shuddered once, then died. A coil of white smoke unspooled from the motor housing.

“Mira,” he said into the comm. “We’ve got a problem.”

She was there fast. No wasted movement. She didn’t ask what happened. Just knelt, glove hovering above the housing. She could feel the heat radiating from it.

“Something’s cooked,” she said.

Jakob bent down. Even through his gloves, the heat was obvious.

“Motor’s burned,” Mira added, flat.

Jakob didn’t answer. He stood, slowly, swearing low and hard. He yanked the saw blade off and put the saw and cutting sled on a cargo sled. He and Tom started pushing it toward the ship, boots grinding over the ice shelf. Mira got up with the saw and began opening up the motor.

“All hands,” Jakob said over the team channel. His voice cracked. “Emergency. Sled saw motor’s blown. Meet at the ship.”
