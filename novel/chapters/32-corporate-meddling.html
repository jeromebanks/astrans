Chapter 32

Corporate Meddling

1,315 words · about 5 minutes

After the heavy lift project, Halden was promoted to Senior System Analyst. Every day Halden’s job devolved to making a report of daily launches, which he fully automated. He spent his days reading the titles of the scheduled talks to be given at the JRI. Everyone in JRC was allowed to attend any JRI seminar. None of his coworkers ever did.

The talks were often exercises in academic diplomacy, full of progress reports on projects that were progressing nowhere in particular. Halden skipped past the safe research. Then, three pages in, a title caught his eye.

“Atmospheric Tether Systems for Jovian Gas Extraction”

Atmospheric tethers. He’d never seen those words combined before. The core concept was a physical connection between Jupiter’s atmosphere and orbital space. Direct resource extraction without the impossible energy costs of heavy lift vehicles.

Halden leaned back in his chair. This could be theoretical materials research dressed up with an exotic application. Or it could be the kind of systems-level thinking that actually solved problems instead of just studying them.

Either way, it deserved a closer look. He marked it in his calendar.

The day of the talk he took the JRC/JRI shuttle to Jamal. Halden got a small plate with grapes and cheese and sat to the side. Savic was speaking about tapers and diameters of the tether straw.

Halden was intrigued by the talk, but he was fascinated by how involved the JRI people were. The talk drew a good crowd, including several professors. Halden put the series on his recurring calendar.

He sat in the meetings quietly, absorbing who the people were. He read abstracts of the earlier talks, and started attending other talks by the key players, learning who the people were as much as what the project was. He even began to chat with them after the talks.

During one such conversation, he followed them into the project room. He saw the whiteboards for the first time. The entire concept, broken into subproblems, each of which had been worked extensively. He read each one carefully, taking pictures. Back in his office, he recreated the whiteboards. Between the talks, he worked on them. Found inconsistencies. Wrote his own ideas.

About a month after he started attending, Rithika was giving a talk on ³He purification. Halden raised a hand. “What is the pressure where your dirigible will be operating?”

She was caught off guard. “When I conceived of this, I was planning to operate at one to two Bar, but recently Savic changed the plan on orbital level. He noticed a high velocity equatorial band at fifty millibar. So I should adjust to use that pressure.”

By the second month, Halden had reframed the problem. He caught Savic and asked him to bring just a few to the project room. Halden was standing in front of a new whiteboard. “What is your project here?”

“I noticed a way to use Rithika’s extraction system with a straw to get ³He into orbit. We thought this might be the solution to Jovian extraction, but there are too many problems, especially energy and durability.”

“Two brothers back in North Carolina thought people could fly. They didn’t know anything about it. They just made bicycles. But they made the worlds worst glider with an engine and it hopped one hundred twenty feet. Their invention didn’t teach anyone how to fly. Planes never looked much like theirs. Why did it matter?”

“The Wright brothers solved the control problem,” Rithika answered.

“They did, but the reason it mattered was, until that one hundred twenty foot hop, flight was all talk. After that, powered flight was real. Everyone got involved. Within a decade, aviation was an industry. I don’t need you to build an airliner. I just need the Orville Flyer. I want a prototype that can endure Jupiter long enough to deliver a single kilogram of ³He to orbit. We can get that sample to Earth and I will set up a press conference and burn that kilogram of Jovian ³He in Ilha Fusão. If we can do that, the world changes overnight.”

“And if it fails?” Savic asked.

“It fails quietly. No one will know,” Halden said.

Rithika was the first to catch on. “My design is for throughput. If you want just a sample, it shrinks down. A multi-stage centrifugal compressor, let the hydrogen bleed out. The dirigible was to support more equipment. We can just let the compressor hang from the tether. I need to simplify purification.” Halden handed her a marker and she put her new proposal on his board.

“Savic, is there something you can do to simplify this?” Halden asked.

Savic stood up and went to Halden’s board. “The work of a space elevator is not so much lifting the payload as it is accelerating the payload tangentially. Hence the string of emitters. But, for a short run, we dont need the complication. We start the process moving too fast, and let the tether straw’s momentum speed it up. After a month, it will be strongly arcing, and maybe break. By then, we will be done.”

Step by step, the goal simplified, until they came to energy. “Making it smaller helps, but it is still a hard problem. I have to power real pumps, and we do not have the power for it,” Jason said.

Halden took the marker. “JRC is not bound by the fissile material ban. What about a KRUSTY reactor? Those are the size of a roll of paper towels.”

Jason’s eyes widened. “If we can use nukes, we can do this.” He began looking up compact fission designs on his slate.

Marco raised his hand. “Excuse me, Halden, sir. I understand your objective, and all the great ideas my colleagues are sharing, but this project is not mostly about helium collection. Mostly it is that straw. My advisor and I have worked out the mass of the straw under current taper models. We believe a production system would weigh twenty million tons, maybe more. The prototype you are proposing will be lighter, and move less gas. We can make the straw a hundred times thinner. Even so, it is still going to be at least two hundred thousand tons of extremely high grade CNT reinforced polymer. That doesn’t look like a prototype.”

Savic was still at the board. “We are looking at ninety thousand kilometers from upper atmosphere to stationary orbit. We are looking at stationary with respect to the upper equitorial band, because that is moving faster. The faster we are synchronized with, the closer synchronous orbit is. I want to put this in a nuclear ramjet, moving at five thousand meters per second. We can do that on Jupiter. The advantage is, at that speed, we can move synchronous orbit down thirty thousand kilometers. Rithika, you started with a dirigible, right? Why was that?”

“The collection and purification steps require a lot of heavy equipment. A dirigible lifts all that without burning energy to fight gravity.”

“Now we don’t have all that heavy equipment, and I don’t mind burning energy for a short prooof of concept. The intake of your compressor could be bleed from the compressed intake.”

Marco stood up. “Your idea brings us a thousand new problems. We don’t know how to build the jet. But if we reduce the height to synchronous orbit like that, we can build this with less than five thousand tons of material. Exponentials are harsh when you ask more, but benevolent when you ask for less.”

The researchers were working together and didn’t notice when Halden left the meeting. On the shuttle, he reviewed the plan. The system would not last, but it would return material from Jupiter. JRC would celebrate the delivery. For a day. Then they would have nowhere left to hide.
