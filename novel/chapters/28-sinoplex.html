Chapter 28

Sinoplex

1,836 words · about 8 minutes

The tunnel stretched twenty kilometers beneath the East China Sea, its walls lined with gleaming superconducting cables thick as tree trunks. Dr. Elin Sarai walked beside Minister Chen Zhengrong and his technical team, their footsteps echoing in the cool air. Liquid nitrogen flowed through the outer jackets, keeping the cables cooled to 77 Kelvin.

“Ten megavolts DC,” Chen said, pride evident in his voice. “No AC/DC conversion losses. No AC at all. Direct from the tokaplex to all of eastern China on a double backbone mesh.”

Elin ran her hand along the insulated housing. Of course he wasn’t mentioning the energy consumption of cryogenic cooling, or accounting for takeoffs. There would be losses, but this was still an amazing achievement. She’d reviewed the specifications, but standing here made it real. This was a powerline scaled for a civilization that had outgrown fossil fuels and was reaching for something bigger.

“The takeoffs?” she asked.

Chen gestured to a junction ahead where smaller conduits branched upward. “Aerial grid at one megavolt. We’ve phased out AC transmission entirely. Every substation, every industrial complex, uses DC. Anyplace with legacy AC equipment runs their own inverters, but everyone knows AC is the past. DC transmission uses the conductor more efficiently and has no radiative or inductive loss. Your tokaplex will feed this grid a full terawatt, continuous. That will allow us to proceed with hyperelectrification.”

“If we can keep it fueled,” Elin thought to herself, trying to keep her expression neutral.

They emerged from the tunnel onto the artificial atoll, a C-shaped ring of reclaimed land and reinforced concrete two kilometers across. The opening faced east, toward the open sea. Enormous voltage converters lined the inner shore, their cooling towers venting steam into the morning air. Connection points bristled with sensors and fail-safes. This was a fortress of infrastructure, designed to channel more energy than most countries could use.

“The platform arrives tomorrow,” Chen said. “Your team is ready?”

“We are,” Elin replied. “Gonçalo Silva will oversee certification. Your site general manager will have full operational control once we’re satisfied with the handoff.”

Chen nodded. “You will find General Manager Zhao to be quite capable. She managed the Tianwan Nuclear Power Plant for eight years and has just completed six months of training at the Monte Hermoso tokaplex. She is ready.”

Elin looked out at the empty water beyond the atoll’s mouth. Somewhere out there, the platform was crossing the Indian Ocean, pulled by a fleet of tugs. She had watched it leave Biloxi two months ago, a floating city of fusion technology, the magnetic containment vessel alone massing fifty thousand tons. It was far too big to cross any canal, so they had to route it around Africa.

“It will work,” she said, more to herself than to Chen.

The platform appeared on the horizon at dawn, a low dark shape flanked by eight ocean-going tugs. Elin stood on the atoll’s southern observation deck with Gonçalo and GM Zhao, watching through binoculars as the convoy approached.

“Mãe de Deus,” Gonçalo whispered. “I saw it in Biloxi. But out here…”

It was massive. The tokaplex core sat in the center, surrounded by heat exchangers, coolant systems, control buildings, and the power conversion equipment that would step the reactor’s output up to ten megavolts. The whole assembly rode on a stabilized floating platform designed to handle typhoon-force seas. Prefabricated in sections, welded together on artificial islands in Biloxi, the entire tokaplex was tested together for six months before the journey began.

The tugs positioned it carefully, fighting current and wind. Centimeter by centimeter, they nudged the platform toward the atoll’s opening. Harbor pilots called out adjustments over radio. Elin held her breath.

The platform’s bow crossed the threshold. She exhaled.

Three hours later, it was locked into place, connection booms extending to meet the platform’s docking hardpoints. The tugs released their lines and backed away, their job done. Now came Elin’s part.

“Zhao,” she said. “Let’s go see your reactor.”

Zhao walked them through the platform’s systems, describing the cooling loops, the magnetic coil assemblies, the plasma diagnostics without hesitation.

“I need to verify you know this unit’s startup sequence,” Gonçalo said, pulling up the checklist on his tablet. “Walk me through it.”

Zhao nodded. “Let’s begin.”

They spent three days on certification. Zhao ran through normal startups, emergency shutdowns, partial failures, grid synchronization issues while Gonçalo observed, checking her procedures against the design specs.

On the third evening, Gonçalo found Elin. “Zhao’s ready,” he said, exhaustion in his voice. “Better than ready. This is her reactor now.”

The test fuel arrived quietly. It was a small bottle of ³He, barely fifty grams, enough for a thirty-second burn. They loaded it into the fuel system at midnight, ran the startup sequence, and watched the plasma come alive. The magnetic fields held. The fusion reaction stabilized. For half a minute, the tokaplex poured a terawatt into the Chinese grid.

Then Gonçalo shut it down, and they spent eight hours analyzing every data point.

“Perfect,” Zhao said, reviewing the thermal curves. “We’re ready for full operation.”

“Fantastic,” Elin said. “The production fuel load will arrive tomorrow.”

The delivery was scheduled for dawn, 6:02 AM, but Elin was on the atoll’s loading platform an hour early. A US Navy Destroyer had brought the ten-ton container from the Helion Dynamics warehouse in Dutch Harbor to a US Navy base in Okinawa. It was being delivered from there by tiltrotor to the terawatt tokaplex.

Raines had arranged it personally. Ten tons of ³He, worth eight billion dollars, representing two months of operation. That was the total lunar output. Helion Dynamics would be dipping into their reserves to keep all the other reactors running for a while. Raines was betting lunar production would increase fast enough to take care of that. The entire supply chain depended on this working.

Elin watched the eastern horizon. At 6:01, the sun breached the sea, and for less than a second the top edge flashed brilliant green, then it was replaced by ordinary gold. The tiltrotor appeared a minute later, on schedule, flanked by two escort craft. It descended slowly, rotors kicking up spray, and settled onto the platform. The rear ramp dropped, and a US Marine officer stepped out, followed by six armed Marines surrounding a cylindrical container the size of a small car.

Minister Chen was there, along with a PLA colonel and a squad of soldiers. The two groups eyed each other across the platform.

“Dr. Sarai?” the Marine lieutenant said.

“That’s me.”

“Captain Pak, US Marine Corps. I have a delivery for the Shanghai Terawatt Tokaplex Project. Transfer of custody requires your signature and authorization from the People’s Republic of China.”

The PLA colonel stepped forward. “Colonel Feng. I will be overseeing security of this facility and all future fuel deliveries.”

Pak handed Elin a slate. She scanned the manifest. 9.8 metric tons of ³He, purity 99.97%, source Tycho Crater, shipped via Lunar Tug. She signed.

“Minister Chen?” she said.

Chen signed as well, transferring $7,840,000,000 to Helion Dynamics. The transfer done, Pak and Colonel Feng added their authorizations. The Marines rolled the container down the ramp. The PLA soldiers came up, face to face with the US Marines. Captain Pak crisply saluted Colonel Feng. The PLA took custody and, with Chen’s technical team, moved the container into the tokaplex’s ³He injection site.

Pak and his detail were still standing near the tiltrotor. He turned and saluted. “Good luck, Dr. Sarai, Minister Chen. We’ll return in two months with the next load.”

The tiltrotor lifted off, and Elin watched it disappear toward the horizon. Colonel Feng was already setting up a security perimeter around the injection site. This was his domain now. Eight billion dollars’ worth of fuel, the lifeblood of China’s energy future, sitting in a tank beneath the East China Sea.

Raines would love the optics. The US delivering fuel to China while both governments postured about energy independence. But Elin knew the truth: Li Yun Tan’s operation on the Moon was the real bottleneck. Even with the Kagutsuchi Vortex online, even with production ramping up, the lunar supply couldn’t keep pace with demand.

The Helion Dynamics reserves were currently over fifty tons, stockpiles Raines had been building since ³He first flowed from the Moon. But reserves wouldn’t last forever. The Moon needed to produce more, or the entire terawatt program would stall.

She pushed the thought aside. Today wasn’t about supply chains. Today was about making history.

The activation ceremony was scheduled for noon. The observation deck was filled with ministers, corporate executives, and international press. Tobias Raines stood among them, shaking hands and making promises about second and third tokaplexes, about China leading the world into a fusion-powered future. Elin avoided the crowd, staying in the control room with Zhao and Gonçalo.

“Are we ready?” Zhao asked.

Elin checked the status board. Fuel loaded. Cooling systems nominal. Grid synchronization confirmed. Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou were standing by for the surge.

“We’re ready.”

Zhao settled into the operator’s chair, her hands steady on the controls. Gonçalo stood beside her, watching. Elin took a position at the monitoring station, eyes on the plasma diagnostics.

“Beginning startup sequence,” Zhao said.

The magnetic coils energized, their hum vibrating through the platform. ³He flowed into the reaction chamber. The plasma formed, a roiling ball of superheated gas trapped in invisible magnetic fields. Temperature climbed. Pressure stabilized. The fusion reaction ignited.

Power surged. One hundred gigawatts. Five hundred. Eight hundred. The numbers climbed smoothly, exactly as designed. At ninety-five percent, Zhao held for thirty seconds, confirming stability, then brought it to full output.

One terawatt. The grid absorbed it smoothly. Across eastern China, coal plants, no longer needed, began staged shutdowns. Gas generators went idle. The superconducting backbone carried the load that had been distributed across hundreds of smaller facilities.

On the grid display, Elin watched the power flow stabilize. This was just the beginning. The capacity was there now for the planned expansions in Shenzhen, for the western hydrogen pipeline, for the urban vertical agriculture complexes waiting for reliable power. For the hyperelectrification Chen kept talking about.

Elin allowed herself a small smile. They’d done it. The first terawatt tokaplex, operational and feeding the grid. Raines’ vision, her design, Zhao’s operation. It worked. But as she watched the ³He injection metrics, she couldn’t ignore the math. This tokaplex was burning as much as the Moon could produce by itself, forcing the smaller tokaplexes onto reserve fuel. And Raines was already negotiating for five more this size.

Outside, the crowd erupted in applause. Minister Chen was giving a speech about China’s energy future, about leading the world, about the dawn of a new era. Raines was smiling, shaking hands, making deals.

Elin stayed in the control room, watching the numbers. Gonçalo stood beside her.

“It’s working, Elin,” he said quietly.

“For now.”
