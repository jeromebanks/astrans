Chapter 17

Lunar Control

4,380 words · about 18 minutes

Price of ³He$8,000,000/ton
Current Production2 tons per month
Current Consumption8 tons/year
Time Since Start4 years

Abundant water from Mars changed operations at Tan’s Cantina. No more gritty, chemically clingy frost scraped out of lunar shadow pockets. Mars had overcapacity, and the Moon had thirst. The pipeline was open.

Steel production, using Lunar iron and Martian carbon, expanded the settlement. The cableway grew, and new habitat shells could be assembled on site. Miners arriving from Earth brought just the core with them, and bought the steel structure in Tycho.

Li and Darrin kept their original hut.

Martian food was the staple now. The cantina smelled like roasted garlic, with cavy and amaranth chicha the most popular menu items. Earth food seemed foreign.

People could come in at any time to get a hot meal, but they did more; people lingered. They talked and laughed.

And Li made money. From the cantina, of course, but also because taking care of miners increased ³He production, and she got her twenty percent.

Helion Dynamics was now serious about building the terawatt tokaplexes. Biloxi had built a large, crescent-shaped artificial island to support their construction. Raines was stockpiling every gram of ³He he could get. When the terawatt tokaplexes came online, each one would consume five tons of helium per month. The Moon was producing two.

Li was in the cantina when a Lunar Tug pilot radioed in. “Tan, we’ve got four cheechakos. Certified claim about a hundred miles from Shackleton. They wanted a direct drop, but I told them the nearest Odd Point Rendezvous near there is in two weeks. They don’t want to wait, and I’ve got nowhere to park them. I need to refuel at Tan’s Depot to make the hop, and that’s going to cost them. Can you work some of your magic on them?”

Li smiled. “Tell them that while you are refueling, they can come over to Tan’s Cantina, meet some fellow miners, and get a free chicha.”

Six hours later, the cheechakos arrived. Li didn’t say anything, but the bartender slid them some chichas, and they began talking. One of the miners took them to see the big map on the back wall of the cantina showing active sites, mined sites, and cableways. Li was strategically seated nearby. They were shocked to see their site had already been mined. Li stepped up. One of the miners introduced the cheechakos to her. “Tan, these guys just came up. Still drinking their free chicha. Looks like their claim is down south, and has already been mined.”

Li shook their hands. “You know, Darrin just put in a spur on the southern line, if you like that latitude. Makes dropoff easier, if you want us to process your regolith.”

“What do you mean? We have an RF extractor. We can process it ourselves.”

“You can. That’s up to you. Do you want to show me your gear?”

They all put their pressure suits back on and left the cantina. Li showed them how to clip their suits onto the cableway, and they rode back to the depot.

She made comments as she looked over their gear. “That RF extractor is pretty small. I have some larger units for sale. Of course, you’d want more solar power, too. How are you fixed for extra pressure suits? Do you all plan to live in that one hut? I have larger ones, if you’d like.”

Li was thorough, and they appreciated her attention to detail. They replaced some of their equipment and left some with Li. After a tour of the ³He extraction facilities, they agreed to let her process their regolith. Her twenty percent cut seemed cheap compared to the cost of learning how to operate an RF extractor efficiently. Finally, they agreed to work the location she asked them to mine, as long as Darrin would show them where it was.

They called back to the pilot. “You’ll never believe what happened while you were getting ready for the hop.”

The pilot suppressed a laugh. “What happened?”

“We were in the bar, and we met a character. She helped us out and set us up with a better location. So I guess we won’t be needing the hop.”

“Was her name Tan, by any chance?”

“It was. Have you heard of her?”

“She does alright by the people nearby. Well, since you don’t need the hop, I am going to get some ³He. Good mining.”

Li was using a hundred-kilogram cryo-cylinders filled with 99.9% pure ³He. Helion Dynamics paid a premium for those shipments. They were reliably pure, and the large containers made for easier logistics. The pilot loaded three and headed back to LEO.

Tan was back in the cantina, adding the cheechako’s new claim to the map when Andrei called. “Tan, I just got a request. A man is looking to go to visit the Moon. He needs a hotel. I told him I would ask around. Do you have space?”

“Sure, we have space, if the price is right. Did you get a sense of how much I can charge this guy?”

“I don’t know. He sounds like a mid-level bureaucrat, but he works for the UN. Name is Tadeusz Vassiliev. Didn’t say why he was going, but if it is official, you can probably charge more. I should have thought of that. I just blurted out my rate.”

“You charge dollars. I charge the miners a gram per night, but this guy doesn’t have ³He. Float $20,000 per night and see what he says. Mention it comes with free oxygen and water.”

“What will he eat?”

Tan had to think. She was charging so much for meals here, no Earther could afford it, but if she cut him a deal, others would expect it. “Suggest he bring his own meals.”

That evening, Li talked it over with Darrin. He was curious. “Why would the UN be sending someone up here?”

Li looked around. The big map. The Martian food. Employees. None of this was actually authorized. It just all kind of came about after they got the reactor. Li froze. “Darrin, he’s an inspector. That is the only reason the UN would send someone to the Moon.”

“What would he inspect up here?”

“The UN still thinks they have jurisdiction up here. And they damn sure won’t like the reactor. That reactor is why we have success here.”

“What’s the play, Li?”

“We have a week before he arrives. When he gets here, Tycho will be a ghost town. Nothing interesting to report.”

Darrin looked at the cableway. “I don’t know how we can hide that.”

Li called a meeting in the cantina. She and Darrin explained the problem to Pavel, Luke, and Tseng.

Pavel spoke first. “Do we know that he is coming to look for a nuclear reactor?”

“No. We know a man is coming to the Moon and works for the UN. But what else would he be doing here?”

Pavel nodded. Li was right. “OK, if he is looking for nuclear power, he is going to bring radiation monitors: scintillators, neutron detectors, Geiger counters. I will shut down the reactor, but that won’t be enough.”

Luke jumped in. “Tan, when you shipped the reactor, you logged it as a double RF, right?”

“Yeah. That was Belov’s idea.”

“Well, if they are looking for a double RF, we give them one. A large sheetmetal structure with two doors and two compartments. I’ll bury a bottle of ⁴He under the device. Put in tailings, send a puff of helium out into a collector.”

Darrin was looking at the equipment connected to the buried hot loop. “He will see everything else. I don’t see how this can help.”

Tseng nodded. “He won’t see anything. We will bury it all, set up Luke’s show closer to Tan’s Cantina. I will smooth it so it looks like none of us have ever been near the north wall. The tailing pile has more than enough regolith to do that.

Pavel liked the plan. “I am going to need to scrub for polonium. Increase the bubble rate of helium and slow the reaction. When I shut it down, we will switch out the cold trap. It will still be detectable if he knows where it is, but at a distance, it will be invisible.”

“I like the plan. If there is anything you want from Earth, you need to order it in the next two days, or it won’t get here before our visitor,” Li said. “Darrin has the accounts, so let him make the actual orders.”

With that, the meeting ended.

Tseng started bagging up tailings and moving them to a minor, secondary crater twenty miles east. Darrin reprogrammed the cableway to drop real bags in a crater just west of Tycho.

Luke set up the fake RF near a cableway pylon north of Li and Darrin’s hut. It measured two meters by two meters by five meters, built from heavy eighth-inch steel. Hidden in the frame was a small tube, connecting a buried bottle of ⁴He to an outlet nozzle. A tiny battery powered a few status lights. It looked convincing to anyone completely unfamiliar with gas extraction.

Tseng inspected the door. “Luke, this doesn’t close properly.”

“It latches. What’s the problem?”

“It doesn’t seal.”

“It’s a prop. No need to seal. Just look good.”

“Doesn’t look good to me.”

“Let’s hope Vassiliev is less discerning than you.”

Luke practiced loading, collecting, and unloading until it looked natural. He kept it in for a minute, then hit the helium button.

Li watched. “Ten seconds is enough. If it’s longer, he’ll start thinking. We don’t want that. Unload before the tension is drained. This is theater, not extraction.”

Luke adjusted his performance.

Two days before Vassiliev arrived, Li sent out the news that Tan’s Cantina would hold a one-day-only sale: half-priced meals and free chicha. Every miner dropped whatever he was doing to attend the blowout.

While they ate, Li briefed the miners. “If you see the inspector, talk slowly and move slowly. Stop by the cantina, but bring your own food. I will dispense water and oxygen. While he’s here, nothing else will be served.”

When everyone had gone home, Li vented the Cantina and removed the airlock. Looking around, she noticed the map. She pulled it off the wall and rolled it tightly, bringing it back to her hut.

Pavel scrubbed and shut down the reactor. Darrin and Tseng disconnected the ovens and the turbine and moved them out of the crater. They then buried the entire site in a meter of regolith. As a last measure, Darrin turned down the speed on the cableway to about a mile per hour.

Li greeted Envoy Tadeusz Vassiliev in Tan’s Depot while the pilot was unloading a pallet of meal kits.

“You’re Tan?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said. That was her first lie. “I can show you to your room. I will have your luggage brought over. Do you need a guide?”

“No, thank you. I’d just like to look around, get a feel for the place.”

Vassiliev opened a case. Inside, Li noticed a Geiger counter, a scintillator, and a thermal scanner. She didn’t let on that the last one surprised her. “Very well. If you’d like to meet up later, your room has a phone.”

They put on their suits, and Li brought him to the cableway. “You can clip in and go wherever you want. Don’t go too far or you can run out of oxygen.” Not exactly a lie. She just hadn’t told him other places he could get oxygen, or how long his suit would last.

As soon as Vassiliev was out of sight, Li called up Pavel. “Pavel, good and bad. No neutron detector, but he has a thermal scanner. Are we in trouble?”

“A thermal scanner? Yeah, those pipes were still hot when we buried them. He won’t see anything under the sunlight, but he might during lunar night. Do you know how long he is staying?”

“He didn’t say. I asked for a week’s pay upfront, and he didn’t complain.”

That evening, Li returned to the depressurized cantina. Vassiliev came by while she was there.

“I saw your large RF extractor,” he said. “It’s just a double?”

“Oversized double core. For regolith processing. Much faster than processing out in the fields. Miners send their stuff here to save electricity.” That last part was totally true.

“I noticed some regolith falling out while Luke was running it. I thought it was leaking. But, in the end, Luke collected two grams of helium. It must have been cruft from loading. Where does the power come from?”

She looked up on the North rim of the crater. “Have you seen the solar panels on the rim? They get light even when the crater floor is dark.” True, she mused, for about ten hours a month.

“I’d like to see outside the crater.”

“Of course. Would you like someone to guide you?”

“I’d like to move around on my own.”

“The cableway is designed for just that. How much oxygen will you be bringing?”

“I’m not sure.” He turned without a word and left.

In the morning, she saw him with a K-bottle of oxygen, heading north to the rim. She watched him get off the cableway and check the solar panels. She was glad they never disconnected them.

Li nudged Darrin. They watched him with binoculars.

“Li, what is he doing up there?”

“He wanted to see where our power came from. I pointed out the panels.”

Darrin called Pavel, who was in his hut. “How far is the polonium waste tube from the panels?”

“About ten yards. Why?”

“Look at the rim.”

Pavel suited up and came out on the crater floor, watching with binoculars. Vassiliev wasn’t just checking the panels. He had his Geiger counter out. Then he stopped and knelt down.

“Oh, fuck! He found something!” Pavel shouted into the team channel.

“That’s in the middle of the panels. Nothing there, right?” Li asked.

“That’s not where the waste pipe is, but that isn’t how a man acts when he finds nothing. Maybe a drop of polonium fell out there?”

Vassiliev pulled out a trowel and began digging around. They watched him for twenty minutes. Finally, he put a sample in a bag and got back on the cable to continue north.

Darrin spoke first. “He doesn’t know what it is. There are other sources of radioactivity on the Moon.”

Li shook her head. “He doesn’t know. As far as he is concerned, you’re right. But he bagged it. It is going back to Earth, and they will find out.”

Pavel closed his eyes.

Li came back on the team channel. “Forget it. It is out of our hands now. Let’s make sure nothing else happens.”

She thought of other ways this could go wrong. A gabby miner might say too much, but anything contradicting Vassiliev’s own observations would sound like the ravings of a madman. Whatever was said, she might be able to talk her way out of.

If, however, he died out there, that would bring enormous attention. Not another inspector, but a battalion with the authority to dig, seize, and incarcerate. That could not happen. She had to keep him safe.

It was time for Rook. Li sent Darrin over the east rim to find him. Rook was on his claim, bagging regolith, when Darrin arrived. For a hundred grams, he dropped what he was doing and followed Darrin straight back. They met Li in the cantina.

Li was pacing. “Sam, I know you don’t like to talk. That’s fine. Lean into that. But I need you to meet this guy. He is carrying around a K-bottle.”

“A K-bottle? Why?”

“I might have suggested it would be safer to have oxygen on hand.”

Sam slapped his thigh. “Cheechakos are the best. No extra battery, but a bottle of oxygen.”

“Yes, well, I need you to accidentally bump into him. Ask if he wants help carrying the bottle. Never act like it is weird. Maybe even make a show of refilling it.”

“Sure. What should I say to him?”

“As little as possible. Move slow, talk slow, don’t know.”

Sam grinned. “That’s me.”

“One more thing. Can you bring this oxygen K-bottle with you? Make it look normal to carry a bottle around.”

Sam laughed. “Anything special I should do?”

“Just keep him alive.”

Sam agreed. Outfitted with the K-bottle, he headed north.

After an hour, Rook was ready to backtrack. He started mapping out different routes Vassiliev could have taken. Before he got off, he saw an odd sight: a miner was sitting on a rover, chatting with a man with a K-bottle.

“This is the talky part,” Rook thought to himself with a grimace. He got off the cable and started walking over. When he got in Local Band range, he stopped and listened.

Vassiliev was trying to figure out who was in charge. “How much regolith do you process in a day?”

The man blinked slowly, checked his bag weight. “Whatever we collect.”

“And your quota?”

“We don’t run quotas. I process my claim at my own rate. I take my own time to get my ton.”

“Your ton?”

“Whatever the claim has. Usually around a ton.”

Vassiliev had seen others, moving slowly, like they had all the time in the universe.

Sam decided to come over. “Hi, guys. I think I went the wrong way on the cableway. Can you help me out? I need to refill my K-bottle.”

The miner switched to FM, leaving Vassiliev out of the conversation. “What is with these K-bottles?”

“Makes our guy easy to spot.”

“Dumbest thing I ever saw.”

They switched back to Local Band. “Two strangers in one day. I can refill both your bottles.”

Vassiliev’s eyes perked. “Thank you. I don’t know how much I have left.”

When the miner took their bottles inside, Rook plugged into the rover and signalled Vassiliev. “Hey, buddy, recharge. You don’t want to freeze out here.” He took a cable from the rover and plugged it into Vassiliev’s hip pack. “Your charge is low. How long have you lived up here?”

Vassiliev wasn’t sure what to say. “I am on vacation from Earth, and was curious to see the Moon.”

Sam shut off his mike while he laughed. Vassiliev continued, unaware. “Look, if you have some time, what would you say to helping me? The Moon has dangers I am not prepared for. Tan offered a guide, but I don’t trust her. I want someone she doesn’t know about. I can pay.”

“What’s it worth?” Rook asked, barely looking up.

“How does five thousand dollars sound?”

Sam smirked. It sounds like a quarter of a chicha. “That would be fine. My name is Rook. Where we going?”

The miner brought back the full K-bottles.

Vassiliev grabbed his like it was a life preserver. “Thank you for the conversation and for the oxygen. I’ve taken enough of your time; we’ll be on our way.”

The miner looked surprised. “You two together? I didn’t realize that.”

“I just hired him,” Vassiliev explained.

Sam kept track of where they were and made sure Vassiliev kept his battery charged. They moved around under the cableway and even past it.

Vassiliev watched miners from behind his visor. Watched a man bag maybe ten kilos in half an hour. No music. No jokes. No camaraderie.

They looked like ghosts.

“I expected… more life,” Vassiliev said.

Sam looked at him. “They’re mining.”

Sam pointed at the sun. “If you don’t want to get caught out here in the cold, I suggest we head back to Tycho Crater by sunset.”

“Sure. Nothing to see out here, anyway.”

The ride back to Tycho Crater was long and quiet. No conversation, no confrontation, no evidence. When night fell, Vassiliev took out his thermal imager and started looking around.

When they came over the ridge, he imaged the entire crater. Li’s hut door and the tube for her Earth window stood out, and the area near the RF extractor. Tan’s Cantina glowed faintly. Some of the regolith in the waste pile was hot.

He dropped by the Cantina to talk to Li. “I took a thermal image of this crater as we came back. The only signature I saw was the things you showed me and some of the regolith on the tailings pile.”

“Probably something fresh out of the RF extractor.”

“Probably. Well, I’m checking out. Lunar Tug will be picking me up here tomorrow.”

“Luke and Darrin are in Luke’s hut, if you want to talk. I heard Luke has some beer, if you’re interested.”

“You know, if you can keep it quiet, a beer might be very nice right now.”

Li escorted him down to Luke’s hut. They came through the airlock and hung up their pressure suits on the wall. As promised, Luke had a six-pack of Earth beer. Pavel was there, too. They chatted for about half an hour. Pavel remembered he had some frozen Earth bread and excused himself to go to his hut. He was back in about ten minutes, and they microwaved it and enjoyed the food. When Luke mentioned he was tired, they all suited back up and left.

Li and Darrin helped Vassiliev get his gear back to the depot. While on Lunar Tug, he called his supervisor. “Tim, this place is like a graveyard. They use solar power, so they just sleep for two weeks at a time, hoping their batteries don’t die and they don’t run out of oxygen. We were looking for black markets? The people are all loners, barely able to talk to others. They couldn’t begin to start a black market. Honestly, it would be better if they did.”

“You didn’t find a reactor?”

“No, they are using solar panels. I verified the connections. Luke is very fast and efficient with the RF extractor. I came over the rim during lunar night and ran a thermal scan on the entire crater. If there was a reactor, even a buried one, it would have shown. There was nothing.”

“No radiation?”

“Yeah, well, yes, I did find some radiation. I searched the area and didn’t find any more, but I bagged what I found. I am curious to see what it is.”

“Get that to the lab. They’ll figure it out. Who is running the show up there?”

“Darrin. He lets Tan do all the legwork, but it is clear that Darrin is the one who is running things. Tan just sits in the cantina all day. She offered me algae soup in a squeeze bag so I could drink it suited. It was disgusting, but she loved it.”

He watched through the porthole as Tan’s Depot shrank in the distance.

“Look, no reactor. No illegal food service. No commerce that I can see. Honestly, Tim, I think we’re worried about nothing. These people are too dead inside to cause trouble. And I mean that literally. This place creeps me the hell out. Do not send me back here.”

Li watched the Lunar Tug shrink to a glint and vanish in the black sky.

Darrin came in and found her. “Li, the Lunar Tug pilot just sent me a recording of a call the inspector placed. He thinks I’m in charge.”

“Good. If they do come back, they won’t arrest me. Now, let’s get back to business. Turn it back on. All of it.”

Darrin didn’t hesitate. “With pleasure.”

Tseng and Luke dug out the reactor, and Pavel began the slow process of bringing it back online. Once there was power, the cableways sped back up to normal speeds, pulling in bags of regolith. Miners could see the motion from their huts and clipped themselves to the lines, gliding in.

Li reinstalled the airlock on the cantina and started repressurizing.

In about ten minutes, the pressure was enough that they could start the music. She played it loud and pumping. The staff flipped the tables upright and set up stools and chairs.

Li grinned. “All drinks are half price. Anyone who can’t get a room is welcome to sleep in the cantina tonight.”

Miners were coming in fast, leaving their pressure suits hanging on the wall. The bartender cracked open a jug of chicha. The earthy smell filled the cantina as he started pouring mug after mug of the cloudy amber.

Li and Darrin stood by the wall, watching the festivities. Luke, Tseng, and Rook showed up. The five of them pushed two tables together and started talking. About fifteen minutes later, they were joined by Pavel.

Sam was talking about the ride back. “It was night, and Vassiliev pulled out a thermal imager. Fine, nothing to see out there, but when we came over the rim, I totally knew the reactor was going to glow like midday. But nothing. Were the pipes really cold?”

Pavel laughed. “When Li told me he had a thermal imager, I knew exactly what our problem would be. I can’t let the pipes freeze. Electrical power was keeping the metal coolant molten. So, while he was gone, we scraped six inches of regolith and replaced it. We dumped all that hot regolith on the tailings pile.”

Sam slammed his chicha down. “Hah! That’s why the pile was hot. I wondered how a fake RF heated the regolith.”

Luke was next. “How was the Earth beer? I had to go with that, you know. Vassiliev isn’t ready for chicha. Pavel, you were smooth.”

“Who? Me? All I did was share some bread. And accidentally slipped a sample bag out of Vassiliev’s suit when I was putting mine on.”

“What did you do with it?” Darrin asked.

“I dumped it near the wall and refilled it with some normal regolith. Looks the same.”

The party continued for another six hours. Finally, Li and Darrin went to bed, leaving the bartender in charge.

Li looked up through the Earth window. “Productivity has made us friends and earned us enemies. Our allies will not always protect us.”
