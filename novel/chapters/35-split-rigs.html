Chapter 35

Split Rigs

1,298 words · about 5 minutes

Halden knew better than to try to run the project. He became a translator. He put Dr. Savic in charge of design. After graduation, Savic took time off to see family. Only Halden knew he went to Biloxi.

Elin brought him to her Back Bay facility. “Manufacturing is not just mostly logistics. Logistics is at least a hundred twenty percent of the game. Fabrication is only a distraction.”

“JRC has no facilities.”

“So you find people who do. And don’t expect just manufacturing. They will deliver expertise. Lean on them.”

“You didn’t do that. You built everything right here.”

“At first I did, but when I started moving operations off site, we became much more productive. Half the land here used to be metal warehousing. Now I let Planetary Metals handle all that, and we are much more productive. Don’t do what I did. Copy my successes.”

When Savic returned, he subcontracted the orbital platform component to Navantia, a Spanish company with a long record in pressurized structures and naval-grade hulls. Their space division handled segment fabrication.

Each segment was as large as current launch systems could manage, just under the payload mass and diameter limits for a heavy-lift orbital insertion. The segments went by boat to JRC’s Punta de Maisí launch facility, where they were lifted individually to the Lunar Tug LEO hub.

The Split Rig program had no major failures. Engineering reviews passed. Procurement delays were minimal. Logistics bottlenecks seemed to resolve themselves. This, more than anything, made JRC uneasy. Nothing could run this smoothly.

Halden knew what made him look effective. Someone kept intercepting weak points before they failed. Vendors were replaced before delays turned critical. Material tolerances were corrected before fabrication. Load structures were rebalanced just ahead of final simulation. No one said how it happened. But the pattern was visible in metadata. Subtle edit trails. Changes logged at the last minute.

That someone was Elin. She was never listed, never named, but always present. Her corrections ran ahead of the project like a shock front. Quiet, systematic, and plausibly deniable.

He was not allowed to contact her directly. Any trace of correspondence would end the relationship. JRC would read it as confirmation that Halden wasn’t in control, and control was his only real capital.

So he worked with it. If a vendor spec changed, he approved it without delay. If an engineer flagged a surprising correction, he noted it and moved on. He never asked where it came from. Never said thank you. He simply treated each intervention as luck. And his luck held.

By the time the platform neared completion, Halden’s internal evaluations had begun to shift. No longer just competent. Now strategic, resilient, effective under pressure. JRC flagged him as a potential executive.

He read the memo, deleted it, and gave no indication he’d seen it.

He knew the truth; it wasn’t his project. It was Elin’s. He just kept the chair warm.

The tether weaving system sat on the space platform itself, eight machines, mounted like outriggers, continuously extruding structural cable, four down into Jupiter’s upper atmosphere. Four up to the space platform. Three machines in each direction built solid braided tethers. The fourth was tubular, the straw that moved the helium.

The raw materials were shipped separately and sourced through different channels. The polymer came from Earth. It was a two-part epoxy, engineered for extreme tensile loading, rapid in-vacuum curing, and thermal resilience. JRC sourced it through duPont. Delivery was handled by Lunar Tug, which managed all outbound mass cargo from Earth to Callisto. The epoxy was packed into reinforced drums with RFID-linked seals. Punta de Maisí heavy lift moved them into the LEO hub, and Lunar Tug brought them to Callisto.

The yarn came from Mars. Lafevre had underbid Earth-based rivals for the carbon nanotube filament contract, partly on delivered price, mostly on quality. Their lines were cleaner, more uniform, and their facility had the vacuum extrusion capacity to scale without bottlenecks. The Martian yarn was packed into dense transport drums and launched from the Olympus Mons mass driver. The driver was extended and powered up to get it to the Jovian gravity well. From there, a navigating rocket took it on a transfer arc to Callisto, where tenders brought it in.

At Callisto, the drums were staged in orbital storage before being transferred to the tenders. Tenders could carry six drums at a time, one epoxy and five yarn. They brought the drums to the weaving platform. Automated mixers combined the epoxy on demand; filament feeders unspooled the yarn, feeding it into the braid channel with high-precision tension control. Each meter of tether contained Earth and Mars, resin and filament, locked in structural code, laid down strand by strand.

The light in the polymer lab was flat and violet, filtered through silica cladding to blunt the Martian sun. Dr. Marc Lafevre was watching the production numbers. Operations were fourteen percent ahead of schedule.

Across the room, one of his chemists, Inaya Cho, adjusted the alignment on a thin scaffold where a carbon nanotube thread had been stretched and exposed to cycles of heat, pressure, and trace reactive gases.

“Three thousand hours,” she said. “Then it starts to pit. At four thousand, you lose twelve percent of tensile strength. At six, the surface is flaking oxides.”

Lafevre didn’t answer immediately. The Jovian lower stratosphere, particularly near the water cloud band, was more chemically aggressive than JRC had modeled. Tethers made of carbon would not survive indefinite exposure.

He looked at the stress curve again, “What do you propose?”

Inaya stepped back from the rig. “Boron nitride nanotubes.”

He nodded. “BNNT has lower tensile strength, but with enough crosslinking, we can hit the load targets. It won’t oxidize the way carbon does in that soup.”

“Cost?”

“Maybe ten times higher. But not insane. And we only need it as a shell on the last thousand miles. I’ll send a note to Halden. He will understand.”

He drafted the memo himself. It was short, formal, with data appendices and a polite recommendation to coat or substitute the last thousand miles of the tether with boron filament. The overall budget impact was only about five percent.

No reply came that day. After a month, he began to wonder if it had ever been delivered. Then he got his answer.

JRC Internal Correspondence
Re. Proposal from Noctis Landing - Dr. Marc Lafevre
To. Halden, Split Rig Director
From. Anya Choudhury, Budget Oversight
Per our earlier exchange, Dr. Marc Lafevre has submitted an unsolicited design deviation regarding the tether material. He proposes replacing the contracted carbon nanotube yarn with a new boron nitride nanotube variant synthesized under controlled vacuum on Mars.

While it may sound like a safer path, it threatens the kind of budget and schedule overruns this project cannot afford. Switching to BNNT, regardless of potential performance gains, would introduce a new qualification cycle, trigger fresh regulatory review, and potentially nullify two binding procurement agreements with Earth-side partners. More critically, the substitution would open the door to renewed political scrutiny from stakeholders who already view the Martian contract as overreach.

Rather than risk the budget and timeline of this project, we will follow the original spec provided by JRI. We will not rebalance material sourcing mid-cycle. Every change feeds the perception that JRC lacks process discipline. That cannot happen here.

Please encourage Lafevre to focus on quality control and filament consistency, which will surely resolve the issue.

A.

Lafevre read the memo. He posted it on the wall and got back to work.
When Cho saw the memo, she found him immediately. “They rejected the proposal. So that’s it?”
”This is a JRC project. We are just here to make the yarn.”
