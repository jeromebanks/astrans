Chapter 6

Beyond Helium

6,249 words · about 26 minutes

Price of ³He$8,000,000/kg
Current Production30 tons/month
Current Consumption30 tons/year
Time Since Start22 months

Li liked the Moon. The silence, the slow drift of shadow, the way boots felt against ground. The Earth was beautiful from the crater.

The Earth was slightly gibbous. She had only a few hours left before the floor turned blue, and another couple of hours before the solar panels on the ridge stopped producing power. She had added to her pack every rendezvous. This lunar night, they would have twenty-five percent more power than they needed.

Every rendezvous, she learned of people who had tried just a little too hard and darksided. She took their orders home. She would pay the next of kin when she could find them.

As they got into bed, Li looked at Darrin. “I’ve been working on a way out of this for almost a year, you know.”

“You will never get enough batteries for us to operate during the lunar night.”

At breakfast the next morning, Darrin prepared the algae biscuit soup, with freeze-dried beef and vegetables. Li took a sip. “Andrei has a friend, Belov. They grew up together. Belov is salvaging old Soviet submarines.”

“And?”

“He was tearing down an Alfa-class submarine.”

Darrin frowned. “You bought a submarine?”

“Just the reactor. An OK-550.”

“You’re kidding.”

Five days in, they were going to bed, watching the Earth through the Earth window. “It’s perfect for us, Darrin. One hundred fifty megawatts thermal. Compact enough to fit in a lander frame if you strip the shielding. Belov will clean it up, replace the fuel rods, and check every weld. When we get it, it will be solid.”

“We barely use one megawatt. What are you planning to do with that much power?”

“There aren’t a lot of tiny reactors lying around. This one meets our needs. We will run it low.”

Li was going over their extraction charts on day ten when Darrin spoke up. “You know nukes are banned here.”

“The law only matters if someone can enforce it. In the meantime, this is power when the sun disappears, and the cold tries to kill us.”

“And if they check?”

“I filed it as a high-output RF extractor. It will come wrapped in tungsten foil and a three-millimeter steel box. Safe enough while it’s cold. And I talked to Andrei Volkov. I gave him an extra ten million to not ask any questions. He can use the cash while he is getting Lunar Tug on its feet.”

Day fourteen, their last night. Earth was almost at its last quarter. “When is this coming?”

“Belov said it would take six months to prepare before he could ship it. He is using Russian heavy lift.”

“Won’t that cost more than getting it to Texas and using SpaceX?”

“Fewer people will handle it. Fewer questions.”

Darrin set the pit near the north crater wall. The wall would shade the radiators from the sun. He dug ten meters deep, using the rock to shield them and provide distance.

“What if this thing decides to go critical on us?” he asked, leveling the excavator.

Li knelt at the edge, scooping up a handful of regolith and letting it sift through her gloves. “Then we won’t have time to worry about it.”

“That’s comforting.”

“Belov said it’s engineered to fail safely. Control rods drop, reaction stops. It was made for military use, so it’s built to operate under duress.”

Darrin’s helmet turned toward her. “Water?”

“Lead-bismuth coolant, but same principle.” She stood, brushing dust off her knees. “Hot liquid in, hotter liquid out. We tap the heat, run it through generators, ovens, and anything else.”

He went back to digging. “You make it sound like ordinary plumbing.”

“Ordinary, but with molybdenum pipes.”

For the next six months, every time they went to rendezvous, there were extra tungsten plates and lead-bismuth alloy pellets and TZM pipe sections. Li had cut a deal with Lunar Tug to ship them as ballast whenever they had spare capacity.

Darrin routed an ammonia cooling loop to radiators on the permanently shaded interior face of the crater’s northern wall. Set in reflective troughs, they would radiate heat into space smoothly.

Lunar Tug actually landed inside the crater to deliver the reactor. It was a cube about three meters to a side. It looked like just plain carbon steel plates, scuffed from the journey. Li watched the Lunar Tug crew offload it with the kind of care reserved for live ordnance. No one questioned the claim that it was an oversized RF. Belov had stenciled “RF Extractor” and “УСТАНОВКА РАДИОЧАСТОТНОЙ ЭКСТРАКЦИИ” on the steel panels. Li wondered how much the Lunar Tug pilots knew.

“Christ, it’s heavy,” Darrin muttered as they guided it toward the pit.

“Be glad Belov removed the depleted uranium shielding before shipping this. It would have weighed four times as much with that in place. What’s left is just enough for transport.” Li ran her hand along its surface.

They spent the next two days preparing it. First came the tungsten plates Li had been accumulating for real shielding. Each plate was five hundred kilograms. Even on the moon, they weighed too much for Darrin to lift. They bolted them to the reactor’s sides and top, piece by heavy piece, several layers deep.

Only then did they lower it into the pit and connect the molybdenum piping. The hot loop ran out a hundred yards from the reactor, then doubled back. Li had already set the pipe stanchions, giving the pipe about one foot of clearance above the exposed rock. Every four yards, Darrin had installed a tap where equipment could draw heat from the flowing metal. Between the taps, silicon aerogel insulation and protective covers kept the heat contained. When it was completed, they buried it in regolith.

Darrin dropped lead-bismuth pellets in a feeder pipe until it was full. Then he looked to Li, who was seated at the console. “Ready.”

Li turned the handle, lifting the control rods slowly, watching temperatures climb degree by degree. As it warmed, the pellets melted and started flowing deeper. Darrin kept feeding in more. They melted quickly, filling the piping and beginning their circulation as a river of liquid metal.

“Temperature’s climbing,” Darrin called from his console.

Li checked her readouts. “The reactor’s warming smoothly. The metal is at operating temperature. How’s the flow?”

Darrin scanned the regolith with an infrared imager. “Steady. No visible leaks.”

The real test came when they started up the supercritical CO₂ turbine. Li had connected it to the first tap on the hot loop, where the liquid metal was hottest. The turbine itself was compact, the size of a car engine, but rated for ten megawatts electric. She let the current into the generator, using it as a motor to turn the turbine slowly, making sure that the blisks were not warped by the density difference between the liquid phase and gas phase of the CO₂ charge. As the liquid entered the heat exchanger on the tap, it heated fast, pushing back against the turbine. She monitored the temperature. It wasn’t moving fast enough. The CO₂ was heating above its dissociation temperature, chemically attacking the exchanger. She increased the power slightly, and the velocity brought the temperature back down. Pressure and temperature were rising. The CO₂ passed the critical point. She kept pushing it deeper into the supercritical range, then opened the valve to the chilled ammonia line and stopped driving the turbine. It kept moving. Li and Darrin watched as the output rose, driving it all the way to ten megawatts before throttling it back down to one.

“We’ve got power,” she announced. “We could run every hut on the Moon with this thing. Let’s set up the ovens.”

Darrin had connected two regolith processors into the second and third taps on the hot loop two days ago. Now, he wired in the electric power to control the rotating drums and the gas extraction. Each oven could hold twenty tons of raw regolith, industrial-scale processors designed to crack rock and extract every trace gas locked inside. Unlike the RF extractor, which gently coaxed helium atoms to vibrate free, these ovens hit the regolith with raw thermal energy from the liquid metal. No finesse. No precision. Just extreme heat.

He loaded a test batch into the first oven, and they watched the internal temperature climb. 700°C is the generally accepted extraction temperature. Darrin let it climb to 1,000°C.

“Jesus,” Li whispered, watching the mass spec data.

The regolith didn’t just release helium. It gave up everything: hydrogen, neon, argon, water vapor, gases that had been locked in the rock for billions of years. Her old distillation equipment, designed for the flow from RF extraction, couldn’t keep up with the torrent of mixed gases pouring out of the load.

“We’re going to need a bigger distillation column,” Darrin said, imitating Brody.

Li laughed. She was already pulling up catalogs on her slate. “Two bigger columns. And a pre-separator to strip out the non-helium gases. We just pulled out 1.8 times more ³He than our old rig could manage. And that’s not counting all the other gases.”

She showed him the readout. Beyond the helium, they were recovering significant quantities of water vapor and noble gases. On a world where everything had to be shipped from Earth, those byproducts were valuable.

When lunar night came, for the first time since coming to the Moon, Li ran their hut at full power. Every light blazed. The air recycler hummed at maximum capacity. She cooked dinner without checking the battery levels. They continued sweeping and extracting right through the night under the blue glow of Earth.

The first visitor was a lean miner from just east of Tycho Crater. Li watched him on her external cameras, a single figure in a battered pressure suit, following their tire tracks across the crater floor. She suited up and walked out to meet him.

“Saw your lights,” he said over Local Band. “Not something you see much this time of night.”

“Name’s Sam,” he said. “My claim’s about ten miles east. I’ve heard of you before. Tan, right?”

Li winced at the name but let it pass. “What brings you out?”

He shrugged, the motion faint in the suit. “Mostly curious. I can’t work at night, so I was just looking around.”

“Most people can’t work at night,” Li said.

“Yeah, but you can. I was thinking maybe I haul some of my dirt here, you run it through whatever you’re doing. You take a cut, I can dig more.”

Li glanced at Darrin. They’d talked about this before, but this was the first time someone had walked in with the offer. “What kind of cut?”

“Ten percent. I do the digging, you do the extraction.”

She shook her head. “It’s more than just running it through. We pull almost double the gas, strip the junk, and bottle the ³He pure in a cryocontainer. Less weight, better price.”

“Cryocontainer? You’ll lose a lot to boil-off. I use K-bottles. No boil-off.”

“I use a pulse tube cooler to boil off sacrificial ⁴He. Using cryocontainers cuts my freight bill, and the mechanism prevents boil-off. I send back ten times as much ³He per kilogram of freight. If I process your helium, you won’t see a freight bill. I’ll cover that.”

Sam paused, thinking about the numbers. “Freight’s killing me. Fifteen percent gone before I see a credit. At even half that yield boost, it’s worth it.”

“Run a test batch,” Li said. “If we don’t beat your numbers by fifty percent, it’s free. If you stick with us, I want twenty percent.”

He grinned. “If you can hit those numbers, twenties are cheap. I’ll go get ten tons of my regolith.”

“Bring twenty,” Darrin called out.

When Rook came back, they dropped his bags into the oven. Darrin eased the heat up, slow at first. At 700°C, the helium started to flow, along with everything else. The mass spec matched Sam’s best RF extractor runs. Rook still looked like he was doing the math in his head.

Then Darrin pushed it higher, up to 1,000°C. Pressure climbed. The display ticked past anything Rook had ever seen, one hundred eighty-two percent of his best yield.

He stayed through the rest of the run, watching the ³He get distilled, weighed, and bottled in the cryocooler. Li sent the credit before the cooler was even sealed.

Sam stared at the number in his account. “I’m used to waiting weeks for a Tug to pick it up, take it to Earth, then wait for Helion Dynamics to pay out.”

“I don’t mind paying fast,” Li said. “It makes accounting easier. So, do we have a deal?”

Sam looked up. “You’ve got yourself a customer.”

A pair of miners Rook knew from east of Tycho asked him why he wasn’t selling helium anymore.

“You know, since I hooked up with Tan, I don’t need to sell helium. She pays me for it right away, and she ships it.”

“How much does she charge for that?”

“Twenty percent. That’s not much more than I was paying Lunar Tug.”

“Yeah, we are generally between ten and fifteen percent on shipping. But I wouldn’t give up that five percent.”

“That’s what I thought until I tried it. She nearly doubles the yield.”

“Fills the bottle with trash?”

“She does her own fractional distillation. You’ve seen her huge cryocontainers. Ultrapure ³He.”

“Nice.”

“Better than nice. I shut down my RF extractor. Without that energy hog, I can sweep a lot more. I am pulling four times as much as I used to.”

“Sam, you have to introduce us to Tan.”

Soon Li was processing their regolith, too. She got two more customers at the next rendezvous, then ten more at the rendezvous after that.

The next rendezvous, Ocean State Resources came knocking. She charged them twenty-five percent, a small surcharge for their rocky start.

Darrin ran the intake. With several clients bringing bags of identical-looking regolith, he needed a way to keep the bags straight. He printed QR codes on the bags, associating each bag with a miner and an account. Every bag got scanned for its QR code, weighed in, logged, and sent to the ovens. The return bags went out with a readout of ³He recovered, and the money was deposited in their accounts.

The miners called it Tan’s Refinery. They’d butchered her name. She liked it, in a way; it gave her just enough distance to keep people from thinking they really knew her, and the money kept flowing.

They made use of the other gases, too. The water they were recovering was small, but there was never enough water on the Moon. They started selling it at rendezvous.

Darrin ran a test on extractions. After the first pass through a pile of regolith, he pumped hydrogen back into the oven. When he brought the heat up again, the sweep pushed out another eight percent helium. After that, he made the hydrogen gas sweep part of every extraction.

At rendezvous, Rook got a large shipment of heavy aluminum tubes. Darrin was intrigued and asked about them.

“I hate hauling my dirt over that crater rim. I plan to make a cableway from the base of the wall on my side to the floor on your side. I have a heavy Dyneema cable and a low-power drum to keep it moving.”

“That sounds fascinating. Can I help?”

They spent the entire lunar day getting the pylons anchored and the cable run pulled over. Darrin kept the drive drum on his side, so it was easier to power.

When it became lunar night, Rook held his breath and clipped a ten-ton bag onto the line. It sagged, but it didn’t touch the ground, and the cable carried it away. Rook started loading more and more bags, and they all just ended up in a pile where Darrin wanted them. Rook came over to see how it was going.

When he arrived, Darrin was looking at the pile of bags. “I love this! I’m going to build this inside the crater, running in a loop. I won’t need to carry bags on the crawler anymore; they’ll carry themselves. Think you can extend yours? I’d like more people to use it.”

Sam hesitated, one hand on the cable. “Most folks are happy enough not to have to climb the crater rim, but sure. I have time during the lunar night. I can probably extend it another twenty miles.”

Other miners, who hadn’t been using Tan’s Refinery, saw the cableway and came to investigate. Soon, they were using it, too, and Tan had more customers.

A month later, Li saw a new series of towers stretching west. Ocean State Resources had copied Rook’s cableway.

Darrin worked with an engineer from Ocean State Resources, and together they built a transfer station. The station would read a bag’s QR code and automatically route it correctly. After that, Darrin could put empty bags back on the line, and they’d go to the right place. Ocean State Resources also agreed to extend their line another fifty miles west, drawing still more customers for Tan’s Refinery.

Inside the crater, the cableway loop dumped processed regolith tailings against the northeast wall. The pale, inert waste piled up slowly, like industrial snow. Li had a depth probe with her to estimate the volume. She saw another miner in Ocean State gear poking at the same pile. His name tag said Tseng.

“I think I’ve seen you around here before,” she said. “What are you doing?”

Tseng smiled and pulled a compact mass spec from his belt. He dropped a sample in and vaporized it with his survey laser. “Just seeing what it’s made of,” Tseng said. “I am a geochemist by training. Mining helium is more profitable, but less interesting. I sometimes wonder what metals are in this waste pile.”

“Waste?” she said. “These tailings are my way of scoring how much ³He we have extracted.” Li made a note on her slate. Tseng loaded another sample.

Darrin still worked his pit when he could, but most of his time went to managing the reactor, keeping its heat curve steady and regulating intake. Regolith arrived constantly, bags flowing in from every miner within reach of the cableway. Li had stopped mining altogether. She was too busy.

New QR codes began appearing on bags from farther out. Some miners built their own cableways, connecting to the network that Rook and Ocean State Resources had started. Darrin built a third one going north towards Ball Crater. Li’s days blurred together under the weight of constant movement.

One evening, she and Darrin were staring out across the crater. “I loved the peace we had out here,” she said softly. “What happened to that?”

“Progress,” he replied.

“Darrin, the regolith in this crater has maybe a fifth of the helium as most places on the Moon. We are making as much from our cut of Rook’s helium as we are from processing our own.”

“I expected that,” Darrin replied. “This is a young crater. It has only been accumulating solar wind for a hundred million years, not the billions of years it has accumulated on most of the Moon.”

“Well, we’re making more money than ever, but I feel like I’m always running. When I was mining, I had a place I needed to be. It was… relaxing, in its own way. I wish I had somewhere to slow down.”

“You want to buy a larger space? Shipping will be enormous, but you can probably afford it.”

“I’ll think about it. Right now, I need to sleep.”

Li was running intake when a miner came by the refinery the next day, dropping off five bags of regolith. “This is my last load. I’ve been here long enough. Time to get back to Earth and spend some of that helium money.”

Li loaded the first bag into an oven. “What are you doing with your gear?”

“I just left it there on my claim. I can’t bring it back to Earth.”

“Yeah, that would be crazy. But I’ll give you five grams for your hut. Another five for the rest of your gear, if you put it all on a cableway.”

“That’s ten grams for nothing. I’ll go get it now, while you are still processing my load.”

The hut arrived sealed and holding pressure. It was followed by his sweeper, batteries, panels, and an RF extractor. Li moved most of the gear to the eastern edge of the crater floor, but kept the hut near the prominence.

Within a week, a dozen more huts came in, clipped to the cableway. They looked odd among the ten-ton bags. Some were clean. Most were scuffed, dented, and patched. It didn’t matter. Darrin hauled them into place with the crawler, welding modules together and cutting through walls between them. Quonset huts were not the easiest shape to use, but over the next few weeks, the cluster began to take shape as a single structure, thirty by thirty, fifteen feet high.

Li and Darrin set up a large kitchen. A crew pulling out from Shackleton sold her a convection oven and some leftover food, including a dozen #10 cans of tomato paste. Darrin mounted speakers in the ceiling panels and hooked them into an old media server loaded with rock and Chinese pop. The floor was still bare metal, and the air always carried the scent of lithium grease. But it felt different. It was a place to sit. A place where you could let your mind wander.

Li watched the cable. A miner was attached. He had come in out of Lexell Crater. His claim lay in shadow now, the sun gone for two weeks, and his crawler was useless without solar power. He clipped his last load to the line, then clipped himself on behind it and followed it in. The QR code on his chest matched the code on his bags, so transfers were automatic.

The ride was silent except for the faint vibration of the cable through his harness. Below him, the Moon stretched away in black and silver, illuminated only by blue earthshine.

When he got to Tycho Crater, he was dazzled by all the lights. This was his first time seeing what was at the end of the cableway. He noticed the large structure they had built. He stood outside for a while, wondering what it was. One section was a mirrored glass window. He looked at himself, the QR code on his chest identifying his claim, dust packed into the seams of his gloves. The lights inside were warm in a way lunar light never was. Li saw him through the window and waved him in.

“There’s stew,” she said when he came in. “Real carrots. Whiskey too, if you’re paying.”

He sat down like he’d forgotten what a chair felt like. The warmth hit first, then the smell of broth and roast beef. He sat, ate everything in the bowl, and drank more than he planned to. He wept as he ate. When he was done eating, he looked out the Earth window, lost in his own thoughts.

The next day, three more miners showed up. They all came from claims where the sun had just dipped below the horizon. Each had dropped off a final bag and decided to follow it down the line, like the miner from Lexell crater.

She didn’t run it at a loss. Everything cost. A hundred milligrams ³He for a hamburger, twice that for a beer. A shot of whiskey could be as much as a gram. The prices were high, but ³He mining paid well. Once the sun set and the work stopped, the food drew them in.

She stayed open through the lunar night. The air recycler couldn’t quite erase the smell of lithium grease. Miners came in, lingered longer than they meant to, and left full.

Li liked serving the guests, but every hour in the cantina was an hour she wasn’t mining. That was the real problem. She put up a sign, “Cook wanted,” with no expectation anyone would answer. Mining paid too well.

One night, a man asked about it. He was making decent money on his claim, he said, but the solitude was grinding him down. Li offered five grams a day. He took it immediately.

With a full-time cook, the quality of the food improved, and Li got even busier serving. Another miner came in asking if she was still looking for a cook. His sweeper had broken. Regolith seeped into everything, and he was done with the constant maintenance. Li hired him as a bartender.

Li still filled in on occasion, but she had time to herself again. People loved the simple meals, warm drinks, and old music. Nobody rushed you out. That was enough. Miners called it Tan’s. Li liked that. She hung a sign outside the airlock. “Tan’s Cantina”.

While they were getting ready for bed, Li talked about the hires. “The cantina gives me a place to think. That’s great. But what really made it work was hiring some staff. Miners get better food and better service. I think we can hire more people to improve operations.”

“We can post some job openings in the cantina. See if anyone is interested,” Darrin said.

The first new hire was Pavel Kerensky. He was a naval reactor tech before he came to the Moon. He’d spent five years in nuclear subs and knew the Soviet reactor’s internals better than anyone else on the Moon.

He didn’t talk much, just circled the reactor pit with a dosimeter and a notebook, checking welds, testing the coolant flow, running diagnostics on the control systems. On the third day, he came back with a list of modifications.

“The control rods are sluggish. The Soviet spec called for replacement every eighteen months of operation. Belov put in fresh ones, but I want spares on hand.”

“How many spares?” Li asked.

“Full set. Plus redundant coolant pumps.” He looked up from the notes. “You want this thing running for years, yes?”

“Yes.”

“Then we do it right.”

Pavel continued, checking the hot loop with a Geiger counter. “Darrin, where is your polonium trap?”

Darrin was confused. “There is no polonium, Pavel. This is just lead and bismuth. Lunar background is higher than on Earth.”

“It was lead and bismuth. The reactor makes polonium.” Pavel ran the counter slowly over the pipe, noting the spikes. “We need an extractor.”

Pavel walked Darrin through the sparger design. “We inject helium here, where the metal comes out of the reactor.”

“How will we force the helium in without hot metal pouring out?”

“Sparge on the lee side of a wedge and the metal won’t come back into it. We only need to overcome the Laplace pressure. A standard air pump will have adequate pressure. Not much volume.”

He drew a diagram on his slate. “Inject here, at the bottom of the hot pipe, right where it comes out of the reactor. Three feet later, we collect the bubbles. Run them through a Graham condenser.”

“Like in chemistry lab?”

“Same thing, but made of tungsten to block radiation and cooled with ammonia. We make two cartridges, so we can swap them out. We will need someplace to dump the polonium, though. Far away and easy to get to.”

“How about the ridge near the solar panels? The cableway goes by it, so we can get there easily, and it is miles from anyone.”

“Da. That will work well. I’ll bury a heavy steel pipe there. We bring the condenser up, heat it electrically, and the polonium will drip into the pipe. Then we cap it.”

“Won’t it fill up?”

Pavel shrugged. “When it does, we can make another. The half-life is short. In five years, it is quite safe.”

Li noticed that Pavel was asking the questions they had never considered. She gave Pavel the budget and let him shut down the reactor while he tinkered. He ordered the parts through Lunar Tug, spent a month installing them, and another week running the reactor through every failure mode he could simulate. When he was done, he pronounced it safe.

“Safe enough for what?” Darrin had asked.

Pavel shrugged. “For us to sleep at night.”

After that, the reactor became his. He ran it steadily, kept the temperature curves smooth, and made sure it didn’t kill them.

Luke Miller was hanging around Tan’s Cantina until Lunar Tug had space to bring him back to Earth. He saw Darrin eating and sat beside him. “I see you are landing regolith faster than you are processing it. What’s your plan?”

“Eat fast, get back to processing.”

Luke chuckled. “That’s a good plan. I was noticing you winch the bags up before dumping them in the oven. I’m stuck here for a couple of days. Mind if I make a suggestion?”

“What do you mean?”

“I’ve been watching the cableway drop bags on the crater floor, and you climb down, attach them to a cable, and winch them up. I was an industrial process engineer. I’d set it up so the cableway drops the bags on a chute conveyor. If you have a wide impact bed, you can easily handle surges, and your operations will be smoother and faster.”

“I don’t have time to build that.”

“You don’t have time to do what you’re doing now, either. Can I ask how hot the waste dump is?”

Darrin’s chest puffed a little. “We’re running them at over a thousand degrees.”

“I thought so. You can alter the ovens for heat recuperation. Getting four times the bake with the same amount of thermal input would be easy.”

“You want to do all that?”

“I thought you’d never ask.”

With that crew in place, Li and Darrin had time again.

In the morning, the automatic lights in their hut hadn’t come up yet. Earthshine spilled pale blue across the bed. The Earth hung unmoving outside the viewport. She lay still, listening to the hum of the air circulator and the slow, even breathing of Darrin beside her.

“You awake?” she murmured.

“Mmhmm,” came the answer, muffled by the pillow.

She stretched beneath the blanket, her muscles sore in a different way now. “We need more huts.”

Darrin rolled onto his back. “For workers?”

“Sure,” she said. “But that’s not what I meant. Miners come in during lunar night. They have a meal, maybe a drink. But when they are tired, they have to go back to their own hut in the cold, in the dark, to sleep. They spend their batteries. Then they wake up with nothing to do, so they come here again.”

“So you aren’t talking about a few huts.”

“No. Dozens of rooms.”

“A hotel?”

She swung her legs over the edge of the bunk, the floor cold under her feet. From the drawer, she pulled her undersuit, sliding it on with practiced ease. “You can call it that. Air, heat, water, and they would be close to the cantina. If that existed, miners might even work a little into the dark, because they wouldn’t be afraid of darksiding. I’d keep rent low, like half a gram per night.”

Darrin laughed. “Low.” Putting on his own undersuit, he continued. “You’re not wrong. Just sounds like a lot of work.”

“They’re coming here anyway. We should give them a bed and a door that locks.”

“You’re turning this into a town.”

“Not yet. But we’re past the camp stage.”

“Alright. I’ll lay it out. Water, power, oxygen. Just don’t ask me to handle linens.”

“You’re the best city planner I’ve got.”

Two days later, Darrin had a plan: a five-by-ten grid of used huts, with utilities, thermally anchored under regolith.

People started staying through the lunar night. Some stayed into the day. It wasn’t enough. By the next month, he was laying out fifty more.

The music system had burned out again, leaving only the low hum of the recycler and the occasional clink of a glass. Darrin sat in the corner, tracing a cableway route on his slate. A lot of miners had built their own lines from their claim to the main route, and he was trying to make sense of the tangle. The new links made people more social. The regolith still all went to Tan’s Refinery, but the miners also visited their neighbors. Without plan or permission, the cableways had become the Moon’s primary transit system.

Li sat with her boots off, legs stretched, staring through the viewport at the growing pile of processed regolith. Mounds of lifeless gray ash lay half-lit by Earthshine beyond the huts.

Tseng had taken to spending evenings here, claiming the background noise helped him think. He was relaxing in the cantina at the far table when Li noticed him.

“Remind me again,” Li said without turning, “what you said was in the tailings piles.”

“You have different minerals coming in,” he said. “Ilmenite is particularly valuable, and you can pull it out magnetically.”

“Why would we do that?”

He shrugged. “It’s an iron-titanium oxide. We could, in theory, extract the titanium from it. That ore is over thirty percent titanium by weight, and valuable by metal standards. ³He is measured in parts per billion. The titanium is actually worth more than the ³He per ton of regolith.”

Li took a sip of water and leaned back in her chair. “What would it take?”

Tseng set the slate down and leaned forward. “You serious?”

“Serious enough to listen. You said titanium. Helion Dynamics is paying very well for titanium right now.”

He rubbed his jaw. “Alright. The first problem is heat. You’d want the FFC Cambridge process. It strips oxygen from ilmenite and leaves you with titanium and iron without using a lot of other chemicals or air.”

“If you need heat, we’ve got a reactor.”

“Sure, but it’s already busy. You’d need to slow down other processing. Better yet, get a second core just for the furnace.”

Li gave him a look. “The reactor’s barely running.”

He frowned. “You’re pulling at least two megawatts.”

“Electric,” she said. “Yeah. The generator gives us ten if we want it, but we’re pulling around two. Add another megawatt thermal for the ovens. But the reactor’s rated for a hundred fifty megawatts thermal. We’re barely tickling it. With Pavel on board, we can pull the full one fifty.”

That made him sit up. “A hundred fifty megawatts?”

“If we feed it right. You give me a reason, and Pavel will give you the power.”

He leaned back, already calculating. “That changes things.”

“How do we separate the titanium from the iron?”

“That’s trickier. You’d need carbon monoxide. The Moon is carbon-poor, but the gas gets recycled, so it’s not impossible. You can buy some from Earth, and we will just keep reusing that.”

“And then we’re sitting on titanium bars?”

“More like sponges. You’d still have to melt them. That’s phase two. Clean it, melt it, cast it, ship it.”

Li let the silence stretch. Outside the viewport, the slag pile lay pale in the Earthlight.

“We can send bars back on Lunar Tug,” she said.

Tseng shook his head. “No. That will never work. Helium works that way because it’s light and pricey. Metals, even at Raines’ rates, don’t fly well.” He paused, eyes narrowing. “But if you sling them with a mass driver, that changes things. Aim for the Pacific, and let them drop.”

She laughed. “You already picked a splash zone?”

“Not yet,” he said, lifting the slate. “But if this project is a go, I will work it out.”

“How big?”

He tapped a few numbers. “Thousand-ton slabs. Big enough to recover, small enough not to vaporize the ocean. Slow them with surface area and an ablative heat shield, keep them under Mach two at impact. Most of the heat shield burns off, and the rest shatters on impact with the ocean. Make them bowl-shaped for stability and cast an eye into the back so a salvage crew can winch it out. It will land flat, and it won’t bury itself in the mud.”

“You’ve thought this through.”

“It works,” he said. “Raines will pay for titanium, even if it’s salty. He’ll fish it out without complaint.”

Li brought up her slate and began calculating. “Tell me more about the mass driver.”

“We’ll need a precision instrument from Earth,” Tseng said. “We can mount it on the ridge. Hitting Earth is easy. Getting it to slow down right, break into the atmosphere correctly, and land where it’s supposed to? That takes real calibration. No shortcuts.”

Li went to the window. The tailings piles caught the faint light, their long shadows stretching across the basin floor, marking her life, devoted to power. Tseng’s idea would erase that pile.

“It’s not just about helium anymore,” she said.

Tseng looked up.

When she saw her reflection in the window, she thought about her family photo. She realized her parents were smiling. This was what goodbye felt like.

“Neither am I.”

She turned toward the kitchen. The stew would still be warm, and someone would be hungry soon.
