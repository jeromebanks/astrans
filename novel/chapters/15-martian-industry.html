Part I · The Spark

Chapter 15

Martian Industry

8,625 words · about 36 minutes

Jakob was up before dawn. “You ready, Callum?”

“I reckon we can take a swing down and find you some ice,” Callum said.

Jakob did not need convincing. They went to the runway and climbed into the gyroplane, ready to check every ridge and hollow on their route south. Callum showed Jakob how to use the shallow radar.

About twenty miles out, Jakob leaned toward the screen. “I found ice,” he said, unable to hide his excitement.

Callum studied the return. “You did, but it is barely anything. We should keep going.”

They crossed a series of shallow basins. Jakob picked up several more signals, each one thin and scattered. With Callum’s guidance, he learned to distinguish lava tubes, dense ore, and ice. He found a few more deposits, but Callum waved them off as insignificant.

Then, a hundred twenty miles out, the display lost its structure. The basin filled edge to edge, every depth reporting the same return. Jakob leaned closer to the screen. “Callum, take a look. It’s saturated. It’s ice all the way down.”

“That is the good stuff, mate. Looks miles thick. Plenty for what you want.”

“Can we set down?” Jakob asked.

“If you like,” Callum said, setting the gyrocopter down on the plain.

Jakob grabbed a shovel from the cargo space and started digging. “I want to see how clean it is.”

After several shovelfuls of dry regolith, Jakob grew frustrated. “Hey, this is dry as dust. There’s no water here. The reading showed solid ice.”

Callum laughed. “When the radar gets saturated like that, you know there’s a lot of ice, but you can’t tell how deep it starts. Could be thousands of feet down. You can’t reach it like that. Petey and I will come back later and do a proper reading.”

Jakob’s shoulders slumped slightly as he realized his shovel would never reach that ice. He put the shovel away, and they started the flight back.

When they returned, Ellie was bent over a block of carbon, checking measurements on her slate. The generator’s hum filled the air. She did not look up until their boots crunched across the dirt.

“Did you boys find anything?”

“We found water. Callum says it’s an enormous field, beyond the horizon, more than we could ever use.”

Mira had come over, wiping regolith dust from her gloves. “How far?”

“Hundred twenty miles south,” Jakob said. “The radar lit up solid blue.”

Ellie straightened, interest flickering across her tired face. “Fantastic! If we can tap it, we don’t need the polar runs.”

“Right,” Jakob said, already thinking through logistics. “We’ll need to set up an extraction site. Same process as Magazine Beach. We haul it all back here, use the settlement’s power to process it.”

Callum set his helmet down on the workbench with a heavy thunk. “Nah, mate. Won’t work like that.”

Jakob turned. “What do you mean?”

“Magazine Beach was surface ice, yeah? Frozen ice sitting right there. Easy as. This is different. This could be thousands of feet down. Permafrost under pressure. You can’t just saw it.”

“So how do we get it?” Mira asked.

“No worries. My team’s done plenty of in situ salt extraction in Southern Australia. Same process. We can bore that for you once we get our own mine sorted. Petey’s making coring bits for us, and he can make your drilling gear, too. We should be ready to take a look in a couple of weeks.

Tom recognized this was out of their league. “So, Callum, what can our team do?”

“The main thing is you are going to need hot water. You build a way to make hot water, you inject that, it does the melty thing down below, you pump out cold water.”

Jakob felt he misunderstood. “We inject water to get water?”

“Too right. You’re not mining blocks of ice, you’re pumping liquid water. ”

Ellie started thinking about power. “How hot?”

“Hot as you can. Ninety degrees going down. Maybe more. Four degrees coming back up.”

Ellie and Tom sidebarred on heating. “Tom, do you think we should go pull the solar panels from Magazine Beach?”

“I was thinking the same thing, Ellie,” Tom confided. “But I have a better idea. You can make glass sheets. We mirror those with a tiny bit of aluminum. Solar thermal. More efficient and much easier to make.”

“OK, I can make the reflectors. Are we going to bubble the extractor side?”

Tom nodded. “The bubble here on Bounty works great. Everything is so much easier without the dust. It will keep our collectors clean, too. What do we do at night?”

“Stop injecting, drain the collector. Start it up again in the morning. But we will still need a small electric panel to run the pumps.”

“That should be no problem. Callum brought a bunch with him. Mostly deployed up by their mining site, but if they have spares, we can use those. Otherwise, it is a long ride down to Magazine Beach for Mira and me.”

When they rejoined the group, Callum was talking about gas relief. “You get deep, the trapped gas is under huge pressure. CO₂, mostly. Trapped in the ice for millennia. It’ll fight you if you don’t vent it controlled-like. Just a reed valve on top. You want some pressure.”

Jakob felt a sinking realization settle in his gut. This wasn’t something his team could improvise. “Look, my team hasn’t got these skills. We don’t know how to do this.”

“I do,” Callum said simply. “My boys drill and mine for a living. We’ll handle the extraction site. You lot handle the heat and getting the water back.”

Mira’s years on oilfields kicked in. “If it is coming out liquid, the only realistic plan is to pipe it back.”

Everyone knew she was right. Production meant there had to be a pipeline.

“Hundred twenty miles of pipe. That’s a big scale for us,” Pacha said quietly.

Ellie nodded slowly. “I can make that much glass, but it will be slow to lay and fragile.”

Callum grinned. “Glass pipe. You lot do everything the hard way. You need something flexible. How about polymer pipe? An inch may do. Go with two. One ton per mile. You can probably move a hundred tons of water daily with that if you push it. More than you need, right? Petey can make you some dies for that.”

Jakob looked at Callum. “How long to drill?”

“Two weeks if the ground cooperates. Maybe three.”

Jakob nodded. “That would be appreciated.”

Callum grinned. “She’ll be right, mate. I’m getting back to my crew. Let us know when you need us.”

The next morning, Callum’s team suited up and got on the cableway. Callum was pleased with their success in building that. It carried crew and equipment right up to the mine and right back down to Bounty. Jakob watched them secure the rig components, each piece strapped down with the casual efficiency of people who had done this a thousand times.

“You’ll want to come see it once we’re set up,” Callum said. “Right at the base of Tom’s new mass driver.”

“We’d love to see it,” Jakob replied.

Jakob turned back to the settlement, where Ellie and Tom were already laying out the pipeline route on a terrain map.

“Straight shot, one hundred twenty-five miles. Let’s do it.” Tom said.

“Nah,” Mira said. “Straight pipes always break. Pipes need to zigzag so they can handle the heat and cold. Expect to add about thirty percent, turns every ten miles. That’s what we did in my dad’s oil fields.”

“One hundred sixty-five miles of pipe,” Ellie calculated. She looked up at Jakob. “That’s a lot, but we can do it.”

Mira was already calculating on her slate. “If we lay five miles a day, it will take less than two months, but that means we won’t be making enough polymer for Pacha. If he doesn’t get his greenhouse, we are all going to stop eating soon.”

Jakob had faced this problem before. They have to deliver, and they have to eat. Last time, they had to deliver. That worked out. This time, there were more people involved, and their margins were thinner. They had to eat. “Alright. The first greenhouse is the priority. After that, half and half. Callum’s crew has their own stuff to do. We let them make steel when they need to. Need to make them carbon for alloying with the iron, too. Can’t stop that, but it won’t be a lot, and we should be able to make a lot of polymer at night. Meantime, Tom and I can start surveying and digging in that trench. Mira, can you make the solar heating troughs? I think we have enough to do for the next month while Ellie and Pacha get that first greenhouse built. Pacha, will that work?”

“Yeah, boss. We can swing it. No worries.”

Mira didn’t complain. “I’ll start fabricating the trough mirrors today. We’ll need at least two hundred square meters of collector surface. “

Tom seemed to be calculating something on his slate. “I think the trenching will be pretty straightforward. With Callum’s excavator, we can probably trench ten miles in a day.”

Jakob watched them work through the details, felt the weight of the project settling onto all their shoulders. This wasn’t as simple as their first operation. The infrastructure had more moving parts and affected more people. But the upside was huge. Permanent, load‑bearing infrastructure that would provide water for the entire inner solar system.

“Let’s get started,” he said.

Petey was satisfied with only five tons of carbon. Ellie got that done the first day, so she didn’t mind. What she hated was when he made iron. That stopped polymer production for up to twelve hours. He was extracting iron, then alloying it with carbon to make mild steel, then forming tubes. He never ran at night, though, and he spent at least two days a week sharpening and grinding, which didn’t use much power.

Pacha just focused on dirt and plants. He trimmed the cavy herd back a little. He had to conserve their feed. That meant extra meat for a couple of days, then down to half as much as they were used to. More algae soup, so less algae biomass for his terra preta.

After two weeks, Tom and Jakob had trenched about ten miles using the excavator. They were eager to start getting pipe in there, but Ellie was focused on making polymer for the greenhouse.

When she finally had enough for the greenhouse, everyone but Mira went with Ellie to start laying down the first large greenhouse. They brought three sleds, the excavator, and a 3D printer.

Ellie, Jakob, and Pacha each had a sled. Tom got on the excavator and started pulling out regolith. Dumping it on their sleds. They hauled it off, just twenty feet north of the greenhouse. As they got closer, Tom asked for direction. “You want it two feet deep? Any slope?”

“Pacha was out of breath from pushing the sleds. “No slope. Two feet straight down. That’s my soil level.”

As soon as the excavation was done, Ellie jumped up on the 3D printer and set in the instructions for putting in the foam floor. There was plenty of fine powder to strengthen it. As soon as the base was done, the corrugated wall started. This one used dust, sand, and stone. It was just like the perimeter wall at the settlement, but this one was thicker and went up four feet, straightening at the top. Tom and Pacha bolted the rails on the inside for Pacha’s equipment to ride. It was a long print, and even with the excavator to keep the hopper loaded, it was tough work. By evening, they had stretched the polyethylene sheet over the structure and secured it with Petey’s steel mesh. Tom brought the power cable over before dinner time, and they turned on the compressor, aiming for one-half psi. The air recycler was set for 0.03 psi partial pressure CO₂. Nice and rich for the plants.

They went back and started dinner.

Callum and Petey were talking as they ate.

Callum was eating the small ration of roast cavy, telling Petey the news. “Yeah, tomorrow is the first blast. We drilled a ring of twenty holes. Electric cardox charges. That why wThat’sed the big twin screw.”

“Callum, I don’t mind sitting down here making steel all day long. I like making sure the pipes and bits are made right. But you can’t cut me out of the exciting parts. I have to be up there to see the seam blow.”

“Oh, yeah, mate. Of course. We should invite the Magazine Beach mob, too, don’t you think?”

Jakob leaned back. “We would love to see what you guys have up there. We will take the day off tomorrow to watch your team work.”

The next morning, the team was excited. They needed the rest after building the greenhouse. Mira loved the cable ride. She sat by Pacha. They started out low and easy, but when they got to the scarp, it kept the same speed while going straight up the face. A couple of horizontal towers kept them off the face. Then they were up on the shield. Mira was wiggling around, looking out over Mars and over the shield. “So different up here,” she whispered to Pacha.

He was doing the same thing. “Amazing,” he replied. “I feel like I can see back to our old Magazine Beach site.” They both laughed.

When they got to the mine, it didn’t look like they expected. Crushers and grinders, with the sun glinting off rows of solar panels. Drill rigs and hardware. The actual mine itself didn’t look like anything.

Callum’s team handled the steel cartridges with the careful precision of miners who’d spent years around explosives. The cardox charges were steel cylinders filled with liquid CO₂, an electric heater, and a rupture disc. They were not going to detonate from a bump, but old habits ran deep. Every charge was treated like it could kill you, because they had handled other charges that could. The habit was what kept them alive. Twenty charges were lowered into holes in a ring about forty feet from the center. They covered the top with stones and regolith and tamped it down.

Then Callum got them all back behind a small metal wall two hundred feet away. He carefully inserted each wire in the trigger box. Picking up his slate, he started counting. “One!” and there was a huge thump. The ground shook, and the rock broke. Debris shot straight up. But there was no pause. He just said “Two!” and the event repeated. He counted out each hole until all twenty were blown.

Mira looked at Pacha. “I wasn’t expecting that.”

“Yeah, I totally assumed he would count down or something. That first one was a shock. But the second one, I think I actually pissed myself. They both laughed.

“I know! I could feel the thud run right up my legs. That was amazing.”

When the dust cloud settled, Jakob looked at the broken rock piled in the center. He could see streaks of green.

Callum came up. “How was the show?”

“That was amazing. We were so close. I can see the green streaks you are after in the rock.”

“Yeah, Cardox lets you get close. Really feel it. But those green streaks, those are just copper. The stuff we’re after is gray. I don’t know if you can even see it.”

The miners went in and retrieved the Cardox cylinders and began putting them on their racks.

“Beautiful blast, that. We’ll be mucking this out for weeks.” Callum gestured at the pile. “Which reminds me, Jakob, we’re going to need to keep the excavator up here now. We’ll be shifting overburden daily.”

Jakob felt his stomach drop. “How long?”

“Permanently, mate. This is just the start. Once we clear this lot, we’ll be opening the next section.”

“But we still need the excavator. You can’t just take it; we’re using it,” Ellie said.

Jakob interrupted. “Callum, that excavator has been a fantastic help to us. Thanks for letting us use it for so long. We’ve got some refiguring to do, but we’ll be fine.”

Ellie interjected again. “How about you guys use it during the day, and we work at night, just shuttling it back and forth?”

Jakob tried to quiet her, but Callum spoke first. “Moving the excavator isn’t just a ride up the cableway. My whole team will work for a full day to get that thing safely up the scarp. Multiple ropes. Then, when it’s up here, it is a ten-hour drive to the mine. Back and forth won’t work. But don’t worry, mates; it’ll work out.”

They rode down in silence. Jakob and Mira had reached the ten‑mile mark. Not done or even halfway, but something.

Ellie was thinking about building more greenhouses without the excavator. She could do that. A one-day job would take a week or so, but it could happen.

Pacha knew this was trouble, but his mind was on the first greenhouse and getting that going. He remembered using shovels to keep the 3D printer running, and wasn’t concerned.

Tom knew this was a disaster. There was no way they could keep trenching with hand shovels. They would be lucky to lay one segment a day. Forty thousand days to finish this. He would pretend, but this was not real anymore.

As they came down the scarp, the pipeline stretched out like a fragile thread connecting the settlement to a distant promise to nowhere.

The next morning, Jakob and Tom suited up before dawn. They loaded two sleds with thirty bundles of ten pipes each, a pipe welder, and shovels. The settlement was quiet with only the soft hiss of recyclers breaking the silence.

Jakob was welding the pipe, and Tom was laying it in place. After ten segments, they went back and started pushing the berm back into the trench. They put a small metal plate on the front of one, which made pushing easier, but not easy. They were coming home exhausted, but after a week, they had finished ten miles that had already been trenched. It was slow, but it was progress.

The next day, they set to digging. Tom was looking at a manual. “The trench needs to be three feet deep. With the angle of repose of this regolith, the top is almost five feet from the center.”

Jakob looked at the line stretching ahead and did the math in his head. For every twenty-foot segment, they’d need to move nearly three hundred cubic feet of regolith. And they couldn’t just toss it aside because it would slide right back in. They’d have to carry it at least nine feet away from the center.

He picked up his shovel and started digging.

The regolith came up easily at first, light and powdery in the reduced gravity. But after the first few inches, it got denser. Compacted. He had to put his weight into it, feeling the resistance through the shovel handle, even with the dulled feedback from his gloves.

Tom worked beside him, establishing the width. Five feet from the centerline on both sides. They marked the edges with stones, then began working their way down.

Scoop. Carry. Drop. Return. Scoop. Carry. Drop.

After an hour, Jakob’s shoulders burned. The suit’s cooling system was running hard, trying to keep up with his body heat. His breathing echoed loud inside his helmet.

They kept going. The trench slowly deepened. Six inches. A foot. Eighteen inches.

Then Tom’s shovel hit something solid with a dull clang. He stopped, crouched down, and brushed away regolith with his gloved hand. A rock the size of a melon sat embedded in the compacted soil.

“Great,” he muttered.

He wedged the shovel underneath and pried. The rock shifted but didn’t come free. Jakob came over, and together they worked it loose, rocking it back and forth until it finally popped out. They carried it twenty feet away and dropped it.

Back to digging.

Two feet down. Two and a half. The walls wanted to slump inward. They had to widen the top edge to maintain the angle, which meant more material to move.

They stopped for lunch. In pressure suits, the only way to eat was paste. They ate paste lunch in silence, sitting on the sled, staring at the endless plain stretching south. They had cleared thirty feet of trench to full depth. Jakob stood at one end, looking at the long, neat cut in the Martian surface. It had taken six hours. Two hours later, they had dug enough for two segments. Installing the segments was easy. The welder only took a few minutes, and the pipe was light.

Tom looked at the rock sitting twenty feet from the trench. “That rock we moved? Dumb. We could easily have just gone around it.”

Jakob nodded, exhausted and humiliated. They had laid two segments today. He brought up the slate, but didn’t log it, yet. He didn’t want to see the number. Two segments. Tens of thousands to go.

That night at dinner, no one talked much. Ellie was running numbers on polymer production. Tom was sketching something on his slate but kept erasing it. Pacha was happy, sorting seeds for the greenhouse.

Jakob’s entire body ached. His hands were stiff, his back tight, his legs trembling slightly from the day’s work. He flexed his fingers, trying to work feeling back into them.

“How far did you get?” Ellie asked.

“Two segments,” Jakob said.

Ellie nodded slowly. She opened her mouth, closed it, said nothing. Everyone knew the math.

The second day was worse. The novelty was gone. Now it was just hard, repetitive, grinding labor.

They dug. They carried. They dumped. They dug again.

That evening, Jakob logged their progress.

Jakob logged it on his slate.

Day: 2
Segments done: 4
Segments remaining: 40,916

“This is impossible,” Tom said finally.

Jakob didn’t answer. They both knew that from the day they visited the mine. The numbers didn’t lie. Two per day. Fifty-six years. Half that if two crews worked. Jakob smiled grimly to himself.

That night at dinner, Magazine Beach got together. Jakob spoke first.

“Guys, this is not working. We are laying a section or two every day. At this rate, we won’t get paid, and the Cereans won’t get the water they need to live. Bounty is nice, but I think we need to face reality. We cannot meet our obligations here. I don’t know what else to do. We need to set up a site on Planum Boreum and start cutting ice. Ice delivery is why we are on Mars. Ceres needs it. Bounty needs it.”

Tom looked up. “Yes, I looked at the numbers, too. I don’t know what to do, but I agree with your proposal. We have to move on. Pacha, can you fly up sometimes and bring us food? ”

“Dudes, I don’t want to hear this. You think I can grow food here without water. This is a mess. The first large greenhouse is growing well. I am washing and tumbling regolith to get nice dirt, adding carbon and organic matter, but the real thing is, I have put in plants to nourish the mycorrhiza in the soil. But to keep it going I need water, too. If you move, what am I going to do. Fly in water?”

“I hate it as much as anyone,” Jakob explained, “But we cannot lay that pipe fast enough to make a difference. Our first obligation here is to ship ice. The time we bought ourselves with the slow shipments is coming close to an end. We have three months left to start shipping or it will be a problem for Ceres.”

Petey was eating by himself at the next table. He overheard them, but didn’t say anything.

That night in the ag dome, the team was down. Mira cried as she packed up.

Jakob kept it inside for the sake of the team, but it hurt. “Guys, pack very light. We need to bring hydrolox for power. It is dark where we were.”

Ellie just stared at him. “What are we doing? Are we going to build a new dome?”

“I am open to suggestions, Ellie, but yeah. We will move our solar panels up, and build a new dome on Planum Boreum. Probably smaller.”

Tom was stoic, working on his slate. He wasn’t sure hydrolox would provide enough power for the mass driver.

Mira wasn’t taking it well, but didn’t want to talk about it.

At dinner that night, Callum and his team came down from the mine. They looked tired but satisfied, their suits streaked with dust and rock powder. Callum dropped into a chair with a satisfied grunt.

“Four days on, three days off,” he said to no one in particular. “That’s the rhythm. You work yourself ragged, then you rest proper. Keeps you sharp. Keeps you safe.”

The miners talked to each other a few minutes, then Pacha brought over plates of roasted oca, algae stew with a little cavy meat in it, and flatbread.

Callum took his gratefully. “How’s the pipeline coming?” he asked Jakob.

“Slow,” Jakob said.

“Yeah, Petey said as much. He wants to help.”

Jakob looked down, depressed. “Callum, we have looked at our options, and have reached a sad conclusion. My team is going to have to make the move north. We have an obligation to get water to Ceres and to your crew, and this pipeline is not working. We are going to need you to fly us up, and bring us food regularly.”

Callum laughed, a genuine, relaxed sound. “Well, before you build a new camp, I want to show you something Petey made for you this morning.” Callum left for a few minutes and came back with a big piece of steel, flat with a curved tube on it. “This here is a plow share for what we call plow laying. You feed the pipe in the top tube and pull the plow along. It deposits the pipe three feet down, easy as. Well, not exactly easy, but you can move with this. No digging.”

Jakob stared at him. “What?”

Callum set down his fork. “Right. Sometimes I forget you lot aren’t miners.” He pulled out his slate and sketched quickly. “You’re digging trenches by hand, yeah? That’s brutal. What you need is a plow. You pull it through the regolith, it lays the pipe. And It’s easy to plow this regolith. Just keep moving, welding the sections of tubing together. You can drive it straight through. Not zero work, but easier than digging. And much faster.”

Mira leaned forward, staring at the sketch. “That works in regolith?”

“Better than anywhere. Loose soil, almost no rocks, piece of cake. Petey heard you were having trouble, thought this would help. It doesn’t strain the pipe. Much smoother.”

Jakob felt something unclench in his chest. “How fast does it work?”

“Depends on the ground, but in this stuff, an experienced crew could lay five miles a day. You lot aren’t experienced. We’ll see.”

Jakob looked at Tom, saw the relief flooding across his face. Then at Ellie, who was smiling faintly.

“Callum,” Jakob said, “can you show us how it works?”

“Sure, mate. First thing tomorrow.” Callum picked up his fork again. “So you know, we all are hoping you folks don’t have to move back south. We like having you around.”

Tom and Jakob talked that night, privately. “Look, Jakob, this project is beyond us. I love the plow laying concept, but we need to be real. This may be another way to fail, and we don’t have much time before we need to start launching ice.”

Jakob looked at him with eyes that could swallow Mars. “I’ve been thinking the same thing. We’ve bitten off more than we can chew with this project. To move, to make a Magazine Beach North, that’s going to take time. But we are going to see how it goes tomorrow.”

Tom and Jakob kept the conversation private and went to bed.

The next day, Callum gathered them around. “OK, first step is to break into tasks. Plow laying is a continuous process. Your pipe is in twenty-foot segments. How are you connecting the segments?”

Ellie spoke up. “I was going to use compression fittings because they are so easy to use, but making so many fittings was daunting. We use butt fusion. Mira helped make the machine, and it only takes a minute to make welds as strong as the pipe.”

“Good on you. I want you two to start welding the pipe. It will take time, but we want a whole, single, continuous pipe from one end to the other. Does that make sense?”

Mira spoke up brightly, “Yeah, we can do that. How close does it have to lie?”

“Oh, just wherever. Anyplace within about ten yards will be fine. The pipe will move itself into the plow. Just make sure the lengths match.”

Ellie and Mira smiled, eager to begin.

“OK, you lot,” Callum continued, looking at Jakob and Tom, “You boys are going to be doing the real work. You push that sled along the track. Add some weight to it, maybe so the traction assist can help. Make sure the pipe is moving in smoothly and follow your map. Sometimes it will hit rocks. Stop, get out the shovels and picks, and figure out the problem. You can back this up a little when you need to, and the pipe will come out. Clear the obstruction and move on. If you can see an obstruction coming up, you can navigate around it. Pipe doesn’t need to be straight. It is stronger when it has some bends.”

Tom considered the task. “A harness will let me pull. I think that will be stronger.”

“You do what you like. Drag that plow from one end to the other of this whole thing, and you will have laid the pipe. Got it?”

Tom and Jakob brought the sled for pulling the plowshare. They carried the plowshare, picks and axes, and ten bundles of pipe. Callum and Petey went with them.

Ellie and Mira brought another sled with them to carry the pipe from Bounty. The sled could carry thirty bundles of ten pipes. Mira bolted the butt welder to the sled, and she and Ellie got started. They could weld pretty fast.

Callum gave a lecture first, to make sure Jakob and Tom understood the plan. Then they demonstrated plow laying. They laid the first mile about as fast as walking. Then they turned it over to Jakob and Tom to see how much they learned. When Jakob and Tom started, everything went wrong. Just getting the pipe to feed properly was far harder than it looked. It was under tension and would twist up. The hard problem was preventing the pipe from getting torqued as it was laid. The sled was another problem. Without enough weight on top, it didn’t have the traction to help pull the plow. If Tom pulled too hard, the front wheels lost all traction. If Jakob pushed too hard, the wheels went under the regolith. Coordination wasn’t easy. The real problem, though, was simply moving the plow, straining their legs and backs for every foot of progress. Jakob and Tom got only half a mile done before dinner. Yet when Jakob glanced back, he saw the line of pipe running straight and true behind them, the plow holding steady despite their inexperience.

Jakob caught Tom’s eye. “Look at what we’ve done. We can do this. This is working.”

They walked back to dinner. Partway back, they met Ellie and Mira.

Ellie smiled. “The welding went well. We welded forty bundles of ten segments, went home, and got thirty more bundles. All told, we covered over two and a half miles today.”

Jakob and Tom were in too much pain to talk. Just walking was pretty rough.

Mira laughed. “Get on the cart, you old men.”

They happily got on, lying down, making sure not to move any muscle for the entire ride home.

When they got back, Pacha had dinner ready.

Tom spoke up. “You two made it look so easy. That is hard work right there. But the thing is, we did it.”

Callum and the miners broke out in laughter. “She’ll be right, mate. Don’t wreck yourselves. I am scheduling time to drill your wells in a month. Reckon you can get it sorted by then.”

The next day, Callum went with them, but Petey stayed behind working on tools. Tom and Jakob were getting smoother. Mira and Ellie were very proud of their innovation. Mira had welded tie-downs on the sled, and they could carry fifty bundles of ten pipes. “We’re gonna weld twice as much pipe as last time,” Mira said with a smirk.

“You do that,” Callum replied.

Tom and Jakob were noticeably better, even though their muscles were sore. They usually kept the sled flat, and they were getting good at putting just the right amount of twist in the cable before it went under, so the cable on the ground didn’t get all kinked up. They got two miles in.

But this time, despite their enthusiasm in the morning, Ellie and Mira were in trouble. They could haul more in a load, but their endpoint moved. Each time, they were farther from Bounty. Longer back to town, longer back to the site. They got three miles in, one and a half loads. They left the sled and twenty-five bundles of pipe there and walked back. They ran into Callum, Jakob, and Tom.

Callum clapped Jakob and Tom on the shoulders. “Great work. You went more smoothly and faster. You’ll get better. Hey, Mira, no ride back for these boys?”

Ellie answered. “We didn’t finish our second load today. Walking back and forth takes too much time. In a week, we are going to have to start walking beach before we start welding. I don’t know what to do.”

Callum smiled. “We can jabber on that over dinner tonight. I’m not worried.”

At dinner, Callum sat by Tom. “So I hear you were a pilot back on Earth.”

“Yeah, general aviation. I had a private plane. Not a career or anything.”

Callum nodded. “What would you think about flying the gyro?”

“I mean, I can see that would be really helpful to the team, but I obviously have work to do, and we are going to need to figure out how to bring more pipe with us tomorrow. I am not sure I have time.”

“Oh, you’ve got time. You just don’t know how to use it, yet.” Callum said with a wink.

In the morning, Callum woke Tom early. “Let’s start loading the pipe. We can try ten bundles at a time.”

Tom and Callum started loading up the gyro. When they loaded the third bundle, Callum explained the plan. “We are going to move this stuff into position. Doesn’t have to be right, has to be close. What I need you to do is watch me closely. See every part of this, from takeoff, climbing, cruising, and landing. Got it?”

Tom looked at the controls. “Wilco.”

With that, Callum read the wind and faced into it, and engaged the driven landing gear. Tom watched the indicators as Callum lifted off. As they flew along, they saw Ellie and Mira’s sled, with 25 bundles on it.

Callum brought up his slate and figured out where they would be at the end of those bundles. “OK, mate. Kick out the first bundle.”

Callum tilted the gyro, slowing it and letting it go lower. Tom kicked out the first bundle. They repeated that maneuver every two thousand feet, following the map on the slate. When the last one was out, they headed back.

Tom watched as Callum brought it in, flaring, bringing it almost to a stop, then settling on the ground smoothly.

They loaded up another ten bundles, but this time, Callum had Tom fly, guiding him the whole way. Tom engaged the landing gear and tractor prop. When he saw the lift rotors turning, he pulled back and hopped about twenty feet.

“Let’er run a little further. See the mark I etched on the airspeed indicator? Don’t try to fly until you hit that.”

Tom kept at it and was soon flying like a pro. When they got to the next drop-off, Callum asked Tom to land. “Don’t worry about slow descents yet. After you have some miles under your belt, you can work on that. For now, let’s just unload this stuff.”

Tom found a place to set it down. He approached pretty steeply, not noticing how fast he was going down. They hit pretty hard, but nothing was broken.

Callum checked himself first, then explained. “The gyro works a lot better with forward airspeed. You can work on short landings later. For now, bring it down smooth like your plane from home. Deal?”

Tom looked sheepish. “Deal. Sorry.”

They unloaded all ten bundles, and Tom flew them back to Bounty smoothly. Tom thanked Callum. “Callum, this has been great, but it’s dawn, and I need to help Jakob.”

“Not today. Petey’ll work with Jakob today. Today you are doing prepositioning.”

Tom wasn’t sure what to say, so they went and grabbed another ten bundles. They worked like that all day, back and forth. Tom got much more comfortable with takeoffs and landings. Ellie and Mira set to welding, easily putting down twenty miles that day, and Jakob and Petey were fast. When it got late, Callum had Tom pick them up.

Ellie and Mira were first. Mira was excited. “Thanks, Tom! No trips back, we always had the stuff we needed on hand. I bet we welded fifteen miles of pipe today.”

Next, they stopped for Jakob and Petey. “Thanks for the lift. Hey Tom, no offense, but with Petey we made eight miles today.”

Tom laughed. Callum reminded them of his schedule. “OK, guys, we did a lot in three days. Tom, you keep flying people out. I wouldn’t bring any bundles when you do it now, though. You are going to have to work the plow with Jakob from now on.”

Tom nodded. That week went pretty well. They would bring a couple of bundles with them each morning when they flew to the starting point. In the evening, Tom and Mira would drop off another load. By the end of the week, they were laying four miles a day. By the end of the second week, they were up to six.

In two weeks, the pipe was fully welded. Ellie went back to helping build greenhouses, and Mira went back to making the solar thermal troughs. In a month, the pipeline and all two thousand square meters of troughs were completed.

As soon as the pipeline was done, Ellie wanted a test. She hooked up a large compressor to the Bounty side, and Tom flew Mira out to the extraction site.

Mira held a strip of Mylar next to the outlet. It didn’t move at all. “Tom, we might have torn that pipe anywhere along the way. We wouldn’t know; it was buried without us ever inspecting it.”

Tom nodded. “I guess you’re right. We will fly back over that area. If we can see a geyser of dust rising up, that will be our leak.”

Mira continued watching. She thought she saw some dust stirring. “OK, clearly the pipe is damaged, but something is still getting through. It must not be too bad.”

The wind grew steadily, lifting a small swirl into a rising column. Mira blinked, taking it in, and Tom’s mouth twitched into a grin. Mira’s chest tightened, then exploded. “It works! It actually works!” She spun toward Tom, laughing and crying at the same time.

Ellie had time to build a glass distiller to process the extracted water. She even started making the glass filament packages to carry the ice blocks. The rest of the Magazine Beach crew was helping Pacha build up more greenhouses.

After lunch, Jakob saw Tom and Petey loading up some twenty-foot casing joints on the gyroplane.

“Hey, Tom, can I join you? I want to see the drilling.”

Tom nodded quietly. “Sure, Jakob. We’re ready to go.”

Jakob always enjoyed the view as the gyroplane took off. Mars looked so different than Earth. The smaller radius meant the horizon was closer. It felt small. As they approached the drill site, the first thing Jakob noticed was the rows of heating troughs Mira had built, ready to heat injection water when this whole thing started. Soon, he saw the short derrick where Callum and his crew were working. The short derrick worked with the twenty-foot joints. Tom set the gyroplane down twenty yards from the structure.

Petey and Tom went in, carrying a single joint between them. Jakob followed.

“Hey, Callum, how is it going?”

“We’re at twelve hundred feet. Hit the first ice layer yesterday. Pressure’s higher than expected, but manageable. We are running out of joints, though.”

Petey and Tom set the joint down. “We got nine more in the gyro, if your crew can go get them. What is the pressure?”

“Nothing bad. Yet.” Callum tapped the gauge. “We’re at nineteen hundred psi downhole. That’s higher than I’d like, but it’s holding and well within our safety margins. The pressure manifold is venting CO₂ faster than I expected, is all.”

“Will it blow?”

“Nah, we’ll be careful. But when we start injecting heat, the pressure’s going to spike. We’ll need to balance heat injection with venting rate, or the whole formation fractures.”

Jakob looked at the manifold, the maze of valves and sensors. “How do you balance it?”

“Gently.” Callum grinned, but there was no humor in it. “We’ll start slow. I’m running CO₂ for a while until the temperature climbs to forty below. Probably half an hour. At that point, most of the frozen CO₂ will have sublimated. Then we will send down warmer air until we get near zero. When we are ready for the water, we trickle it in, five liters a minute. Watch the pressure. If it climbs past twenty‑two hundred, we shut down and sit while the gas vents.”

“When do you plan to start?”

“Tomorrow morning. Soon as the sun heats the troughs.”

Jakob didn’t sleep well that night. He lay in his bunk in the ag dome, listening to the soft hiss of the recycler. Outside, the Martian night was silent and cold, the stars sharp and unblinking.

At dawn, the team flew down and gathered around the manifold. Mira had the solar troughs aligned, and the holding tank was reading ninety‑two degrees Celsius.

“Ready?” Callum asked.

His team nodded. One of the miners opened the injection valve slowly, and heated CO₂ began flowing down into the frozen ground.

Jakob was surprised they didn’t inject water. They had done all this to make hot water to inject, after all, but he had learned that Callum talked in straight lines, but operated in a complex manner that was second nature to an experienced miner.

Callum saw Jakob’s confusion. “We are not sending water down yet. It’s too cold down there. We would just end up with a solid ice block at the end of our borehole. We’ll pump warm air until the temperature gets to about ten below.”

Jakob trusted Callum, even if he didn’t say anything. Still, he appreciated the occasional explanation.

For the first minute, nothing happened. The pressure gauge held steady at nineteen hundred psi. Then it ticked up. Nineteen ten. Nineteen twenty.

“Easy,” Callum murmured.

The pressure climbed. Nineteen fifty. Two thousand. Two thousand fifty.

“Gas vent is maxed,” one of the miners reported.

“Hold steady,” Callum said. “Let it equalize.”

The gauge crept higher. Twenty‑one hundred. Twenty‑one fifty.

Jakob felt his pulse hammering in his ears. The numbers kept climbing. Twenty‑two hundred. Twenty‑two twenty.

“Shut it down,” Callum ordered.

The injection valve snapped closed. For a long, terrible moment, the pressure kept rising. Twenty‑two forty. Twenty‑two fifty.

Then it stopped. Held. Slowly began to drop.

“Relief valve’s doing its job,” Callum said quietly. “We’re venting faster than we’re building now.”

The pressure fell back to nineteen hundred and stabilized.

“We’re going to inject slower,” Callum said. “We can’t risk the formation.”

“Slower? That’ll take forever.”

“She’ll take as long as she takes.” Callum looked at him. “We don’t rush this, mate. We rush, and the formation cracks. It cracks, we lose the whole site.”

Jakob nodded, throat tight. “Of course. I don’t mean to rush you.”

Instead of a half hour, it took four days before they were ready to start injecting water. Callum’s team adjusted the flow until the pressure held steady at twenty‑one hundred psi. On the fifth day, cold water began flowing from the recovery well.

Well, not really water. Sludge. Jakob was there when the first liter came up. He was devastated. Expecting clear water, the sight of this heavy mud was too much.

Callum saw the pain in his eyes. “She’s got some color to her. We’ll need a basket centrifuge here to clean it up before sending it back to Bounty. I think it looks pretty good. Petey will take care of us.”

Ellie was listening. “I can work with Petey to make fiberglass filters that will fit his baskets.”

“That’s the spirit, Ellie. But I need to warn you, the filters will wear out. I don’t know how fast, but probably a new one every couple weeks. How’s that sound?”

Ellie thought about it. “I make a lot of fiberglass anyway. Pulling a little aside for filters will be easy.”

Petey didn’t worry about making a great centrifuge. He used woven steel mesh and only designed it to spin up to a thousand rpm. But it had volume, over a ton capacity, and he made two, so they could switch when one got blinded. That would catch all the sand and rocks.

Next, he built a bowl centrifuge. This would fill up, too, but slowly. All the remaining fine sediment would end up on the walls. The water would come out transparent, good enough to pump to Bounty.

In a week, they had it operating. Two of each centrifuge. When they filled, they would switch to the other one. Ellie had used a sixty-mesh in the basket centrifuges. Pulled out all the big stuff. The water came up like sludge into the basket centrifuge, and exited like chocolate milk. Then it went into the bowl centrifuge, which spun faster, four thousand RPM. The liquid that came out looked like Lipton tea.

Tom connected the recovery line to the pipeline.

“Send it,” he said.

The pump engaged, and water began flowing north toward the settlement. It would take six hours to reach the distillation plant Ellie had built. Tom and Mira flew back, slowly, going over the track of the pipeline, looking for any leaks. They went slow and low, but saw no problems.

Jakob was at Bounty when the water started flowing. The still was connected to the waste heat from the generator, which boiled it fast. The steam went through a cooling coil filled with cold ammonia, which dumped its heat into the Martian atmosphere with a radiator. The water that condensed was clear and pure. Jakob watched as it flowed into the hundred-ton freezing tray. He pulled out his slate and sent a message to Dr. Knox. “The system is working. Your next shipment is freezing now.”

Jakob didn’t notice the concentrate. That coffee colored mother liquor was freeze-dried. The dry chemicals were stored, and the water was condensed, providing enough water for Bounty.

Jakob stood beside the pipeline, watching the pressure gauge hold steady. He felt pride. Extraction, cleaning, pumping, purification. So much more complex than Magazine Beach, but actually easier to operate. The two teams had really pulled together.

Ellie came over and joined him. She checked the water output from the freeze dryer. She loaded up a bottle of five gallons, and went to find Pacha.

Pacha was working on greenhouse five. Inside, the air was warm and humid, the soil beds ready for planting. Goldeneye was spread around, and the buds were showing. In the middle of the greenhouse was a lined area for a large pond, gently sloping to about a foot deep in the center. This structure was running at three psi to sustain cavies. While he used the same seeds, he was not planting for crops; he was planting for pasture. He scattered duckweed on the pond and planted water spinach. Fast-growing and nutritious, these plants would keep the CO₂ levels low, and the cavies could eat them. He walked the length of the greenhouse, checking the irrigation lines, the grow lights, the ventilation ducts. Everything worked. Everything was ready. He planted amaranth first, because it made great fodder for cavies, but he planted buckwheat and Tibetan lentils as well for nutritional and textural variety. Oca would follow in a few weeks. None of it was in neat rows; it was scattered around. Here, the herd could thrive.

Pacha was still in the greenhouse planting when Ellie found him. “This looks great, Pacha.”

“Right now, only the goldeneyes are growing. They are helping build the soil. Don’t say anything, because it isn’t exactly legal, but Callum brought me some inculums from mountainous areas. They seem to be thriving. That’s why I planted the goldeneyes. Let’s see what it looks like when it starts greening up. The soil here will be wonderfully healthy. That’ll make expansion easier. Growing soil is the hardest part. We need twenty greenhouses just to handle all the orders we already have.”

“I think the flowers are wonderful. Are you going to put the cavies in now?”

“If I put the cavies in here now, they’ll eat the seeds. I need to wait another six weeks.”

Ellie listened, but really she was bursting to share what they had done at the Medusae Fossae Formation. She calmly walked over to the pond. Pacha’s jaw dropped when she nonchalantly dumped in the five gallons of water she brought. “Well, I have good news, too. No one is moving. Water is flowing to Bounty.”

“I knew you guys would get that done. Fantastic. So we’re back in business.”

Ellie nodded. “It’s all working.”

At dinner, Mira sat by Jakob. “We did it.”

“We did,” Jakob agreed.

“So what’s next?”

Jakob thought about the ice field, thousands of cubic miles of frozen water waiting beneath the Martian surface. He thought about the beryllium mine, the greenhouses, the settlement that was no longer a camp but a colony.

“Honestly,” he said, “I have no idea. But whatever it is, our team can do it.”

“Yes, we can.”

Outside, the Martian sun was setting, casting long shadows across the plain. The solar troughs were folding for the night, the mass driver was idle, and the settlement lights were coming on one by one.

Jakob watched it all and thought: This is real. This is permanent.

And for the first time since landing on Mars, he believed it.
