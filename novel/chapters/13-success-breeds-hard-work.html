Chapter 13

Success Breeds Hard Work

2,901 words · about 12 minutes

The shutters of Le Puits Perdu were drawn against the light, though the Parisian street outside was already beginning to hum with delivery bikes, mopeds, and the low murmur of pedestrians threading through narrow corridors of the sixth arrondissement. Inside, the café was quiet. The tables were unset except one, where two white plates rested on clean linen, and the smell of baking butter and yeast softened the cold stone walls.

Emmanuel, the owner, was somewhere in the back. He had opened early for Catherine Dahl and no one else.

Catherine sat alone, her coat folded neatly beside her. The waiter brought a basket of pain d’épi, still warm, its golden crust flaking under the cloth, and a terrine of whipped salted butter. Beside it, a small plate of Saint-Marcellin, a wedge of Ossau-Iraty, and two slices of a dense Alpine tomme. The second plate held a small galette filled with caramelized onion and mushroom, topped with soft goat cheese and a single runny egg. Her espresso was untouched.

When Max Juno arrived, the door opened without ceremony. He stepped inside, blinking once at the dim light, then smiled as he spotted her.

“You look rested,” he said.

“I fake it well.” She gestured to the empty seat. “Come in. It’s good to finally meet you.”

Max slid into the chair opposite her. The waiter appeared without prompting, placing a fresh plate for him, along with his own galette, tomato, melted comté, and thin slices of dried lamb shoulder.

“I told Emmanuel you eat what’s in season. If you’d like something else, tell them.”

The waiter returned again, placing a small dish of fig preserves, a bowl of clementine segments in thyme syrup, and a second espresso.

Catherine reached into her folio and placed an envelope on the table. She slid it forward without commentary.

Max glanced at it but didn’t pick it up. “That for me?”

“Four hundred twenty million,” she said. “That’s more than the agreed price. Payment in full for the first fourteen shipments, with a fifty percent bonus for delivering full loads all fourteen weeks.”

He reached for the envelope now, weighed it briefly, and tucked it inside his coat. He never dreamed of this much money, even when Jakob had explained it.

“You didn’t think we’d pull it off.”

“I didn’t think you’d go to Mars,” she said. “When I made the offer, I thought Jakob would try to source from Earth. I knew there was no way he could move that much water from here. He never mentioned Mars. He’s full of surprises.”

Max smiled faintly. “He is.”

“Still,” she said, slicing a piece of bread, “he did it. That water’s not just for drinking. It’s in our smelters, our reactors, our cooling cycles. It’s Hydrolox for our ships. The algae tanks are running full tilt, and the team is taking showers. What you delivered is the reason my project made it through its first phase.”

Max spread a bit of fig preserve on a corner of bread, took a bite, and nodded. “Then we’re in business.”

Catherine wiped her fingers on a napkin and pulled out a second envelope. Thinner than the first. She placed it beside her plate.

“This one isn’t a check. It’s a proposal. New contract. Triple the water. Same quality, same schedule. I want to add one hundred tons of refined carbon per week. And food.”

Max raised an eyebrow.

“Meals,” she clarified. “For my crew. Prepared. They are to include meat, vegetables, and bread. What they are eating is horrid. I tried it. I want the miners to remember what a meal feels like.”

He looked at the envelope but didn’t open it.

“You’ve got production running. Why upscale so fast?”

“Because Raines wants more. Because demand from Earth isn’t slowing. I am sending another sixty miners, ten replacements, and fifty new headcount, bringing the total up to a hundred. What we’ve built on Ceres works, but only just. We sent people up there to dig. Jakob’s team is living. Mine is surviving.”

Max leaned back slightly. “You know what this would cost you from Earth.”

“Which is why I’m not sourcing from Earth. You have a working supply chain. The margin is yours.”

He took a long sip of coffee, savoring it. “I’ll talk to Jakob. No guarantees.”

“I wouldn’t trust you if you offered one. See what Jakob can do.”

They ate quietly for a few minutes, the soft scrape of cutlery and faint clatter from the kitchen the only sounds. The bread was still warm. The clementines were perfectly balanced, acidic, sweet, and herb-laced.

After a while, Catherine added, almost as an afterthought, “Tell him thank you.”

Max looked up.

“Not for the water,” she said. “For listening that night. For letting me feel like someone out there actually cares and wants to help.”

He smiled. “Jakob is remarkable. When he first presented his plan, I almost dismissed him, but I saw his determination and unique perspective. He is inventive. He never ceases to impress me.”

When Catherine was done, they said their goodbyes, and Max stayed. Not a lot of people came down to Lagos, and Max liked the idea of investing in other ventures. He had made some calls and set up a few meetings while he was up in Paris.

Some of the people he met, he didn’t trust. Some other ideas were good, but didn’t speak to him, per se. Max was focused on space. His time on the Moon had left that hunger in him.

By midmorning, the café had opened to the public. Max lingered at a side table near the rear window. Emmanuel, out of sight but never far, kept the space open for him between bookings.

One fellow impressed him. An Australian arrived without fanfare. Broad across the shoulders, wind-chapped, and moving with the careful assurance of someone accustomed to uncertain footing, he stepped inside and paused just long enough to scan the room. He spotted Max, gave a brief nod, and crossed over.

“Callum Haldane,” he said. His voice carried a dry, gravelly cadence, shaped more by red dust and engine noise than formal instruction. He set down a narrow duffel and offered a calloused hand.

Max shook it. “Glad to meet you.”

The waiter arrived without being summoned and poured coffee for them both. Callum watched in silence, waited until the cups were settled and the waiter had retreated, then leaned back slightly and took stock of the room.

“I’ve been watching what Raines is buying,” he said. “He’s driving up the price of beryllium, trying to stockpile for those tokaplex builds. Ceres gives him tungsten, tantalum, molybdenum, vanadium, all the big refractory metals. But beryllium’s not there in ore form. Too diffuse. No concentration.”

He paused for a sip of coffee. No reaction to the taste.

“That’s what got me thinking about Mars. Volcanic history, past water flow. You get both, you might get proper ore bodies. So I pulled old Martian data. Started combing through rover sweeps. Found a crater about sixty klicks east of Olympus Mons. One of the polar satellites passed over on a low-angle sweep. I found a density anomaly. Off-pattern. I ran the spectrum against Earth-side samples. Closest match was beryllium.”

Max said nothing. He took a slow sip, watching Callum over the rim of his cup.

Callum continued. “I’ve got three others ready to sign on. Crew from Western Australia. We’ve worked lithium fields, deep copper. We know how to run a mining site. What we don’t have is infrastructure.”

Max nodded slightly. “What are you missing?”

“Transport. Shelter. Food, water, air, heat, sewage, the full stack. We’ve got rigs, partial automation, but we’re not building from bare regolith. You get us to the site and keep us alive long enough to dig, we’ll pull ore.”

He glanced at Max, then added, “And we both know it won’t sit on the shelf. Raines has a standing buy order for every ounce of beryllium he can find. Those tokaplexes aren’t made of dreams and kind words.”

Max rested a hand on the table. “I think we may be able to help you with that.”

Callum nodded once, almost to himself. “You drop Mars-pure beryllium in Raines’ lap, and he’ll crawl over his own funding board to get more.”

Max leaned back slightly. “I can fund that. I can put you where you need to be. I want forty percent. What do you think?”

Callum’s expression didn’t change, but he took a moment before answering.

“Forty’s heavy,” he said. “But you’re taking the risk up front. If you’re covering transport, infrastructure, and coordination, I can live with that. But once we’re operational, the terms stay fixed. You don’t touch the technical, and we don’t chase investors.”

Max gave a slow nod. “Not exactly. I’ll buy equipment and pay your early expenses, but you’ll pay those expenses as soon as you start making money.”

They sat for a moment, the soft sounds of the café rising around them. Max stirred his coffee once, then looked out toward the street.

Eventually, Max nodded. “There is a team on Mars. Magazine Beach. They can get you housing. They are on the South Pole now, but they need to move to a temperate location. They might be happy to move near Olympus Mons. They’ll want to be paid. They’ll also need mineral mines for fertilizer. Could your people help locate that?”

“If we get dug in, yes,” Callum said. “We’re not agronomists, but we know how to find what’s buried.”

“You’ll need to handle smelting and casting yourselves.”

Callum gave a small nod. “We’ve got someone lined up. Not fully industrial, but enough to get clean metal. We’re not planning to ship raw ore.”

“The South Pole team can take care of getting it to Earth.”

Callum rose, adjusted the strap, and met Max’s eye.

“If you’re in,” he said, “we’ll be ready.”

Then he turned and left, without looking back.

Max stayed seated a few minutes longer after Callum left. The maps were still in the duffel. Nothing on the table but a cooling cup of coffee and a few grains of sugar near the saucer’s edge.

Forty percent. Housing, logistics, launch. If the site held, it would be worth ten times that. If it didn’t, well, they’d burned fuel on worse guesses. And they could help Magazine Beach move.

Outside, the sky was quiet. Dust curled just above the regolith, lifted, then dropped by cold crosswinds.

Max sat with a new cup of coffee and opened the second envelope Catherine had left that morning.

It was a full draft contract. The figures were typed cleanly in the margin.

Max set the paper down, took a slow breath, and looked out the window. The city moved with its usual rhythms. Nothing was scarce here.

But out there, between three other worlds, scarcity defined everything. And now, just barely, a few people had begun to price survival like a market.

It was time to speak with Jakob. He keyed in the transmission and let the recorder settle. The slate light blinked once. Then he spoke.

“Jakob. Pacha. I’m sending this from Paris. Just a short update to begin with…”

On Mars, the dome lighting was fixed at midday levels, but Jakob hadn’t adjusted. He and Pacha sat at the main table in the fabrication wing, both with slates open, half-finished reports abandoned in the corner of the screen. A tone sounded, low and deliberate. Message in queue.

Jakob pulled it up and scanned the header.

“It’s Max. He’s in Paris today,” he said.

Pacha pushed his tablet aside. Jakob tapped play.

Max’s image appeared, seated alone at a narrow café table. He looked rested, measured, maybe even a little too calm. The message played.

“Jakob. Pacha. I’m sending this from Paris. Just a short update to begin with. Dahl was pleased. Ceres got more than they expected, and the ice was cleaner than they hoped. She gave a fifty percent bonus. She wants more. Three times more ice, but also carbon and food this time too. Processed, sealed, frozen. She is impressed with your agriculture and wants to feed her team better. I attached the contract.”

Another pause.

“I also talked to Tan. She wants ice and food as well. She is willing to pay what Dahl is paying. She has less need for water, but more need for food. She runs a cantina there and is feeding a lot of Lunans. Right now, she is feeding them Earth food, but believes Martian food would be a real hit.”

Pacha sat forward when he mentioned food. Jakob said nothing, but his eyes narrowed slightly at Tan’s name.

Before it ended, Jakob was already flagging Tom, Ellie, and Mira. They needed to hear it. Jakob replayed the message when they were all there.

Max continued, his voice lower and more deliberate. “What I’m about to propose wasn’t on the table when we last spoke. But the opportunity came fast, and I think it’s real.”

He glanced down, as if reviewing something. Then back to the camera.

“A miner named Callum Haldane came to see me this morning. Australian. Solid. His team’s been analyzing Raines’ buy orders and looking for new sources of beryllium. According to some old rover data and old orbital telemetry, they found a crater about sixty kilometers east of Olympus Mons with a high-density anomaly. Spectrum scan lines up with beryllium signatures.”

Another pause.

“They’re miners. Not settlers. They’ve got the tools, partial automation, and one smelter tech. But they have no life support, no base. They need housing, power, food, and water. They need someone to keep them alive long enough to get the ore out.”

Max leaned slightly toward the camera.

“I told them I could fund it and coordinate logistics. But that only works if you’re willing to establish the site. You’d build it for them, not just for you. If it goes ahead, they’ll arrive with a nuclear thermal ship. Once they land, the engine becomes a reactor. You’d have more base power than you’ve had since the landing day. Enough to expand food, keep lights on, and maybe even grow.”

He let the weight of that settle.

“They’ll refine the metal on site. If you can get it to Earth, we can sell it. Raines needs it.”

He looked straight into the lens.

“I know this complicates your move. But Olympus might be the right place to build for real. They can help you move if you can take care of them. Think about what you would need.”

When the message ended, there was a brief silence. Then Tom gave a short, dry cheer. “Bonus! No one said anything about a bonus. And now three times the water? Sweet!”

Mira tore up some paper to make confetti and threw it in the air. “Four hundred twenty million. We’re all rich. And next year. Another three billion. Yes!. She began doing cartwheels, alarming the cavies.

Ellie just fell on the floor, half crying.

Tom and Pacha sat thinking. This was a lot of work for them. The money was on Earth, not here. They both started making lists of equipment they would need. They hoped the Aussies could bring it.

Jakob was all business, maintaining his calm composure. But his mouth didn’t get the message and was grinning of its own accord.

Jakob let them have their moment. Then, when Ellie sat up and wiped her eyes, when Mira stopped bouncing, and Pacha looked up from his notes, he leaned forward. “We need to talk about what this actually means. Four months more here, then we relocate north. If we don’t move above the Antarctic Circle before winter, we’ll die. No sunlight means no solar power means no oxygen.”

The team sobered. Pacha nodded slowly. “And the food scaling. Feeding the four miners is just more of what we’re doing. But a hundred people on Ceres? That’s industrial agriculture. Different greenhouse designs, different water chemistry, and completely different waste management. We’d need to figure out how to even ship it without it spoiling.”

“One step at a time,” Jakob said. “First, we tell Max we’re in. We can handle the water and carbon. The miners bring the reactor, and we build them a base at Olympus Mons. Food production, we’ll scale up as we figure it out.”

Tom was already sketching on his tablet. “If the miners arrive first, that actually helps us. We can use their ship’s reactor to power the move, build the new base with real power instead of scraping by on solar.”

“Exactly.” Jakob pulled up the recording interface. “Let’s tell Max what equipment we will need. By the time the Aussies arrive, we’ll be ready.”

Back in Paris, Max was packing up. He thought about the other groups he had talked to that day: a chemical engineer ready to build a chemical industry on Mars, a hotelier ready to provide hospitality for tourists and Cereans on vacation, and a lunar helium miner who thought Mars would offer easier living than the Moon and still be profitable. All good pitches, but not tonight.

He thought about what made Mars special.

Earth had power. The Moon had money. Ceres had metals.
Mars had a business plan.
