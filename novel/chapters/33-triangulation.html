Chapter 33

Triangulation

1,918 words · about 8 minutes

Amira Bastos sat in her office looking out on Bahía de Miel, a perk of being the CEO. But right now she had a call to make.

“Hello, this is Gary Thompson, General Atomics space division.”

“Hi, Gary, this is Amira Bastos. What is this request for authorization to refuel one of your reactors in space? You know there is a fissile launch ban.”

“Yes, ma’am. However, JRC is exempt from that ban. We have an order here from Terrence Halden requesting a refueled nuclear reactor for use in orbit around Jupiter.”

“Halden? Who is Halden?”

Gary brought up the requestor line on the order form. “Well, ma’am, it says here he is a senior systems analyst in applied research.”

“Hold that order. I need to look into this.”

“Very well, ma’am. I await your call.”

Bastos walked out to the receptionist and asked to see Halden immediately. The nature of a large organization like JRC is that something being done immediately may take a week or so. In this case, Halden was in her office in less than three minutes.

“What is this order from General Atomics?”

“Yes, the order for a refueled reactor? Well within my budget authority. We are planning a total PR coup for JRC.”

“You might have mentioned it to me first.”

Halden was amused at the absurdity of her comment. As if he could just drop by and chat. “Yes, ma’am. I should have let you know.”

“OK, what is this PR project about?”

“I had an idea about extracting ³He from Jupiter. I got the lab guys at JRI to work out the details. It looks like we can pull out a kilogram of ³He from Jupiter and bring it to Earth. We would burn that at Ilha Fusão, providing one month’s power with real Jovian fuel. I think that will get some pressure off your back.”

“Are you certain this will work?”

“I am not. My plan is no press until the helium is on its way here. If we fail up there, no one need know about it except us.”

Bastos looked out on the bay again. People did not understand that this was not as simple as just flipping a switch. Raines had been publicly complaining about the lack of progress repeatedly, and others were starting to join in. This might be just the project to buy some breathing room. But Halden’s leash was clearly too long.

“OK, here is what we will do. You proceed, but keep me informed. I want a status report every week. Two things: no nuclear power, and don’t let Raines or anyone from Helion Dynamics find out about it.”

“Yes, ma’am, but one thing.”

“What?”

“Nothing can get us to Jupiter and back except a General Atomics ship.”

“You can take a Lunar Tug ship.”

“They are promising to have ships that can do it soon, but right now they are limited to Mars, and they are very slow. With our payload, it would take several trips, probably decades.”

She stared at him for over a minute, searching for a weakness. “I hate orbital mechanics. Fine. Nukes are authorized. Keep it low key.”

“Of course, ma’am.”

At JRI, the prototype was coming apart under its own complexity. The light ramjet design was not coming together and there was no easy way to separate ³He from ⁴He. At the current weight, the odds of surviving a full harvesting cycle were marginal. Marco floated a solution in the project chat. “Top off the ³He with ⁴He. Symbolic payload either way.”

Symbolic. Halden read the message and understood. Halden knew he needed experienced engineers to get through this phase. Helion Dynamics had the engineering expertise and the drive to push this project forward, but Bastos had left him with strict orders not to involve them.

Halden took a walk down the street. He stopped at his favorite taqueria, La Esquina, painted yellow with blue trim. He didn’t order anything, just asked to use the phone. The manager let Halden use his office. Calling Raines in Geneva was too risky. He called the operator in Unalaska and got the phone number for the Helion Dynamics storage facility. He navigated the directory to Raines’ extension and left a voicemail.

Halden gave the manager twenty bucks and his number, offering another twenty every time he relayed a message. The manager nodded. “Si, No problem.”

Raines got the voicemail. He called Elin. “Do you remember Terrence Halden, the heavy lift guy? I got a message from him. He’s taking a shot. The kids at JRI have worked out a plan, but they are going to need an adult to help.”

“You know Bastos won’t want me within a hundred miles of Baracoa. What can I do?”

“Can you talk to him? He left me a number.”

Halden just got back to the building when he received a message from the taqueria manager. “Hola, Señor Halden, Doctora Elin Sarai has called. She left a number.”

“I’m on my way.”

Halden didn’t even sit down. He sent her the designs as he walked back to La Esquina. At the restaurant, he called her back from their office. “Dr. Sarai?” he stumbled. He never thought he would talk to her.

A moment of silence. Elin was scanning through the files. “Interesting concept. I may be able to help build that. I have a shop that specializes in limited-run, hard-to-manufacture prototypes. How quickly can you get me solid specifications?”

“They aren’t making solid specs; they are sharing ideas. Recently, it has started to overwhelm them. People were even talking about bringing back a bottle of helium without purifying the isotope.”

“Maybe I can help.”

And that was it. He looked at the waves on his walk back. The sea hadn’t changed. The clouds were still dragging themselves over the edge of the horizon. Inside, everything was shifting.

A week later, Halden waited by the whiteboard, hands behind his back. The room was packed already. Several postdocs, engineers from the cryogenics group, and a bunch of interns were eager for the next talk. The project was frustrating, but it also felt like the single most important problem on Earth. The morning sun over Baracoa flooded in through the wide louvered windows, pooling light across the tiled floor, throwing long shadows under the chalk trays. People were chatting, arguing over details, talking about designs.

Then Dr. Elin Sarai stepped through the door, and everything stopped.

She wasn’t tall, but she moved like someone who had never been second-guessed. No lab coat. No prepared slides. Just the calm certainty of someone who had already done the thing the rest of them were still designing. Her presence sent a shock through the room. Not noise, not energy. Silence. Reverent, involuntary. A couple of them actually stood up. One nodded stiffly and muttered something reverent. Another bowed. Halden didn’t smile. He waited, then spoke her name as though it needed no title.

“This is Elin.”

That was all. He stepped aside. She looked around, scanning their faces like she was reading field data.

“I’ve read your plans,” she said. “Conceptually, it’s good work. You’ve shown flexibility from core concept to prototype which senior engineers still struggle with.”

They sat straighter.

“The thing you must stay true to, in the midst of this flexibility, is your purpose. If this vehicle goes up and brings back a bottle of ⁴He with trace ³He, the world will see a meaningless gesture. If it brings back one kilogram of high-purity, Jovian ³He, we wake up the next day in a different world.”

She paused.

“You’re building the first example of an extraction supply chain for fusion-grade material. Do not think of this as a mock-up. This is every bit as important as Ilha Fusão.”

The tension in the room shifted. Before, it had been the strain of trying to live up to a concept. Now it was the recognition of obligation. Their work had consequences.

“The demand for isotope feedstock is set to jump. China’s terawatt reactor burns four tons per month. We have twenty more on order. Lunar extraction is slow and inefficient. You get one kilogram back from Jupiter, one real kilogram, measured and sealed, and it will no longer be a dream, it will be demand.”

Halden finally spoke again. His voice was level.

“If we design a system that can deliver one kilogram, we will surely fail. Too many things can go wrong. The system has to be designed to survive long enough to deliver ten kilograms. Plenty can go wrong once it gets there. If you design for ten, we have a much better chance of getting at least one.”

The earlier proposal to top the tank off with ⁴He to “simulate delivery” had evaporated. That kind of thinking wouldn’t survive in a room where Dr. Elin Sarai expected action.

The room became a working room again. They weren’t guessing anymore. They were delivering specs to Dr. Sarai. In the quiet moments between tasks, a few still stole glances at her. That she had read their work and taken it seriously was an overwhelming validation.

Elin worked with them for an hour before leaving. Halden left a few moments later, and went to his office first.

Elin arrived first. She looked like someone waiting for a ferry, not the engineer behind the largest change in electric power since Edison.

The restaurant had once served construction crews during the early buildout of the JRC headquarters, and had never entirely closed. It served straightforward Cuban fare: rice, beans, fried plantains, slow-cooked pork. More importantly, the people who still ate there didn’t ask questions. It was a good place to be unseen.

Halden slipped in five minutes later. He ordered without looking at the menu and took the seat across from her.

Elin wasted no time. “This prototype is good,” she said. “But I don’t care about one kilogram or even a hundred. I want scale. I want to see if this pipe dream of yours can give me a hundred tons a month. I want production.”

Halden leaned back. “That’s not how the JRC management sees it. They need a headline. Something that photographs well and carries no risk.”

Elin didn’t bother to look surprised. “They don’t want helium,” she said. “They want relevance. I’ve met these people.”

“I report to them. My hands are tied.”

“So play along. Just don’t forget the real work. You’re not building a story. You’re building an economy.”

He watched her carefully. “I know the difference. But I can’t bring this to them. Not directly. Not yet.”

“That’s fine,” she said. “Bring it to me.”

They ate slowly. The pork was heavy, the plantains sweet. No one at the other tables seemed to notice them.

When the check came, Elin paid in cash.

She stood, adjusting her satchel, and glanced back at him one last time. “You’ll need allies, Halden. More than you’ve got now. When it’s time to move, don’t do it alone.”

Then she left, walking to the edge of the plaza and stepping onto the bus headed for Santiago de Cuba. From there, a hopper would take her to Biloxi, where the next terawatt tokaplex was already under construction.

Halden stayed behind, finishing his coffee. The restaurant was quiet again, the world unchanged. But the message was clear. The fiction had a deadline. After that, it had to be real.
