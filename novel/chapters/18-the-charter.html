Part II · Jovian Resource Corporation

Chapter 18

The Charter

1,516 words · about 6 minutes

Restrepo arrived early. The Kuwaiti delegation had called the meeting, but arriving first meant watching the others enter, reading their faces before the diplomacy locked into place. He chose a seat near the window where he could see both the room and the city beyond.

New York stretched outward in its familiar grid, financial towers pressed against older districts, everything held in place by quiet authority. For a century, oil had been so organized: predictable, distributed, controlled. Now ³He threatened to redraw the entire map. And Venezuela, already bleeding, would be the first to die.

The others arrived in silence. Al-Rashid from Kuwait took the head of the table with the easy confidence of someone who’d orchestrated a hundred meetings like this. Morrison, the Canadian delegate, looked tired, his jaw set. Okonkwo from Nigeria carried himself with the alertness of someone determined not to be sidelined. Dr. Lais, representing Indonesia, moved with careful precision, her expression giving nothing away.

Al-Rashid opened his slate. “Thank you for coming. The numbers have worsened. Fusion uptake is accelerating faster than our models predicted.”

Restrepo didn’t wait for the polite circling. “We are running out of time. Venezuela cannot survive another decade of this. Oil revenue is the only thing between us and complete collapse.” His accent felt thicker when emotion crept in. He forced himself to slow down. “Fusion is extinction for my country.”

“Nigeria faces the same reality,” Okonkwo said, leaning forward. “We are funding infrastructure, education, healthcare. All of it depends on petroleum revenue. We will not be pushed aside while others decide our future.”

Morrison’s voice carried a bitterness Restrepo recognized. “Alberta’s been keeping Canada solvent for years. Now Ottawa vilifies us for it while spending every dollar we send. Fusion gives them an excuse to turn off the taps completely.”

At least Morrison’s resentment came from being unappreciated. Restrepo’s came from watching his country starve.

Restrepo pulled up his own slate. “I’ve been looking at the output numbers from the Moon. They don’t make sense. I know they’re using unlicensed nuclear power up there. What did the inspector find?”

Al-Rashid winced. “Vassiliev found a radioactive sample, but nothing else. I’ve talked to him extensively. When we gave the sample to the labs, they found ordinary ilmenite. The lab says it must have been cosmic radiation. Based on what he found, I’m surprised the Moon is delivering as much ³He as it is. We thought Tan had a nuclear plant, but he checked it personally. Solar arrays on the ridge of her crater and a very large dual RF processor and absolutely no heat signature anywhere else.”

Morrison shook his head. “No way. They can’t deliver those numbers without nuclear power. He didn’t look hard enough.”

“I share your frustration.” Al-Rashid’s tone carried the weight of someone who’d already fought this battle. “But Vassiliev is a good inspector. There may be a nuclear plant up there somewhere, but we need to accept we cannot find it. And even if we did, our options are limited. Besides, since we authorized General Atomics to build nuclear ships, Mars and Ceres just park the ships and use the reactors for power. All legal.”

Morrison looked up. “Then we hit the supply chains. Can’t touch the reactors themselves, but we can restrict what feeds them. Lunar operations depend on regular fissile shipments for auxiliary power. Mars and Ceres too. Slow those shipments, production tapers.”

Okonkwo’s arms crossed. “How?”

“Environmental safeguards,” Morrison said. “Several delegations have already raised concerns about moving enriched materials through Earth’s atmosphere. The language is vague enough to expand.”

Restrepo saw it immediately. The logic was elegant, almost beautiful. They couldn’t stop fusion, but they could starve the mechanism feeding it. “Frame it as international responsibility. A phased restriction tied to environmental review. No one will understand the impact. It’ll pass overwhelmingly.”

“Perfect,” Al-Rashid said. “Most delegates won’t consider the implications for lunar production. It looks prudent. Responsible.”

Al-Rashid’s expression darkened. “Next topic. There’s an urgent development. Raines is pushing a Jovian Resource Corporation. He claims the Moon cannot supply enough ³He to meet global demand. He wants the UN to charter a corporation to unlock unlimited fusion fuel from Jupiter.”

Restrepo felt his stomach drop. “The vote?”

“Will pass easily,” Al-Rashid said flatly. “Raines has built a coalition we cannot break. Too many nations see it as their path to cheap, unlimited energy. We cannot stop the charter.”

Morrison shook his head slowly. “If Jupiter opens up, lunar production becomes irrelevant. Everything we’re doing won’t matter. We’ll be finished.”

Restrepo looked at the city beyond the glass and felt the old helplessness rising. Venezuela had been outmaneuvered for decades. OPEC had done it. The Americans had done it. Their own incompetence had done it. Now fusion was finishing what sanctions never quite managed. And Jupiter would be the final nail.

Lais looked up from her slate. “I think Jupiter may be an impossible problem. This corporation won’t matter at all. Let the baby have his bottle.”

Okonkwo’s face darkened. “I was sold that promise before Ilha Fusão. Then they produced steady fusion. I was told they would never fuel it, but now they’re mining the Moon. These people will find a way. They are very inventive, and we cannot tolerate that risk.”

“Influence the board,” Lais said, her earlier skepticism replaced by calculation. “Fill it with the right kind of people. Treaty specialists. Governance experts.”

“People who move slowly,” Morrison added.

Restrepo nodded. “People who look ideal for a new space institution. Impartial. Scrupulous. They conduct studies, hold consultations, write reports. Progress looks like it’s happening, but the corporation never advances beyond planning.”

Okonkwo uncrossed his arms. “And if someone notices?”

“They won’t,” Al-Rashid said. “It will be interpreted as best practice. No one objects to caution in space.”

The room settled into understanding. Restrepo felt the weight of what they were doing. They were buying time for their economies. They were also sabotaging humanity’s expansion into space. He pushed the thought away. Venezuela was dying now. Whatever Jupiter might offer humanity could wait.

“We coordinate quietly this week,” Al-Rashid said. “The fissile resolution needs the right sponsors. The board nominations need discreet support. Nothing overt.”

They agreed without ceremony.

Restrepo stood, closing his slate. “My country is dying, and we are trying to buy enough time to find another way to survive. I hope you all understand what is at stake.”

“We do,” Al-Rashid said quietly.

A week later, the General Assembly chamber hummed with anticipation. Restrepo took his seat in the Venezuelan section, several rows behind the Helion delegation where Raines sat with his aides. The American looked confident, relaxed. He had no idea.

The fissile ban resolution appeared on the docket. Restrepo kept his expression neutral as the reading began, though his heart rate picked up. Everything depended on the sponsors holding, on the environmental language resonating, on dozens of delegations voting without thinking through the implications.

The electronic board flickered green and red.

The resolution passed. Overwhelmingly.

Coño. Relief and guilt hit simultaneously. The ban was binding. Lunar reactors, Martian grids, and Cerean foundries would all feel it within years. A decade at most. The installations would wither slowly, starved of fuel they couldn’t acknowledge needing.

And hunger for energy would not go away. Venezuela would sell oil again.

Across the chamber, Raines rose to speak. Restrepo watched the man’s confident cadence, the rehearsed gestures, every appeal to global prosperity. The Jovian Resource Corporation speech was polished, persuasive. Raines believed every word.

The vote was called. Overwhelming approval.

Raines sat to polite applause, relief visible in his shoulders. The man thought he’d won.

Then the board appointments were read aloud.

Dr. Emile Carson, the author of the seven-volume treatise on the Outer Space Treaty.

Ambassador Chidinma Okafor, the steward of treaty review committees.

Professor Liu Wei, a sustainable development theorist, cited in every UN resolution, but never put into practice.

Restrepo watched Raines’ face. The smallest flicker of confusion. A tightening around the eyes. The man was beginning to understand that something had gone wrong.

Delegates swarmed Raines afterward, offering congratulations. Restrepo remained in his seat, gathering his materials slowly. He felt no triumph, only a hollow kind of satisfaction.

He stood and made his way toward the exit. As he passed the Helion section, he caught a glimpse of Raines accepting praise with a strained smile. The man understood now. The fissile ban would strangle the outposts. The Jovian Resource Corporation, his great victory, had been hollowed out before it began.

Outside, the New York evening pressed against the glass facade. Restrepo walked toward his hotel, phone already buzzing with messages from Caracas.

He had done what he could. The fissile materials ban would slowly strangle the ³He supply, and Jupiter was decades away, if it ever happened. Venezuela was back. Maybe they would even find another path forward, diversify, prepare for a future that no longer needed its oil.

He told himself it was enough. This time, he believed it.
