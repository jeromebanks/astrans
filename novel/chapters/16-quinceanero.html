Part I · The Spark

Chapter 16

Quinceañero

4,872 words · about 20 minutes

Maria stood on the roof of her villa, watching Peces de la Luna come in. At forty knots, it was riding high on its plane.

Mateo Torres was at the helm. Tonight, they were coming back from a fifty-drop run. When he reached the first buoy, he throttled back. The wake caught up to the boat as it lost its plane and settled in for maneuvering.

When it docked, Mateo jumped off the ship and got on a scooter to ride home. He ran up the stairs to José’s office.

“¡Papá! I’m home. Make the call.”

Felipe and José were waiting in the office.

José made the call to Bounty. “Callum, I hear you are ready to send us metal, too. Each metal from Ceres has posed special changes. I hope you are sending something easy.”

It would be at least fifteen minutes before there was a response.

Mateo told them about the run. “Mixed metals. We got thirty bowls of manganese, fifteen of tungsten, and five of tantalum. They overshot the target by almost a hundred miles.”

“They can’t overshoot the target zone. It’s a hazard,” Felipe said. “Don’t we have a penalty for that in the contract?”

“I called Catherine Dahl directly about it. She accepted the penalty, waiving their cut on the value of the bowls that missed. That was two bowls of molybdenum,” Mateo said. “It’s much more profitable for us when they miss, but from what she said, I don’t think they will be missing much in the future.”

“Frankly, I’m afraid to talk to her,” José said.

“She’s sweet, but she doesn’t play around. You need to get used to her bluntness to see her kindness behind it.”

José’s slate crackled. “G’day, mates. Raines tells me you lads can grab up our metal. He’s been hollering for some beryllium.”

José was excited. “Absolutely. No problem. You know the bowl dimensions?”

As soon as the message was sent, Felipe spoke up. “Carnal, we cannot handle beryllium. It’s toxic. Are you going to let Mateo haul it in?”

“I’m not a child. I’m fourteen. I can handle it safely.”

“I don’t doubt you can, but the ocean can’t.”

Mateo turned to his slate.

Fifteen minutes later, Callum’s voice came through. “Thing is, we don’t have all that much. Two ingots.”

Mateo sent Callum a diagram from his slate. “Two ingots are OK. Can you send us bowls clad in an inch of CuBe1? Use the CuBe for the crossed shackles.”

After the voice message was sent, Mateo described his plan to José and Felipe. “The copper-beryllium alloy is strong and won’t corrode in the seawater. We can inspect the cladding for damage. If there is none, we can bring it back and melt off the cladding, then maybe scalp a couple millimeters to leave the beryllium pure.”

Felipe grimaced. “It sounds like a lot of work for only a couple of bowls. Maybe we pass on this.”

Mateo was shaking his head. “No, tío, we do not pass on this.” Mateo brought up his slate again, showing what Elin would pay. “Our cut for two bowls would be forty billion pesos. For that money, we can do it.”

José’s eyes were smiling as he listened to Mateo.

Felipe looked at the numbers.

Mateo continued, switching to another window. “We can process the swarf, too. Even a single bowl will give us twenty million pesos. Half that if it is cash from ArcelorMittal.”

José finally spoke. “Carnal, you are not wrong. This is a risk. But I will vote with Mateo. He can set up the new facility. And Callum won’t stop with two bowls. He’ll find another vein.”

Felipe reflected for a bit before responding. “I remember when we first talked about tungsten. You had already shot your mouth off to Raines, saying it was easy. We were lucky he gave us eighteen months. Nothing about tungsten was easy. But it was valuable. Now we routinely handle eight different, difficult metals from Ceres. If Mateo here thinks he is ready, I offer my full support.”

“Thank you, both, familia. You won’t be disappointed.”

Callum came back on José’s slate. There was a pop before he started talking. “Mates, I talked to Petey. It’ll be easy to make that. Just like making a candied apple. How soon will you be ready for us?”

José looked to Mateo.

“Callum, sir, we can be ready in eight months.”

Felipe facepalmed. “Why did you tell him eight months? Do you have any idea what can go wrong on a project like this?” Felipe asked.

“Many things can go wrong, but delay won’t be one of them.”

“Felipe, this isn’t the old days. Mateo won’t be showing contracts to a bank. He will be making a small withdrawal.”

Felipe knew he was right. “OK, I will go along with you. But charge an extra five percent for hazardous materials.”

Mateo looked at his father, who nodded. José and Felipe left the room. José left his slate with Mateo.

Two hours later, Mateo emerged with a signed, printed contract: twenty percent plus five percent hazmat fee. “Tom is going to send us a bowl of iron first, to ensure they have the launch calibrated right. Once that is ready, and we are ready, they will send the beryllium. I backtracked on eight months. Thank you, tío.”

“Fantastic,” José said. “Let’s go to the square for paletas.”

When they got out of the taxis, Lupita walked slowly, with Felipe’s help. He found a large stone table. Mateo found a paletero and brought him to the table. Lupita asked for coconut with spice, but the paletero didn’t have that, so she got a regular coconut paleta. Felipe got a creamy spicy mango paleta and traded with her when she didn’t like the coconut.

Mateo checked the ship locations on his slate. “I was expecting Corriente to be back by now.”

“Which ships did the far pickup?” Felipe asked.

“It took all three recovery ships today. Estele took the far pickup. It won’t be back for days. Ráfaga took twenty in the middle. But Corriente is only pulling ten this time, so it should be back soon.”

“Bajamar and Pleamar could only ever carry five.”

“Papá, that was the old way. Today we have too much work for that system.”

Maria looked at Lupita. “How are you doing?”

“I am queasy sometimes, and hungry.”

“You look the same, but you walk a little differently,” Sofia said.

“I don’t feel the same,” Lupita replied.

Maria was looking towards the kiosko. “I remember my quinceañera was here. It was so bright and festive back then.”

Mateo laughed. “They still have quinceañeras here, Mamá. Our neighbor had one here last week. And they are as bright and festive as ever. Just not today.”

Maria turned to Sofia. “What do you think? Will you have yours here?”

“I don’t know, Mamá. That’s a long time from now.”

Maria turned to Mateo. “We don’t do big things for boys on their quinceañeros. It’s unfair.”

“It’s not unfair. I wouldn’t want all that fuss.”

They continued eating. Felipe let Lupita eat the rest of his coconut paleta, too. She whispered in his ear, and Felipe addressed the family. “The titanium sheet metal business has been very profitable for us. It paid for the new recovery boats. I believe we could do more like that.”

“Sheet metal from other metals?” Mateo asked.

“Sure, but not just sheet metal. Mill works. Pipe, beams, plates. And alloys. The reason Elin mostly takes ingots is that she needs to alloy them before forming the products.”

“We can afford to build that, but I don’t know where we could put it.”

Felipe looked at Lupita, who nodded imperceptibly. “Mobile, Alabama.”

José stopped breathing for a moment. “What are you thinking, Felipe?”

“Here is our plan. Elin is running a huge operation, and just getting the right metals is a constant strain. We build a new site. Mobile is connected to the CPKC railroad we use, but also other tier one railroads, including the CSX that Elin is on. And not all metals come by rail. Mobile has a great seaport. That will allow me to ship to Elin by sea when needed. And it is a very nice place to raise a family,” he said, placing a hand on Lupita’s belly.

“Uncle, what about Acero del Pacifico? We still need you.”

“There are many competent people in this town, and we know most of them.”

“Felipe, Mateo is right. I still need a partner to keep this operational. This is your dream, and this town should not limit you. I will support you in any way you need, but I need you to help me find the right person,” José said.

The next day in school, Mateo was distracted, sketching out ideas for the beryllium processing mill while pretending to pay attention in Mexican history class. He was playing with the idea of how much could be done on a single pedestal.

After school, he went down to the mill and found Felipe. “I was looking at how we can build the beryllium mill. The SPMTs will deposit the ingot on this pedestal.”

“A wash pedestal?”

“Of course, the first step is to wash it. The crew can use high-pressure hoses and detergent to wash it off.”

“How will they get to the bottom?”

“The edges are hydraulic lifters that can turn it over. It has a center drain.”

“Very nice wash station, Mateo.”

“It’s not simply a wash station. Once it is washed and dried, the people leave, and we flush with argon. The same pedestal has induction coils and a cap with more induction coils. We heat it in place to melt off the CuBe, which drains down the center.”

“This is no longer just a pedestal.”

“Under the pedestal, the liquid CuBe can pour into molds easily. While it is still hot, a mill moves into place and scalps the bowl. The lifters turn the bowl over until the milling is complete. We wash all the swarf together.”

“How do you wash it? It will react with water.”

“But we used water to wash it before, and you thought it was fine.”

“It was fine; you were washing CuBe. Beryllium reacts with water. You need to find something nonreactive.”

“OK, then the swarf is swept up and washed with something else. I will keep working on it.”

Felipe laughed. “That is a nice design. You kept it very compact. With only one pedestal and no crane, it will fit easily between the other buildings. What is the pedestal made of?”

“The exterior is beryllium oxide, a nice ceramic that won’t contaminate the pure beryllium. Once the pedestal and bowl are clean, we replace the casting system to cast pure ingots.”

“The idea is very good. I will help you find someone to build the pedestal.”

“Felipe, I need to make another change. We need one more place to process tungsten.”

“Why? We are doing OK.”

“No. We were backed up three weeks on tungsten last time. I left twenty bowls just sitting in the Ráfaga until there was space to unload them. It delayed the recovery of lunar titanium.”

“Have you talked to José about it?”

“Not yet. To build the expanded tungsten facility and the beryllium facility, I am going to need to completely tear down the original steel works you two built. How do you think he will take it?”

“Explain it to him. He’ll listen.”

At dinner that evening, Mateo waited for a lull in the conversation. “Papá, I showed my plan for the beryllium facility to Felipe. He likes it. It is very compact. It will be slow, but that’s fine.”

“Wonderful. When will you build it?”

“I contacted a construction company. We are looking at companies to make the machinery. But, also, I need to make some other changes.”

“You often do. Why do you bring it up at dinner? This is time for family.”

“Because, Papá, it means tearing down the old steel works. I don’t want to, but Ceres is just too productive, and we need more space.”

José lifted his wine glass and looked Mateo in the eye. “Well, either you tear it down or the rust will. Felipe and I should thank you.”

That evening, Mateo had another call with Mars. José relaxed with Maria and Sofia.

“José, Callum here. Tom and I want to send a test drop. He never tried to hit Earth before. So we were hoping to send a bowl of just iron.”

“Sorry, José isn’t here now. It is seven pm here, and he is with his wife and daughter. This is Mateo. We are ready for a bowl of iron. Let me send you the coordinates and times so you know where to hit. Once you launch, I will coordinate with shipping.”

As the months passed, Lupita got out less and less. Maria missed her and gave her a call. “Lupita, you need to bring Felipe over here for dinner tonight. I heard people in Mobile eat oysters chargrilled, so I am cooking that tonight. Come tell me what you think of them.”

They arrived at six o’clock and were greeted by Sofia. “Aunt Lupita, you look enormous! I mean, come in.”

Sofia led Lupita to the table, and the family gathered there, while Maria finished preparing dinner.

Sofia put out pickled green tomatoes and okra early.

“I heard the first one is hardest. I hope so,” Lupita said, snacking on pickled okra.

Maria called out from the kitchen. “How many are you planning to have?”

“Six may be enough. We’ll decide when we get there.”

Dinner was simple, with only the pickles, oysters, and toast. They all continued sitting around the table, chatting, but when it came time to go, Lupita asked to stay.

“Of course,” Maria said. “You are always welcome here. We will see how you feel tomorrow.”

Maria washed her face, then went to bed. At four, she woke Felipe. “Manuel is coming! Get me to the doctor.”

Felipe jumped up and dressed, but Lupita couldn’t get out of bed. Soon, the whole family was awake. Jose called a doctor, while Maria and Sofia took care of Lupita.

Mateo, Felipe, and Jose waited in the living room. Mateo brought the ladies extra towels. Sofia took them.

“Bring me a bowl and pitcher of warm water,” Maria requested. Mateo ran to get that, but Felipe had overheard and was already filling the pitcher when Mateo got to the kitchen.

The doorbell rang, and Mateo went to answer it, letting Felipe bring the bowl and pitcher to the ladies.

As the doctor stepped inside, Manuel’s first cries rang out.

“I guess I know where the mother is,” the doctor said, bringing her things and running to the guest room.

When she arrived, Lupita looked a wreck, and Felipe was holding his son, wet and sticky everywhere.

Dr. Ramos quickly took the baby’s vitals, then the mother’s. She busied herself with paperwork. Sofia was washing Manuel gently.

There was no hurry to go home, so Lupita, Manuel, and Felipe stayed for a week. Sofia was their constant nurse.

But time came when Lupita wanted to go back to her home. With hugs and kisses from everyone, they said goodbye and went back home.

Manuel had arrived, and the iron bowl was now a month out.

Maria cooked for Lupita and family and sent the food with Sofia, who became Lupita’s assistant. Lupita was grateful for her help, especially at night when the baby cried.

Mateo came home from the dock and found his father in the office. “Papá, Martian iron is coming. Let’s go pick it up.”

“Why? It’s just iron. Record the impact site and pick it up next time we are out there.”

Mateo went to his room and started analyzing the costs and the value. He knew his father was right. Iron wasn’t valuable enough to pay for recovery, but it was out there, and so should they be. He called ArcelorMittal. The best they could give was five million pesos. Looking at the fuel, crew, and lift reagents, he calculated they might come in under three million pesos, as long as nothing went wrong.

That night, Felipe, Lupita, and Manuel came over for dinner. Maria and Felipe helped Lupita up the stairs. José opened a bottle of Nebbiolo, pouring for Felipe, Maria, and himself. Maria brought water for Lupita.

While they ate, José brought up the subject. “Felipe, my son proposed we go hunting for the iron test ingot. It will be here in a few days. What do you say?”

“I think we would have a great time, but you know I need to take care of Lupita and Manuel now.”

“Do you? Are you saying you don’t trust Maria and Sofia? Lupita can live here and be waited on hand and foot. We three men go fishing,” Jose answered.

Felipe looked at José and then at Mateo. “One bowl of base metal. I doubt it will bring a profit.”

“I talked to ArcelorMittal. It could bring a small profit. I would like to see that the shape is correct.”

“Lupita?”

“Go, Viejo. Maria and Sofia take better care of me than you do, anyway. Go catch that Martian fish.”

José was at the helm of Peces de la Luna. He insisted on packing three recovery kits. Lupita and Maria took turns on the telescope watching the bright yellow ship with a blue racing stripe pull slowly out of the harbor, then lift up on its plane as José hit the throttle. With only three lifting kits, it was light and moved fast. José enjoyed the heel as he turned first a few degrees south, then a few degrees north, just to feel the ship handling.

Maria turned to Lupita. “That man is still twelve. I think he loves that boat more than his own family.”

“No, Maria. He loves that boat, but he loves you and Sofia much more.”

It took them a day to get to the holding point, 18°N, 120°30’W. Felipe went below deck for a moment and came back with three huge fishing rods. “We’re here a day early. Let’s see who catches something first.”

The pilot took a wide circle at five knots. They all went down to the fantail. Every three or four minutes, José would throw out a live sardine. The three of them let their lines run. The sun kept moving, but no one caught anything.

Mateo saw the first one, blue and yellow, jumping high enough to be seen. “Dorado!”

Each of them started adjusting, pulling the line in, releasing it a little, hoping to get a bite.

Felipe called out, “I got one!” as his line pulled hard. Immediately, the fish started greyhounding over the water, flashing blue and yellow.

“He’s as beautiful as my boat,” José said. He and Mateo pulled their lines in and stowed their rods.

Felipe controlled the line as the fish started cutting first to one side, then the other. Slowly, he pulled the bull in closer. Mateo got the gaff when suddenly, the bull dived in a spiral.

“Pull the head up!” Mateo called out.

Felipe was way ahead of him, but the bull was strong. He got it up to the surface, and Mateo was again ready, when it dove again. José had the pilot slow down a little. Soon, the school was all around them. José kept dropping sardines, and when a cow got too close to the fantail, Mateo grabbed her with the gaff and slid her into the scupper. Felipe continued fighting the bull. When Felipe’s bull was exhausted, Mateo hooked it, too, and dropped it in the scupper.

Two fish gave fifty pounds of meat. They cleaned them right on the fantail and had fish tacos for dinner.

José was at the helm at four in the morning, watching for the fireball. Nothing came. Mateo checked the schedule. It should have hit the atmosphere. A space telescope gave him telemetry, and he recharted the course.

“Papá, it’s late. It’s going to hit outside the safe zone.”

José gunned the engine. Mateo plotted the course. It was going to miss the target by a thousand miles and the safe zone by five hundred miles.

Mateo called the US Coast Guard. “Sécurité, sécurité, sécurité. This is Peces de la Luna. Information update regarding kinetic event. The impact zone has shifted west. The new target coordinates are eighteen degrees north, one thirty-five degrees west. All vessels are advised to maintain a wide berth. The event is expected in one hour. Out.”

It took a day to reach the impact site. Estele was another day out. Since all they had was a splash location, José dropped three UUVs to scan the area. They found it three miles from the calculated impact site.

Mateo ran the glider deployment. The gliders were really just rails around the bags. They were filled on the second deck overlooking the fantail. With the aluminum/sodium hydroxide bag ready to go and preattached to the glider, the ammonia was poured into the rear bag, and the glider went off the launch ramp, diving fast.

“It looks much smaller than the old tanks.”

“The best part is no cryogenics, so it is stable. Easier to carry, less concern when it’s on the bottom if there is a lift problem. We can even resupply from a freighter if we need to.

Two UUVs surfaced while the other one guided the recovery glider to the bowl. The heavy hawser was snapped on, and the water vents on the aluminum bag were opened. As the temperature rose in the ammonia tube, it began pulling ammonia faster and faster through the nickel wool reactor. The lifting bag caught enough hydrogen to lift up, pulling the glider and reactants with it.

The reaction continued until gas started to spill out from the bottom. At fourteen hundred tons of lift, the iron only looked stationary. It was climbing, a millimeter at a time.

José suggested they blow the silt away. Mateo told him to wait. The motion had been imperceptible, but not zero. As they watched, suddenly it became obvious, and they heard the snap of the sticktion release.

As they headed home, they hailed Estelle, still coming in. Mateo suggested they transfer and let the crew take Peces de la Luna home.

José had never been on the new recovery ships. The twelve-hundred-ton crane was amazing.

Mateo was carefully monitoring ascent speed, running about one foot per second, slowing slightly in the last 50 feet.

José saw the numbers. “Mateo, slow down. I always go four inches per second. Bobbing can destroy the lift hawser.”

“Papá, don’t worry. The coil spring connector takes the shock out of it.” Mateo slowed the ascent, anyway.

Estelle moved slowly, and the UUV dragged the gear under the crane. The crane lowered the connector, and the UUV switched the bag lift for the crane lift. At sea state four, the active heave compensation made the lift smooth and easy. One attached, the crane simply winched it up, letting the ACH deal with the waves. The UUV hauled the lift bag and gear forward in the tunnel, where a light fifteen-ton crane pulled it all up and transferred it to the second deck, where it was washed, fogged, and secured.

The entire recovery had only taken forty-five minutes. The three men called home. Felipe talked first.

“How are you feeling, Lupita?”

“Maria is very kind to me, but your son misses you.”

“Does he? You put him on the line.” She held Manuel on her lap and put the phone to his ear. “Manuel, you need to help your mother.”

Manuel cooed.

Mateo spoke next. “We will be back in four days, Mamá.”

“I’ll watch for the fast yellow ship with a blue stripe,” Maria said.

“No, Mamá. That ship will be back before us. We transferred to Estelle, a catamaran.”

José took the phone call into his cabin to talk to Maria.

By the time they got the iron ingot back to Lázaro Cárdenas, Mateo had worked out a deal with ArcelorMittal for the iron, if they could break it down into pieces no larger than one hundred tons.

Mateo put up temporary walls around the bowl and filled it with dry ice. When it was cold, a single drop from a wrecking ball shattered the bowl into manageable pieces. José kept one piece, and they sold the rest for five million pesos.

As soon as they returned, Mateo called Mars. “Callum, what happened? You guys missed by a thousand miles. It was an emergency shipping hazard.”

There was no response for thirty minutes. Finally, “Sorry, mates. I brought Tom. Let’s work out what happened.”

Tom had sent his targeting data already. “When we talked before, you said you were in Mountain time. You calculated the impact site and the time of day for that impact, and sent it to me. I input that on the targeting software on the mass driver and on the navigation pack. By my calculation, it landed as scheduled.”

“I am not in Mountain Time, I am in Tiempo del Centro. Why is this scheduled for four AM, mountain standard time?”

Mateo sat quietly for the entire fifteen-minute wait.

“When we talked, you told me the time. It was the same as Denver, which is Mountain Time. That was Daylight Savings Time, but now it is standard time, isn’t it?”

“Mexico doesn’t have daylight savings time, and we are not in that time zone.”

Mateo waited again.

“I am so sorry, Mateo. I see what I did wrong. We will use Mexican Central Time from now on,” Tom said.

Callum was as upbeat as ever. “Well, we got that sorted. But, given the timezone issue, we actually hit our target spot on. Are you lot ready for the beryllium?”

“It is still under construction, but will be ready in a month or so. You can send whenever you’re ready,” Mateo replied.

A few weeks later, Mateo was at the docks when they finally tore down the first building. When it came down, Mateo found an old sign, a little smaller than the one he was used to. He brought it home.

“Papá, I thought the company name was Acero del Pacífico. This says, ‘Hermanos Acero del Pacífico.’”

“That’s the full name when Felipe and I started this. He was twelve at the time. Grew up in it. That is still the legal name, but Acero del Pacífico is snappier, so we made a new sign.”

The next few days, José and Felipe spent more time together, up in the villa and down at the mill. They were talking very seriously, and whenever Mateo came by, they would stop, then talk about lighter subjects.

Four days later, Mateo was walking down by the dock when José found him. They bought some burritos from a truck.

“Happy birthday, Mateo.”

“Thank you.”

“What do you think about Felipe and Lupita moving to Alabama?”

“Papá, we still need him.”

“We will miss him, but we will survive without him. When I bought him out, he also let me buy twenty percent of Planetary Metals.”

“How will we operate?”

“We will continue to recover interplanetary metal and sell to Felipe, but he will be buying metal not only from us, but from around the world, and doing millwork and even fabrication there in Mobile. It’s easier for the railroad, and easier for us.”

“OK, Papá. Do you know when they are going?”

“Felipe is going to scout out locations next week. He plans to move in a month. You know that Martian Iron?”

“Sure. You kept a piece.”

“Yes, for a new sign. I pounded it out in my blacksmith’s shop in the villa. You can see the workers are putting it up.”

“I can’t wait to see it.”

“You know, Mateo, wth Felipe gone, I will need a partner. Since you were a boy, I always dreamed of this becoming ‘José and Son’s Acero del Pacífico.’”

“I’ve been watching you, though, this last year, and I don’t think that really makes sense.”

“What do you mean, Papá? I’ve worked very hard. The beryllium project is going well.”

José held up his hand. “Don’t try to change my mind. I discussed it with Felipe while we were doing the pickup, and more recently, when I was buying him out. We had to refile the paperwork, anyway. Felipe recommended one man he has worked with, who has been in metals all his life and knows the deep-sea recovery business. I have talked to that man, and agree with Felipe.”

“Si, Papá. I trust you and Felipe.”

They had been walking as they talked and were now standing in front of the sign. The workers were bolting it in place. When José looked at the workman, he pulled the paper wrap off the sign. It read “Mateo’s Acero del Pacífico.’”

“Happy Quinceañero, son.”
