Part II · Jovian Resource Corporation

Chapter 20

Baracoa

1,214 words · about 5 minutes

The province of Guantánamo no longer belonged to Cuba. By vote of the United Nations, its territory had been transferred to the jurisdiction of the Jupiter Resource Corporation, an arrangement argued in back rooms then framed as visionary. Washington objected to surrendering its naval base, Havana to losing land for symbolism’s sake. Amira Bastos, CEO, and Dr. Emile Carson, Chairman of the Board, were charged with turning this provincial corner into JRC’s visible capital. Neither relished the assignment, but both understood its necessity. They were stewards of a prestigious enterprise, and appearances required that they be seen arriving here.

The seaplane banked wide, engines straining against the humid air. A Grumman Albatross, old and noisy, vibrated through the floorboards, its interior faintly scented of oil and damp canvas. Bastos and Carson exchanged glances. Neither liked it, though it was the only practical way here.

This was not the way they were accustomed to travel. Amira Bastos and Emile Carson had flown between New York, Geneva, and Vienna in wide seats with polished attendants. Neither spoke of it, but both regarded the flight as an indignity, the kind of compromise expected of lesser officials.

Through the windows, the coastline broke into ridges and narrow valleys. Rivers cut through the forest and spilled brown into the sea. Ahead lay Baracoa, pressed between the hills and the surf, its colored houses fading under salt and heat. To Bastos and Carson, it appeared not as a town but as a stage. What mattered was how JRC would be seen arriving here, what image they could project against the landscape.

The Albatross came down hard on the bay, its hull shuddering as spray burst across the windows. The fuselage groaned while the engines throttled back, leaving only the uneven slap of water against metal. Bastos tightened her grip on the armrest. Carson muttered something under his breath, then fell silent. For both of them, landing this way felt provincial, as though international executives had been reduced to tourists arriving at the edge of nowhere.

The seaplane drifted toward the spider dock, a wooden structure that swayed with the tide. Planks shifted underfoot when they stepped out, knocking against each other with every wave. Salt rose in the air, mixed with the odor of fuel. The dock was too narrow for ceremony, but that did not stop Bastos from imagining how it would look when foreign visitors first arrived. The sightlines mattered. The dock should lead straight toward JRC’s offices, visible immediately, not tucked behind warehouses or houses.

Baracoa opened before them in narrow streets and painted walls, uneven rows pressed between forested slopes and the sea. Pastel facades were faded where the sun struck them and brighter in the shadows. Murals chipped but recognizable still carried scenes of boats and storms. Children ran through alleys, their voices carrying above the sound of waves. For Bastos, these details suggested opportunity: texture that could be framed as authenticity. Carson, watching in silence, saw only how small the town was.

They left the dock for the narrow street beyond, their shoes striking uneven stones polished by salt and years of rain. The air was heavy, almost sweet, carrying the scent of fruit and wood smoke. Vendors had laid out baskets on low tables: guava, plantain, mango. Children darted past without slowing, their games ignoring the two officials in pressed linen. Bastos slowed only long enough to place a hand against a sun-bleached wall, testing its rough plaster.

“This is where it should be,” she said, her voice measured. “Headquarters at the water’s edge. Not behind warehouses or on some hill, but here, facing the dock. A visitor should step off the plane and see us immediately. The message is that we belong.”

Carson kept his gaze on the slope above the bay, terraced with houses and patches of green. “Belong,” he repeated, though his tone was unreadable. He tapped the stone with his heel, frowning at the uneven surface. “And where do we live in this arrangement?”

Bastos smiled faintly, as though she had anticipated the question. “The hillside. Villas already under construction. Terraces cut for privacy, each room with a view of the water. Gardens where you can walk after meetings. Not offices dressed up as houses, but actual homes. Mine near the beach, yours higher up where it is quieter.”

Carson’s expression softened, though his voice remained flat. “So, a retreat. That I can accept.”

Bastos nodded. “A retreat, but one that shows we came not to dominate, but to fit. Walls in color, courtyards open to the light, walkways shaded with vines. We will make the right impression. That is what matters.”

They walked a little farther, past a courtyard where laundry hung between two balconies and a mural of a fishing boat peeled against the plaster. The streets bent inland, opening onto a view of the fields before the ridges swallowed them. Bastos slowed, her hand resting on the edge of a gate.

“Headquarters, villas, docks, I can picture those clearly,” she said. “But what comes after, I do not know. We are chartered for Jupiter, yet Jupiter is distant. Something here should show we are moving forward, but what should it be?”

Carson studied the open land beyond the last houses. The fields were dotted with palms and cut by a narrow road, then rose into slopes streaked with forest. “An institute,” he said finally. “Not a factory, not a launch site. A place for research, debate, engineering trials. Just out of town, within reach, but separate.”

Bastos tilted her head, as though weighing the word. “An institute. Yes, that feels right. Not too large. A few hundred people. Laboratories, classrooms, housing along the cliffs. Visible enough to suggest progress, but not so much that we overextend.”

“It would buy time,” Carson added. “A structure people can point to. Something to fill the gap while we prepare for what comes later.”

She nodded slowly. “Yes. A language for Jupiter, without the risk of failure.”

They stood for a moment, both looking across the bay. The town continued around them; children were still shouting in the alleys, shutters half-open against the sun, a vendor calling out the price of oranges. To Bastos and Carson, the sounds were no more than background. What mattered was the picture that could be drawn from this place, and how well it would play elsewhere.

They turned back toward the dock as the afternoon light stretched across the bay. The ridges glowed green in the distance, and the sea flattened into silver. Bastos walked a step ahead, already outlining in her mind how the headquarters would look from the water, how the institute would rise against the cliffs. Carson followed, content with the thought of terraces and gardens above the town.

Neither spoke again until the dock came into sight. The Albatross waited there, rocking gently in the tide, its engines quiet for the moment. Bastos paused, watching the sun drop lower behind the ridge.

“It will work,” she said with certainty. “Headquarters, villas, institute. Everything in place.”

Carson gave a single nod, as though no more needed to be said. They had seen what they needed to see. The rest would follow.
