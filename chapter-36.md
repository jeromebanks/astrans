Chapter 12: Launch
Catherine watched the Seine from her apartment. Le Puits Perdu had made croissants from amaranth. This was Bolivian amaranth. The Martian supply was only sold in the restaurant, even to Catherine. 
Her slate showed an incoming call from a restaurant in Baracoa.  She didn’t know the caller and let it go to voicemail.  “Miss Dahl, this is Terrence Halden. I need help.  Elin suggested I call you.”
Catherine knew of Halden, but had not met him.  She called Elin. 
“Hi, Catherine.  How are you doing?”
“Doing well.  Ceres production numbers are ahead of projections. But I just got a call from Halden, and he said it was your idea.”
“You’ve seen the news? They are doing it. They are building the split rigs. ”
“I heard. They are launching every twenty minutes. Nothing like this has ever happened.”
“They would be launching a lot more if I hadn’t convinced Halden to award the CNT contract to Lefavre. Mars has a mass launcher that can send it to Callisto.  This whole thing is working. But the thing they are not doing is staffing.  They need a couple of hundred people willing to go to Jupiter and use untested rigs that, frankly, JRI isn’t sure will survive.”
“And you think I can recruit them.”
“Halden can’t.  If no one gets recruited, the project fails. I hoped you might.”
After the call, Catherine looked for the job ads. 
Job opportunities on Jupiter.
Chemical engineers preferred. Pay DOE.
“He does need help,” she thought.
She took the Gulfstream out of Orly. She called the number and got a restaurant.  En route, her slate showed a call again.  “Hello, Catherine Dahl speaking.”
“Miss Dahl, I am so glad to reach you.”
“Why are you calling me from a restaurant?”
“That’s just the way we need to do things around here. Bastos doesn’t like us talking to Raines’ people.”
“I don’t hide.  I am on my way to Baracoa. I’ll be there by three. You, Bastos, and I will talk then.”
She ate on the flight.  It was 2:30 PM when she arrived.  Halden was there to greet her with a JRC car.  It was obvious he had been chewed out badly.  “Our meeting with Bastos is scheduled for four. She may not be in a great mood.”
“That’s perfect.  There’s my bag.  Bring me to my hotel; I’d like to freshen up.”
Despite the jet lag, Catherine was sharp and ready for the meeting. 
Bastos welcomed them in.  The marina view from her office had a calming effect on most visitors.  Catherine began speaking before she took a seat. “This project can’t succeed.  You thought you needed to solve the engineering problem, but all the engineering in the world won't help you if you can’t hire.”
“Halden is perfectly capable of hiring…” Bastos sputtered before Catherine interrupted her.
Catherine projected the job ad she found on the wall. “No, he is not. How many have you hired so far?  This ad screams incompetence.”
“I’ve really just gotten started, Miss Dahl.  I’m trying a few different ideas.  I have time.  The first split rig isn’t complete yet.”
“Halden, I respect your work at Puna des Maisi, and your ability to wrangle this design out of JRI, but right now you are making excuses because you don’t know what to do. Let me lay this out for you.  You need trainers, a training facility, a program, and, mostly, people willing to risk their lives. The trip to Jupiter takes a year right now, so you want to leave a year before the first one is complete.  Let me ask again, are you ready?”
“No,” he whispered.
“If he is a little slow to hire, that’s not a problem.  This is the JRC.  We have hired a thousand people.  I can put him in touch with our HR department and start filling the reqs.  Who are you to tell us how to run our business?” Bastos said.
“How many of the people you hired knew that there was a good chance they would be killed?  How many agreed to live isolated from friends and family for up to a decade? The project is failing, and I came to help, but if you would rather do this yourselves, I will see myself out.”  She shut off the projector,  stood, and walked out the door.
Halden turned to Bastos. “Please, Amira. You and I, we deal in bureaucracy.  Managers reporting to other managers, and someone making spreadsheets.  She runs mining operations. It’s not the same thing,” Halden said, holding up his hands.
“She disrespected you.”
“She told the truth.”
“Fine, run after her. You hire her.  I don’t like her.”
“But you know we need her.”
Bastos looked pained. “Yes, we need her.  Go.  Get her.”
***
Halden found her in her hotel room. “You impressed Bastos. She wants to hire you.”
“I’d hate to see what unimpressed looks like.”
“If can tell you.  She would have been sweet the whole time, pitying you. But she would not have told me to hire you.”
“OK, if we work together, I will bill corp-to-corp and pull 1% of the gross as long as my hires are up there.  I can take the lead on hiring, build the training facilities and curriculum.” 
“Corp-to-corp is fine. As for a percent, that will require board approval.”
“That’s necessary. I want our goals aligned. If I am getting paid for how effective the team is, it changes my mindset. And board approval is needed, anyway.  I need to be able to make decisions without begging Bastos for an approval.”
***
Catherine was in Bastos’ office again. Bastos was in rare form. “You asked for a percent! Outrageous, but I got it for you.  I called in some favors with the board, and they agreed to it. But now this.” Bastos showed the salary range for Jovian workers. “One million dollars base for an engineer?  That’s more than I make!”
“I notice you haven’t signed up,” Catherine said.  “We need top engineers. People who are fastidious and flexible.  Those two characteristics don’t often go together. Such people are already expensive. Then we send them five hundred million miles away to work on unsafe machines.”
Bastos shut off the projection. Catherine tapped the wall where the projection had been. “And if they die, that salary continues to be paid to their beneficiaries for twenty years. You talk about excess salaries as if they will affect your bottom line, but you spent more than that in the last hour on launches. Each split rig is five hundred billion dollars, and you begrudge them their pay. You need a few hundred people to operate the entire fleet.”
***
The new ads were getting attention. She offered internships for training. Halden, Dr. Savic, and Dr. Patel offered to sort through the applications to find the right five hundred.  
Catherine explained the process.  “I asked JRC’s HR department to filter through the resumes, verifying everyone is who they say they are. They check nothing beyond that. I depend on you guys to pick people who are technically expert and work well on teams.”
“So we each give a resume a score and pass it along, and you pick the ones with the highest total score?” Halden asked.
“Don’t try too hard to make it fair or perfect. Pick up two resumes at a time, and choose the best one. After doing that a few times, you’ll know what to look for. I need three hundred from each of you.”
***
Dr. Ji-Wu Kim’s office was in the basement of the Interplanetary Travel building. Austere, but functional.  As the JRI director, he could have had a nicer office, but he liked the space and rarely had visitors.
Today was an exception.  Bastos had told him Catherine Dahl was coming to visit.  
She opened the door and stepped in.  “Dr. Kim, I need some space at your institute.  I will be bringing in five hundred interns.”
He knew she was coming.  Bastos had told him.  He was still taken aback by her directness. “I want to help.  We have large lecture halls, but I don’t have nearly that much dorm space.” 
Catherine showed Kim her slate. “I want to extend your campus to create a space to train them.” Catherine brought up a relief map of Jamal on her slate.  “If you let me use some of your land just south of your main buildings, I will build dorms and training facilities. The initial group is the largest, but it won’t be the last.”
“It sounds like you are building a separate institute.  Why here?”
“Your staff are the only people qualified to teach. Here makes sense.  I have other facilities, but they are for people moving rocks. I need classrooms for engineers. Your institute is built around four problems: heavy lift, interplanetary travel, extraction, and recovery. This group will solve the fifth problem, staffing.”
***
Catherine needed housing for five hundred and had no time to wait. Savannah sold her refurbished shipping containers as apartments. While it rained every day, she got work crews to set the concrete footings. They assembled five stacks of a hundred apartments each, with steel stairs in front, making them look more like ships than dorms.
She got Savic and his team to help her build the replicas. The ground was soggy and the incessant rain made it impossible. She constructed a tower with a large canopy covering the area of a large jet hangar. Inside, there were ten replicas of each major structure: the elevator, the dirigible gondola, mockups of the dirigible envelope, and the space platform.  She also built some small mockups of the dirigible envelope.  In the front, she built a simple assembly room by laying plywood on the grass and adding folding chairs.
Every morning, they had safety drills. She had forty mockups made of the elevators, the space platform, and the dirigible.  Klaxon alarm, get your breathing gear on.  Get to the elevator.  Get to the evacuation tender. Same drill, just a flashing light.  Same drill, just something wrong in the mockup. Dr. Nikola Savic, who designed the system, Rithica Patel, who designed the 3He extraction system, Marco Bianchi, who worked out the envelope, and Jason Cole, who designed the power supply, were the lead instructors. 
During the lectures, she would hit them with tear gas.  Same routine: grab their mask, put it on, find your way out. 
Tests on theory were rigorous.  They stayed up late studying.  The early tests were warnings, but after two months, those Fs came with a ticket home and severance pay.
Work in the mockups was also demanding.  Sometimes with an arm tied, sometimes blindfolded. 
Some days they would go to Punta de Maisí. There they would watch launches and landings. The Babbal Tube was the main launcher.  Every twenty minutes a rocket would emerge, and they could hear the thud for over five miles away, but the rocket itself was pretty quiet. “These were designed for heavier rockets, and we were going to burn ramjets.  Instead, they lightened the rockets a little and pushed them at over 10g.  They come out at Mach five and max Q.  This works for sending up the resin, but it wouldn’t work with delicate equipment or people.”
They also watched the descenders, running on two parafoils, hitting the net precisely.  It was suspended in the air, and let the descender come down softly.  Then the heat shield base was removed, and people or usually just 3He canisters were removed.
The class size dwindled from five hundred to three hundred.  On graduation day, Caterine gave a quick talk. “All of you have done exceptionally well.  Two hundred of you will go to Ceres, and Dr. Knox will chose the top hundred.  The rest will be offered jobs on Ceres.  Those of you who are not going, we will be sending more later.  You will be called first.”
They partied all night long.  In the morning, the two hundred made their way to Punta de Maisi.  The rocket held twenty at a time.  Every thirty minutes from eight AM to twelve thirty another group launched, bound for the LEO hub, then Mars, then Ceres.
